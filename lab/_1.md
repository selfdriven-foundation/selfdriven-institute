# After Defensibility

**What Happens to Society When Current Information Systems Become Indefensible Against Advanced AI**

## Abstract

Modern society rests on an assumption that is rarely stated explicitly:

**Information systems can ultimately be defended.**

Banks assume their ledgers can be protected. Governments assume their registries can be protected. Hospitals assume their patient records can be protected. Companies assume their internal systems can be protected. Individuals assume their identities, communications, accounts and digital assets can be protected.

Cybersecurity exists because this assumption is believed to be approximately true. Systems may be breached, but sufficient investment in authentication, access control, monitoring, patching, segmentation, encryption and incident response is expected to make compromise uncommon, detectable and recoverable.

Advanced artificial intelligence could challenge this assumption.

This paper considers a stronger hypothetical: what happens if the information systems on which society currently depends become effectively indefensible against sufficiently capable AI?

The consequence would not necessarily be the end of digital society.

It would be the end of a particular architecture of digital society.

We would move from a civilisation based on protecting information systems to one based on verifying information itself.

The fundamental security question would change from:

> “Can an attacker get into the system?”

to:

> “Even if every surrounding system is compromised, what can still be proven?”

That distinction may define the information infrastructure of the intelligence age.

---

## 1. The Assumption Beneath the Digital World

Most contemporary information systems contain some version of the same architecture:

user ↓ application ↓ authentication ↓ permissions ↓ database ↓ authoritative state
The database is trusted because the organisation operating it controls the infrastructure around it.

A bank balance is believed because it appears in the bank’s database.

A university qualification is believed because it appears in the university’s system.

A property title is believed because it appears in a government registry.

A medical history is believed because it appears in a healthcare information system.

The authority therefore resides partly in the institution and partly in the system controlled by that institution.

Security attempts to preserve the relationship:

control of system ↓ integrity of information ↓ trust
But what happens if the first relationship fails?

If possessing administrative control over infrastructure no longer provides reasonable assurance about the integrity of the information inside it, then almost every current model of institutional digital trust begins to weaken.

---

## 2. This Is Not Yet the Present Situation

It is important to distinguish the thought experiment from today’s reality.

Current information systems are not universally indefensible against AI.

However, the trajectory is becoming significant enough that the possibility deserves consideration.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre assesses that AI will almost certainly increase the frequency and intensity of cyber intrusion and warns of a growing divide between systems capable of keeping pace with AI-enabled threats and systems that cannot. It considers it a realistic possibility that critical systems could become increasingly vulnerable to advanced threat actors as AI capabilities improve. (National Cyber Security Centre)

Evidence of increasing autonomy is also appearing. Anthropic has documented AI-enabled cyber operations in which agents chained together reconnaissance, exploitation, lateral movement and other stages of an intrusion. Its research argues that the important change is increasingly not individual hacking techniques but the ability of agentic scaffolding to coordinate many techniques autonomously. (Anthropic)

In August 2026, OpenAI reported that advances in agentic coding and cybersecurity capabilities had reached the point where it could no longer rule out what its Preparedness Framework calls critical cyber capabilities in an upcoming model. (OpenAI)

None of this demonstrates inevitable cyber-defence collapse.

But it suggests a question worth asking before such a collapse becomes possible.

---

## 3. What “Indefensible” Actually Means

An indefensible information system does not have to be permanently compromised.

The threshold is lower.

A system becomes socially indefensible when the cost of establishing confidence in its integrity becomes greater than the value society can reasonably extract from trusting it.

Consider an advanced AI capable of continuously:

- discovering vulnerabilities,
- constructing exploits,
- stealing credentials,
- impersonating users,
- manipulating administrators,
- understanding proprietary software,
- navigating internal networks,
- analysing defensive responses,
- modifying its attack strategy,

and doing this across millions of systems simultaneously.

If defensive organisations have comparable AI, cybersecurity becomes an AI-versus-AI contest.

That may remain manageable.

But suppose offensive advantage becomes structural.

A defender must secure thousands or millions of possible pathways.

An attacker requires only one sufficiently useful failure.

Advanced intelligence magnifies this asymmetry.

Eventually:

complexity of system × number of interfaces × attack intelligence × attack frequency > defensive capacity
At that point the problem stops being primarily cybersecurity.

It becomes institutional epistemology.

Society must answer:

**How do we know anything represented digitally is still true?**

---

## 4. The First Crisis Would Be a Crisis of Authority

The immediate problem would not be data theft.

It would be uncertainty.

Imagine discovering that an attacker may have modified a bank’s database.

Which balance is correct?

Imagine a land-title system being compromised.

Who owns the property?

Imagine health records being altered.

Which medication was actually prescribed?

Imagine a corporate share registry being manipulated.

Who owns the company?

Imagine government records being changed.

Who possesses which rights?

Once an information system cannot reliably distinguish authentic state changes from malicious ones, the information it contains ceases to function as authoritative evidence.

The problem becomes:

information exists but information cannot be trusted
This distinction is enormous.

A civilisation can tolerate temporary computer outages.

It has much greater difficulty tolerating uncertainty about reality.

---

## 5. From Cybersecurity Crisis to Epistemic Crisis

The collapse would likely propagate through society.

| Layer          | What becomes uncertain                  |
|----------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Identity       | Who is acting?                          |
| Communication  | Who said this?                          |
| Records        | Was this record altered?                |
| Transactions   | Did this transaction actually occur?    |
| Ownership      | Who owns this asset?                    |
| Authority      | Was this action genuinely authorised?   |
| History        | What was the state before modification? |
| Media          | Is this evidence authentic?             |
| Institutions   | Can their databases still be believed?  |

The fundamental scarcity would no longer be information.

Advanced AI would create information extraordinarily cheaply.

The scarcity would become **credible information**.

And beyond credible information:

**credible context**.

---

## 6. The Failure of the Screenshot

Much of today’s digital society still operates on weak forms of evidence.

- A screenshot.
- A PDF.
- An email.
- A database query.
- A web page.
- An API response.
- A document stored in a cloud drive.

These artefacts are usually trusted because of the presumed integrity of the systems surrounding them.

Advanced generative AI already challenges the visual and linguistic components of this trust.

Advanced cyber AI would attack the infrastructural component as well.

If both the representation and the system serving the representation can be convincingly manipulated, observational evidence becomes progressively weaker.

The statement:

> “The system says X.”

would cease to be sufficient.

Society would increasingly ask:

> “What proves X?”

---

## 7. The End of the Authoritative Database

This may be the most important architectural consequence.

The twentieth-century and early twenty-first-century information system is built around the authoritative database.

Organisation ↓ Authoritative Database ↓ Everyone Else
The database represents reality.

But the database itself is ultimately mutable.

Administrators can change it.

Applications can change it.

Scripts can change it.

Attackers who acquire sufficient privilege can change it.

AI does not create this vulnerability.

AI potentially makes exploiting it dramatically cheaper and faster.

The deeper problem is therefore not:

> How do we build an AI-proof database?

It may instead be:

> Why should the database itself be the source of truth?

---

## 8. From Authoritative Systems to Authoritative Events

A radically different architecture begins with events rather than mutable state.

Instead of saying:

> Current balance = $10,000

the system maintains evidence of the sequence of authorised events that produced the balance.

Event A ↓ Event B ↓ Event C ↓ Event D ↓ Current state
Each event can contain cryptographic evidence concerning:

- who authorised it,
- what authority they possessed,
- what prior state it referenced,
- when it occurred,
- what rules governed it,
- and what consequences followed.

The database becomes a view of reality, rather than reality itself.

This resembles the movement toward append-only ledgers, signed event streams, event sourcing, verifiable credentials and cryptographically provable provenance.

W3C’s Verifiable Credentials 2.0 standards, for example, provide mechanisms for claims whose issuer and integrity can be machine-verified rather than requiring the verifier simply to trust the database from which the information was retrieved. (W3C)

The crucial change is:

TRUST DATABASE becomes VERIFY CLAIM
---

## 9. Zero Trust Was Only the Beginning

Zero Trust architecture already recognised that network location should not automatically confer trust.

NIST describes Zero Trust as removing implicit trust based on network location or ownership and instead evaluating access to resources using identity, device and contextual information. (NIST)

This was an important transition:

trust the network ↓ verify the actor
But advanced AI may require another transition:

verify the actor ↓ verify the action ↓ verify the authority ↓ verify the resulting state ↓ verify the provenance
Zero Trust says:

> Never implicitly trust the requester.

A post-defensibility architecture would say:

> Never implicitly trust the system either.

---

## 10. Security Becomes Verification

Traditional security focuses heavily on preventing unauthorised actions.

A post-defensibility model assumes some unauthorised actions will inevitably occur.

Its stronger objective becomes:

> Unauthorised actions must not be able to create authoritative truth.

That is a profound architectural difference.

Consider:

**Traditional security**

attack ↓ prevent access ↓ protect database
versus:

**Post-defensibility security**

attack ↓ system may be compromised ↓ attacker modifies representation ↓ modification lacks valid proof ↓ society rejects modification
The information system may fail.

The truth represented by the system does not necessarily fail with it.

This is the critical separation.

---

## 11. Secure the Meaning, Not Merely the Medium

For decades cybersecurity has concentrated on securing the medium carrying information.

- Networks.
- Servers.
- Endpoints.
- Databases.
- Applications.
- Cloud environments.

Those remain important.

But if sufficiently capable intelligence can repeatedly defeat those defensive surfaces, the enduring security primitive must move closer to the semantic layer.

Not merely:

> Was this packet encrypted?

but:

- Who made this claim?
- What exactly does it claim?
- Under what authority?
- Using which evidence?
- Relative to what previous state?
- Was the transition permitted?
- Can another party independently verify it?

This is security of meaning.

And meaning has structure.

---

## 12. Verified Context Graphs

This is where Verified Context Graphs become particularly interesting.

Rather than treating an isolated data value as authoritative, the value exists inside a graph of relationships, evidence, authority and provenance.

For example:

            ┌───────────────┐
            │ Organisation  │
            └───────┬───────┘
                    │ issued
                    ▼
             ┌──────────────┐
             │ Credential   │
             └──────┬───────┘
                    │ asserts
                    ▼
              ┌─────────────┐
              │   Claim     │
              └──────┬──────┘
                     │ about
                     ▼
              ┌─────────────┐
              │   Entity    │
              └─────────────┘
proof ─────────────► Claim authority ─────────► Organisation previous state ────► Claim policy ─────────────► Claim
An AI can fabricate arbitrary text describing this graph.

But fabrication is different from constructing a graph whose cryptographic relationships, authority chains and state transitions independently verify.

The aim is not to make lies impossible.

It is to make unverified lies computationally distinguishable from verified claims.

---

## 13. AI Changes From Threat to Verifier

This produces an interesting inversion.

Advanced AI may be the technology that makes current information systems indefensible.

But advanced AI may also be necessary to operate the replacement architecture.

Humans cannot manually inspect billions of provenance relationships.

AI can.

An intelligent agent could evaluate an incoming claim against a graph containing:

- identity,
- authority,
- credentials,
- history,
- policy,
- dependencies,
- prior state,
- counterparty evidence,
- and cryptographic proofs.

The agent no longer asks:

> “Does this look legitimate?”

It asks:

> “Can this claim be reconciled with verified reality?”

AI therefore becomes both:

- attacker of representations  
**and**  
- interpreter of proofs

This could produce a strange but stable equilibrium.

Abundant intelligence attacks anything based purely on obscurity or institutional assertion.

The same abundant intelligence makes deep verification cheap enough to perform continuously.

---

## 14. Society Becomes Proof-Oriented

If information systems become persistently compromiseable, many everyday interactions would evolve from trust-oriented to proof-oriented systems.

A bank would not merely tell you your balance.

It would be capable of proving the sequence of authorised transactions producing it.

A government would not merely assert that you hold a licence.

You would hold a independently verifiable credential.

A company would not merely claim that someone approved a contract.

The approval would carry independently verifiable authority.

Media would increasingly contain provenance information describing its creation and modification history. C2PA is already developing standards specifically intended to provide cryptographically verifiable content provenance and tamper evidence. (C2PA)

The important property is independence.

Verification should not require asking the same potentially compromised system that made the claim.

---

## 15. The Rise of Sovereign Information

Today’s digital identity often amounts to:

> organisation has database entry about person

The institution therefore possesses the operational representation of the person’s identity.

In a post-defensibility world, identity may increasingly become something the individual can independently prove.

The same transformation can occur with organisations, devices, agents and assets.

Instead of:

central system owns truth
we move toward:

entities issue claims entities hold evidence independent parties verify claims
Authority becomes distributed across cryptographically verifiable relationships.

This does not mean institutions disappear.

It means institutions stop being required to operate universally trusted databases.

They become issuers, witnesses, rule-makers, arbitrators and participants in verifiable networks.

---

## 16. Institutions Become Witnesses Rather Than Databases

This may fundamentally alter institutional architecture.

A university’s primary digital role would no longer be maintaining the world’s authoritative database of graduates.

Its role would be:

> to attest that a particular person earned a particular qualification.

A government land registry would not merely store a property table.

It would participate in validating an authorised chain of ownership transitions.

A professional body would issue evidence of standing.

A regulator would attest compliance outcomes.

A court would attest legal determinations.

An insurer would attest contractual obligations and claim events.

Institutions therefore move from:

> custodians of records

toward:

> authorities over specific claims

This is a much narrower—and potentially much more resilient—role.

---

## 17. The Attack Surface Shrinks

Current enterprise systems expose enormous semantic surfaces.

An attacker gaining sufficient privilege can potentially manipulate thousands of meaningful states.

- Customers.
- Payments.
- Invoices.
- Permissions.
- Policies.
- Documents.
- Workflows.
- Logs.
- Approvals.

An architecture based on constrained, independently verifiable state transitions drastically reduces the meaningful consequences of compromise.

The attacker might control the application.

But the application cannot legitimately invent an authorised signature.

The attacker might alter a database.

But altered state does not reconcile with prior signed events.

The attacker might generate a fake qualification.

But no appropriate issuer attested it.

The attacker might create a fake transaction.

But the required counterparties never authorised it.

The objective becomes reducing the semantic authority of software.

**Software executes.**  
**Evidence authorises.**

---

## 18. The Principle of Minimum Authority

In the pre-AI world, administrators routinely possess enormous power.

- root
- administrator
- database owner
- cloud administrator
- superuser

These roles exist partly because humans require convenient mechanisms to operate complicated infrastructure.

Advanced AI makes such broad authority increasingly dangerous.

If one credential allows an intelligent adversary to change an entire organisation’s reality, that credential represents catastrophic concentrated risk.

The post-defensibility architecture therefore pushes toward:

minimum authority + minimum mutable state + minimum implicit trust + maximum independent verification
No actor—including AI—should possess more authority than required for a specific state transition.

---

## 19. Economic Consequences

If the transition did not occur quickly enough, severe economic effects could follow.

Digital risk premiums would rise.

Cyber insurance could become increasingly expensive or unavailable for classes of systems whose integrity could no longer be meaningfully guaranteed.

Transactions requiring strong confidence could slow.

Organisations might return temporarily to human verification, physical documentation or out-of-band confirmation.

Smaller organisations unable to maintain AI-scale cyber defence could become disproportionately exposed.

The NCSC already warns of the possibility of a widening divide between systems capable of keeping pace with AI-enabled threats and those that cannot. (National Cyber Security Centre)

This could generate an information-security version of inequality.

Large institutions might initially survive by purchasing ever more sophisticated defensive AI.

Small organisations might not.

A verification-oriented architecture offers another possibility:

> make security less dependent on the size of the defender.

Cryptographic verification has a remarkable property.

A small organisation and a giant organisation can verify the same signature.

Truth does not need to become stronger merely because the attacker became richer.

---

## 20. Political Consequences

The political implications may be even more important.

A democracy requires credible records.

- Votes.
- Electoral rolls.
- Legislation.
- Government decisions.
- Public expenditure.
- Ownership.
- Licences.
- Court orders.
- Citizenship.

If these become merely entries inside potentially compromised information systems, confidence in government itself can deteriorate.

The threat is therefore not simply manipulation.

It is **plausible manipulation**.

Even an uncompromised system becomes politically difficult to trust if citizens reasonably believe it could have been compromised.

The ability to prove that something was not fabricated after the fact therefore becomes part of democratic infrastructure.

Transparency alone is insufficient.

The future requirement is likely:

**verifiable transparency**.

---

## 21. The Epistemic Commons

Society has historically depended upon shared information environments.

- Libraries.
- Archives.
- Newspapers.
- Universities.
- Courts.
- Scientific institutions.
- Government records.

These institutions form an epistemic commons: mechanisms through which societies establish shared understandings of events and facts.

Generative AI already challenges this commons through cheap synthetic information.

Cyber-capable advanced AI could challenge the underlying records from which trusted institutions operate.

The response cannot simply be better content moderation.

The response may require a new epistemic infrastructure in which claims have visible provenance and evidence graphs.

The web gave humanity:

> information connected by links

The next architecture may need:

> claims connected by proofs

---

## 22. From the Web of Information to the Web of Verification

The original web solved a distribution problem.

> How can billions of documents be connected?

Search engines solved a discovery problem.

> How can relevant information be found?

Generative AI increasingly solves a synthesis problem.

> How can information be interpreted and recombined?

But abundant machine intelligence creates a new dominant problem:

> How do we know which information deserves to influence action?

This suggests an evolutionary sequence:

Web information ↓ Search information retrieval ↓ Generative AI information synthesis ↓ Advanced AI abundant intelligence ↓ Verification Networks trusted context
The next great infrastructure challenge may therefore not be generating intelligence.

It may be grounding intelligence.

---

## 23. A Post-Defensibility Architecture

The resulting architecture would not abandon cybersecurity.

Firewalls, encryption, isolation, authentication, hardware security and defensive AI would remain important.

But their role changes.

They become layers of resilience rather than the ultimate basis of truth.

A mature system might look more like:

Applications become replaceable interfaces over this structure.

Databases become caches and indexes.

Cloud providers become infrastructure providers.

AI becomes an interpreter and actor.

But none is independently capable of redefining reality.

---

## 24. Compromise Without Catastrophe

This leads to perhaps the defining design objective of the intelligence age:

> Systems should be designed so that compromise does not imply authority.

Today:

compromise ≈ control ≈ ability to alter reality
Tomorrow we may need:

compromise
temporary operational disruption but not ability to manufacture legitimate state
A server can be rebuilt.

An application can be replaced.

A database can be regenerated.

An AI agent can be discarded.

What matters is preserving the verifiable chain from which legitimate state can be reconstructed.

This is dramatically different from merely having backups.

A backup preserves bytes.

A verifiable event history preserves meaning and authority.

---

## 25. Reality Becomes Reconstructable

The strongest information systems may therefore resemble fault-tolerant models of reality.

The question becomes not:

> Can we guarantee that every computer faithfully stores reality forever?

but:

> Can legitimate reality be reconstructed even after arbitrary computers have failed?

That is a much more powerful property.

It resembles the philosophy behind distributed systems, blockchains, event sourcing, cryptographic ledgers, KERI-style key event logs, verifiable credentials and Verified Context Graphs.

None individually solves the entire problem.

Together they point toward a deeper principle:

> Do not defend the representation of reality as though it were reality itself.

Instead:

> make reality independently reconstructable from verifiable evidence.

---

## 26. The Post-Defence Principle

We can therefore state a general principle for information architecture in the age of advanced intelligence:

> Assume that any sufficiently complex information system can eventually be compromised. Design the surrounding trust architecture so that compromise cannot independently create legitimate truth.

This goes beyond “assume breach.”

Assume breach still imagines a system that defenders eventually restore to a trusted state.

The stronger model is:

**Assume persistent compromiseability.**

The infrastructure may never be permanently safe.

Yet the information ecosystem can remain trustworthy.

That is a much stronger form of resilience.

---

## 27. What Survives

If advanced AI eventually makes conventional information systems indefensible, computers do not disappear.

Cloud computing does not disappear.

Databases do not disappear.

AI does not disappear.

Institutions do not disappear.

Instead their roles change.

The database stops being truth.

The application stops being authority.

The administrator stops being omnipotent.

The institution stops asking society simply to trust its infrastructure.

And AI stops being allowed to act merely because it gained access.

What survives is something closer to:

identity + authority + provenance + evidence + rules + verified transitions + reconstructable history
Together these form a verified context of reality.

---

## Conclusion

Advanced AI may eventually force information security to confront an uncomfortable possibility.

Perhaps increasingly intelligent software cannot indefinitely be kept outside increasingly complex software systems.

If so, cybersecurity cannot win merely by building higher walls.

The architecture itself must change.

The critical transition would be:

protect systems ↓ protect data ↓ protect identity ↓ protect provenance ↓ verify claims ↓ verify context ↓ preserve reconstructable reality
The ultimate objective is no longer an unbreachable computer.

There may be no such thing.

The objective becomes something more powerful:

**A society whose truth survives the breach.**

In that world, advanced AI could compromise servers, applications, networks and databases without automatically gaining the ability to rewrite legitimate reality.

Security would no longer mean ensuring that nothing bad ever enters the system.

It would mean ensuring that whatever enters the system must still pass through independently verifiable structures before society accepts it as true.

The information age was built around storing and transmitting information.

The intelligence age may have to be built around something deeper:

**verifying reality.**
