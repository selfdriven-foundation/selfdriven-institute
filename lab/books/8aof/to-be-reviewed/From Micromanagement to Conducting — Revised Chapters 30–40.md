# Part V — Making the Shift

# Chapter 30

## Stop Starting with Tasks

Organisations solve problems by creating work.

A problem appears.

Create a project.

Create a workstream.

Create tasks.

Assign owners.

Set dates.

Track progress.

This reflex is so deeply embedded that even attempts to escape task-centric management often begin by creating more tasks.

An organisation decides:

> We need greater autonomy.

So it creates an autonomy program.

A project manager is appointed.

Workshops are scheduled.

A roadmap is developed.

Tasks are assigned.

Progress is reported.

The organisation is using the architecture of task control to implement freedom from task control.

This is not necessarily useless.

Transformation requires action.

But the sequence matters.

Conducting begins one level above the tasks.

### Conditions before actions

The framework offers a practical transformation pattern:

1. State the Direction.
2. Explain the Engagement context.
3. Define the Enablement sought.
4. Establish the Protocols.
5. Check Sustainability.
6. Design the Processes.
7. Build in Accountability.
8. Synchronise through Organisational conducting.
9. Allow local actions and tasks to emerge.
10. Reflect and adapt.



Notice where tasks appear.

Ninth.

This is deliberate.

Tasks are implementation.

They make more sense after the organisational conditions surrounding them are understood.

### Compare two instructions

Imagine a team receiving:

> Complete these fifteen tasks by the end of the month.

The team can execute.

Now imagine the same team receiving:

Here is what we are trying to make possible.

Here is why it matters.

Here are the relevant constraints.

Here is what good looks like.

Here are the Protocols that apply.

Here is the authority you possess.

Here is the evidence we need.

Here are the situations that require escalation.

Here are the outcomes we will reflect upon.

Now the team can do more than execute.

It can think.

The tasks may still exist.

But some may disappear.

Others may change.

New useful actions may emerge.

The organisation has moved from predefining movement towards creating conditions for appropriate movement.

### The task-list trap

A common failure occurs when organisations adopt the Eight Areas and immediately create:

Direction tasks.

Engagement tasks.

Enablement tasks.

Protocol tasks.

Sustainability tasks.

Process tasks.

Accountability tasks.

Organisational tasks.

The framework has now become another project board.

The source framework explicitly identifies this as an anti-pattern. The Areas of Focus are intended to maintain organisational conditions and focus, not become categories for another task system.

Tasks can exist within the Areas.

But tasks are not the Areas.

This distinction must remain clear.

### What starting with conditions changes

Suppose a company wants to improve customer response times.

The task-first approach may create:

new response-time targets;

daily reporting;

additional training;

manager review;

new escalation tasks.

The conditions-first approach asks:

**Direction**  
What customer experience are we actually trying to enable?

**Engagement**  
Do staff understand why speed matters and where speed should not override care or accuracy?

**Enablement**  
What should customers be able to accomplish?

**Protocols**  
Which decisions can customer-facing staff make independently?

**Sustainability**  
Are delays caused by overloaded people or unnecessary coordination?

**Processes**  
Where does work wait?

**Accountability**  
What evidence actually matters?

**Organisational**  
Which dependencies create the delay?

The resulting tasks may be completely different.

Perhaps the organisation needs fewer reports, not more.

Perhaps the primary intervention is an authority Protocol.

Perhaps the manager approval can disappear.

Perhaps customer information needs to become accessible earlier.

The task list becomes the consequence of understanding rather than the substitute for it.

### Conducting Question

> **Where are we responding to an organisational condition by immediately creating work before understanding what condition actually needs to change?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**What tasks should we create?**

ask:

**What must become true?**

# Chapter 31

## Diagnosing Management Dependency

The shift to conducting should not begin with an organisation-wide redesign.

Begin where dependency is visible.

Find the places where useful activity repeatedly waits for management.

Examples include:

too many approvals;

recurring status meetings;

duplicated reporting;

slow decisions;

people waiting for instructions;

work blocked by unavailable individuals;

senior staff overwhelmed by routine exceptions;

repeated clarification;

cross-team conflict;

manual evidence reconstruction;

AI outputs requiring constant human correction.

These are symptoms.

Do not assume the symptom tells you the cause.

### Start with the visible tension

Suppose employees complain that approvals are slow.

The obvious problem appears to be the approval Process.

Perhaps it is.

But approval may be compensating for:

unclear Direction;

weak Protocols;

ambiguous authority;

poor Accountability;

missing information;

high consequence;

inconsistent Engagement.

Accelerating the approval may simply accelerate the workaround.

The framework's diagnostic workflow therefore begins by separating the visible problem from the underlying system condition.

A useful diagnostic question is:

> **Is the organisation trying to solve missing shared context with more task control?**

### Map across all eight Areas

For each management dependency, examine:

current condition;

tension;

missing information;

dependencies;

possible intervention.

Do not force every Area to contain a problem.

The objective is not completing a framework.

It is understanding the system.

Suppose the issue is:

> Managers review every customer response.

The map may reveal:

**Direction**  
Clear.

**Engagement**  
Tone and customer principles are inconsistently understood.

**Enablement**  
Customer resolution is frequently delayed by review.

**Protocols**  
Authority boundaries are weak.

**Sustainability**  
Managers spend eight hours each week reviewing routine communication.

**Processes**  
Everything follows the same review path regardless of risk.

**Accountability**  
Relevant customer commitments are not reliably captured.

**Organisational**  
Legal and customer-support assumptions differ.

Now the problem looks different.

The manager is not simply interfering.

The review is compensating for several organisational weaknesses.

### Find the primary Areas requiring attention

The framework is systemic.

That does not mean all Areas require equal intervention.

In the example above, Protocols and Engagement may be the primary focus.

Accountability may support them.

Direction may require no meaningful change.

This matters because transformation programs often spread attention too thinly.

Conducting is about focus.

Which condition is creating the most leverage?

Which improvement would eliminate the greatest amount of repeated intervention?

### Move intervention upwards

The framework explicitly recommends moving interventions from activity into conditions.

Instead of:

> Require daily status reports.

consider:

> Improve Accountability and Organisational observability so status does not need to be manually reconstructed.

Instead of:

> Manager approves every customer response.

consider:

> Define Direction, Engagement principles, Protocols, escalation boundaries and Accountability evidence so authorised actors can respond independently.



This is the diagnostic heart of conducting.

### Conducting Question

> **Which management intervention looks like the problem but is actually compensating for a deeper organisational weakness?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we improve this management activity?**

ask:

**Why does the organisation require this management activity?**

# Case Study

## The Software Company Where Every Customer Problem Became a Meeting

Consider an enterprise software company called Cloudline.

A major customer reports a problem.

Support investigates.

The issue might involve Product.

Or configuration.

Or infrastructure.

Or security.

Support opens a ticket.

Engineering asks Product how important the issue is.

Product asks Account Management about customer impact.

Account Management asks Support for history.

Security joins because access might be involved.

Someone creates a meeting.

Five people attend.

The problem is eventually resolved.

Two weeks later, another customer issue produces almost the same meeting.

Cloudline describes itself as collaborative.

Its calendars tell a different story.

### The obvious diagnosis

There are too many meetings.

Management responds with:

meeting rules;

shorter default durations;

better agendas;

fewer attendees.

These changes may help.

But the meeting is not the underlying problem.

The meeting is acting as organisational middleware.

It is the place where:

context is assembled;

severity is interpreted;

authority becomes clear;

dependencies are discovered;

decisions are recorded;

people synchronise.

Remove the meeting without replacing these functions and the organisation loses coherence.

### The conducting diagnosis

**Direction**  
Everyone agrees customer reliability matters, but trade-offs against roadmap commitments are inconsistent.

**Engagement**  
Different teams interpret "critical customer issue" differently.

**Enablement**  
The real output is rapid resolution and organisational learning, not completion of incident tickets.

**Protocols**  
Severity definitions and ownership boundaries are unclear.

**Sustainability**  
Senior engineers and product leaders spend significant time reconstructing routine context.

**Processes**  
Information crosses systems manually.

**Accountability**  
Evidence and reasoning from previous incidents are fragmented.

**Organisational**  
The meeting synchronises all the above.

### The shift

Cloudline creates shared incident Protocols.

Severity carries common meaning.

Relevant customer and technical context is assembled automatically.

Normal ownership paths become explicit.

Recurring incident classes improve the Process.

High-consequence events still generate human coordination.

Routine issues no longer require a five-person synchronisation meeting.

### What happened to the meeting?

Some meetings disappeared.

The remaining meetings became more valuable.

Participants arrived with shared context.

The meeting was no longer where everyone discovered what was happening.

It became the place where people exercised genuinely cross-organisational judgement.

### Case Lesson

> **Before removing a meeting, understand which missing organisational condition the meeting is currently providing.**

# Chapter 32

## A Practical Transformation Pattern

Conducting becomes useful when it changes actual organisational behaviour.

Consider a recurring approval.

Every external customer report must be reviewed by a manager.

The organisation wants to remove the bottleneck.

A simplistic transformation says:

> Reports no longer require approval.

This removes the mechanism.

It does not replace its function.

Conducting proceeds differently.

### Direction

Why does the report exist?

To create reliable shared understanding of the customer's situation.

### Engagement

What principles should report authors understand?

Accuracy.

Usefulness.

Clarity.

Respect for customer context.

### Enablement

What should become possible?

Customers and internal teams can make better decisions.

### Protocols

What shared structures reduce uncertainty?

Approved evidence sources.

Definitions.

Privacy boundaries.

Reporting schema.

Materiality thresholds.

Escalation criteria.

### Sustainability

How much recurring attention should reporting consume?

Normal reports should not require senior review.

### Processes

How does reporting happen reliably?

Collect signals.

Identify material change.

Prepare analysis.

Escalate only exceptions.

### Accountability

How can claims remain trustworthy?

Evidence remains linked.

Sources are identifiable.

Material judgement is attributable.

### Organisational

How does the output remain synchronised with Product, Support and customer teams?

Only after these conditions exist does the approval disappear.

The difference is important.

Control was not removed.

Its function moved into:

context;

Protocol;

Process;

evidence.

### Transformation without pendulum swings

Organisations often alternate between extremes.

Too much control.

Then empowerment.

Then inconsistency.

Then control again.

The conducting pattern avoids the pendulum.

Do not remove structure indiscriminately.

Ask which functions the structure performs.

Then move those functions to the most appropriate layer.

Routine coordination becomes Process.

Normal decision boundaries become Protocol.

Verifiability becomes Accountability.

Purpose becomes Direction.

System-level tension becomes Organisational attention.

Human intervention remains where judgement deserves it.

### The general pattern

When a recurring management intervention appears:

**1. Identify its function.**

What risk, information, authority or coordination is it supplying?

**2. Identify whether the function can become structural.**

Can it move into Direction, Engagement, Protocols, Process or Accountability?

**3. Preserve human intervention for material exceptions.**

Do not eliminate judgement where judgement matters.

**4. Release bounded agency.**

Allow normal action to occur locally.

**5. Observe the result.**

Did coherence hold?

**6. Reflect and adapt.**

The organisation becomes capable through iteration.

### Conducting Question

> **Which control can be removed only after its useful function has been relocated into stronger organisational conditions?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we remove control?**

ask:

**What function is the control currently providing, and where should that function live?**

# Chapter 33

## The First Ninety Days

A transition towards conducting does not require an enormous transformation program.

In fact, beginning with one may reproduce the very architecture the organisation is trying to change.

Start with one management dependency.

Something visible.

One recurring approval.

One meeting.

One report.

One escalation.

One indispensable person.

One human review loop around an AI system.

The aim is not to transform the whole organisation.

It is to learn how to remove unnecessary management dependency without losing coherence.

## Days 1–30: Observe

Do not redesign too quickly.

Watch reality.

Where does work wait?

Which questions repeat?

Which information is missing?

Where is authority ambiguous?

Which decisions depend on undocumented knowledge?

Which exceptions occur regularly?

How much attention is consumed?

Where does the formal Process differ from the lived Process?

Map the issue across the Eight Areas.

The objective is to understand what the management intervention is actually doing.

### Week 1 — Find the dependency

Choose something recurring.

Avoid something so large that cause and effect become impossible to observe.

### Week 2 — Understand its function

Ask:

What breaks if this intervention disappears tomorrow?

What risk does it control?

What context does it provide?

What decision does it legitimise?

### Week 3 — Map the Areas

Identify the primary tension.

Do not force equal attention across all eight.

### Week 4 — Measure attention

Include:

time spent acting;

time spent waiting;

time spent asking;

time spent reconstructing;

time spent reviewing.

Attention cost is often much larger than the visible activity suggests.

## Days 31–60: Redesign conditions

Now move upwards.

Clarify Direction.

Improve Engagement.

Define Enablement.

Strengthen Protocols.

Reduce Sustainability pressure.

Redesign Processes.

Build in Accountability.

Improve Organisational synchronisation.

The objective is not:

> Tell the manager to stop interfering.

It is:

> Make routine interference less necessary.

### Weeks 5–6 — Strengthen context

Make the relevant intent, information and boundaries accessible.

### Weeks 7–8 — Improve architecture

Clarify normal authority.

Improve Protocols.

Simplify Process.

Generate better evidence.

Make exceptions more meaningful.

## Days 61–90: Release agency

Now reduce the management intervention.

Not everywhere at once.

Inside explicit boundaries.

Observe.

Does work proceed?

Does quality remain acceptable?

Do people understand their authority?

Does evidence improve?

Do unexpected behaviours appear?

Has management effort genuinely reduced?

Or did it simply move elsewhere?

### Week 9 — Test bounded agency

Choose a defined normal case and remove the old approval or coordination step.

### Week 10 — Observe failure without immediately restoring control

Something may go wrong.

Do not automatically conclude:

> We need the old approval back.

Ask:

Was Direction unclear?

Was a Protocol incomplete?

Was Engagement weak?

Was capability missing?

Was the Process poorly designed?

Was the actor genuinely outside legitimate authority?

The distinction matters.

### Week 11 — Improve the condition

Fix the systemic weakness revealed by the failure.

### Week 12 — Expand

Apply the method to another dependency.

The organisation learns conducting through repeated cycles.

### What ninety days should produce

Not a finished transformation.

Something more important.

Evidence that:

less intervention can coexist with stronger Accountability;

greater agency can coexist with coherence;

leaders can remove recurring work by improving conditions;

management dependency is not inevitable.

This creates confidence for the larger transition.

### Conducting Question

> **What is the smallest recurring management dependency we could redesign deeply enough to prove that coherent agency is possible?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we transform the organisation?**

ask:

**Where can we demonstrate the new operating logic first?**

# Chapter 34

## The Conducting Meeting

Meetings reveal how an organisation thinks about management.

A task-centric meeting often sounds like:

What did you do?

What are you doing next?

Is the task complete?

Why is this late?

Who owns this?

When will it be finished?

These questions can be useful.

But when they dominate management interaction, leadership becomes a status-collection mechanism.

A conducting meeting asks different questions.

### Direction

What changed?

Which assumptions need reflection?

Are we drifting from intent?

### Engagement

What is being misunderstood?

Where does the lived organisation contradict the stated one?

### Enablement

What useful outcome is appearing?

What activity is failing to translate into usefulness?

### Protocols

Where are people repeatedly asking permission?

Where does interaction depend on folklore?

### Sustainability

Where is human attention being consumed?

What depends on heroic effort?

### Processes

Which recurring friction deserves redesign?

Where is work waiting?

### Accountability

Which significant activity is difficult to establish or verify?

Are we substituting visibility for evidence?

### Organisational

What is out of sync?

Which dependencies or competing priorities need attention?

### Not an eight-part agenda

A common mistake would be turning this into another mandatory agenda:

ten minutes Direction;

ten minutes Engagement;

ten minutes Enablement.

That would miss the point.

All Areas persist.

Not all deserve equal attention in every meeting.

If Accountability is healthy, move on.

If Protocols are stable, do not manufacture a Protocol discussion.

The framework's central conducting question is:

> **What needs our attention now?**

The meeting should follow attention.

Not ritual.

### Status should increasingly be ambient

Another shift occurs.

Meetings should not exist primarily to reconstruct state that systems could already reveal.

If the manager spends half the meeting asking:

Where is this?

What happened?

Has that been completed?

the organisation may have an observability problem.

Status is valuable.

Manual status reconstruction is often management debt.

The more normal organisational state can be accessed asynchronously, the more synchronous human time can focus on:

uncertainty;

interpretation;

trade-offs;

reflection;

relationships;

cross-system judgement.

This increases the value of meetings by reducing the need for them.

### The test

A useful test for a recurring meeting is:

> **If everyone already knew the current state before they entered the room, would this meeting still need to exist?**

If the answer is no, the meeting may be a reporting mechanism.

If the answer is yes, the meeting may contain genuine conducting work.

### Conducting Question

> **How much of our meeting time exists to reconstruct organisational state rather than interpret it?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we run better status meetings?**

ask:

**What human judgement deserves synchronous attention once status is already known?**

# Chapter 35

## Measuring What Matters

What does progress towards conducting look like?

Not simply fewer managers.

An understaffed organisation can have few managers and terrible agency.

Not more automation.

Automation can accelerate bureaucracy.

Not fewer rules.

Weak Protocols can increase management dependency.

Not more freedom.

Agency without coherence can fragment the system.

The target is **coherent agency**.

Measurement should therefore ask whether useful action can increasingly occur without unnecessary central intervention while preserving organisational coherence.

### 1. Decision latency

How long does a normal decision wait for permission?

Long waits may indicate unclear authority or unnecessary approval.

### 2. Approval density

What proportion of routine actions require human approval?

Are approvals concentrated where consequence genuinely warrants them?

### 3. Clarification demand

How often do people ask questions the organisation has effectively answered before?

Repeated clarification may indicate missing Engagement or Protocols.

### 4. Management attention cost

How much senior attention is consumed by:

status;

routine review;

repeated questions;

manual coordination;

reconstruction?

### 5. Evidence completeness

Can material events be reconstructed without extraordinary effort?

### 6. Exception quality

Do escalations represent genuinely unusual situations?

Or has the exception queue become the normal Process?

### 7. Directional coherence

Do locally initiated actions remain connected to organisational Direction?

### 8. Enablement

What useful capability or outcome is improving?

### 9. Sustainability

Can activity scale without equivalent growth in coordination overhead?

### 10. Learning velocity

How quickly does repeated friction cause the system to change?

The source framework recommends related reflection signals, including fewer unnecessary approvals, reduced waiting, less repeated clarification, improved quality, stronger traceability, reduced human attention cost, better alignment and faster autonomous action within boundaries.

### Measure conditions, not just people

This represents an important shift.

Traditional management measurement often asks:

Which individual is performing?

Conducting also asks:

Which condition is helping or constraining performance?

Suppose a customer team takes too long to respond.

The conventional metric identifies individual response time.

Useful.

But the conducting analysis might also measure:

how long responses wait for approval;

how many systems staff must search;

how many exceptions require manager judgement;

how often Protocols are unclear.

The individual remains accountable for their part.

The organisation becomes accountable for its part too.

### Avoid turning conducting into another scorecard

Every useful measure can become dysfunctional if optimised mechanically.

If leadership targets:

"reduce approval density by 50 per cent"

managers may simply rename approvals.

If leadership rewards:

"fewer escalations"

people may stop escalating important risk.

Measurement is therefore for reflection.

Not merely control.

The purpose of a conducting metric is to help the organisation see whether its operating conditions are improving.

### Conducting Question

> **Do our metrics tell us only what people are doing, or also whether the organisation is making good action easier?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Who is performing poorly?**

ask:

**Which conditions are shaping the performance we see?**

# Chapter 36

## The Conducting Maturity Model

Organisations do not move from micromanagement to mature conducting in one step.

Agency develops.

Context develops.

Protocols mature.

Evidence improves.

Leadership behaviour changes.

A maturity model can help describe the progression.

It should not become a ranking system.

Different circumstances may legitimately require different degrees of centralisation.

The purpose is orientation.

## Stage 1 — Directed

Work depends heavily on explicit instruction.

Managers allocate tasks.

Approvals are common.

Context is concentrated.

Evidence is often reconstructed.

People learn that asking is safer than acting.

This model can work in:

small organisations;

highly repetitive environments;

early training situations;

crisis conditions.

It scales poorly when judgement becomes important.

## Stage 2 — Delegated

Managers transfer greater responsibility.

Teams receive ownership.

Decision rights widen.

People gain discretion.

But management still originates much of the activity and context.

The organisation says:

> You own this.

Agency exists mainly inside assigned domains.

This is a meaningful improvement.

It is not yet conducting.

## Stage 3 — Contextual

The organisation begins making shared context explicit.

Direction becomes more accessible.

Engagement improves.

Protocols replace recurring permission.

Normal authority becomes clearer.

Processes produce better evidence.

People can recognise appropriate action without waiting for tasks.

This is where genuine agency begins becoming systemic.

## Stage 4 — Conducted

The Eight Areas are actively maintained as an interconnected system.

Managers increasingly work at the condition layer.

Routine activity proceeds without continuous central instruction.

Human attention concentrates on:

exceptions;

Direction;

reflection;

relationships;

synchronisation.

Accountability becomes increasingly generated through operation rather than separate reporting.

The organisation is not managerless.

Its managers are becoming conductors.

## Stage 5 — Agentic

Humans and autonomous systems both exercise bounded agency.

Organisational context is explicit enough to support machine-speed activity.

Agents operate through Protocols.

Consequential activity creates evidence.

Human attention concentrates on:

purpose;

legitimacy;

significant exceptions;

values;

relationships;

novel judgement;

system reflection.

The organisation can operate at different speeds while remaining coherent.

### Maturity does not mean maximum autonomy

Stage 5 is not the destination for every activity.

A nuclear safety Process should not pursue autonomy simply because autonomy sounds mature.

A trainee may appropriately work under close guidance.

A crisis may require temporarily concentrated authority.

A deeply consequential decision may always require human approval.

Conducting is not ideological decentralisation.

The appropriate degree of agency depends on:

capability;

consequence;

context;

evidence;

uncertainty;

legitimacy.

A mature organisation knows **why** a particular level of control exists.

That is more important than maximising or minimising it.

### Conducting Question

> **Where is our organisation genuinely ready for greater agency, and where would increasing autonomy simply expose immature conditions?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How autonomous should we become?**

ask:

**What degree of agency is legitimate given our capability, context and consequences?**

# Part VI — The Organisation Ahead

# Chapter 37

## Beyond the Organisational Chart

Ask someone to draw their organisation.

They will probably draw a tree.

Board.

Chief executive.

Executives.

Managers.

Teams.

Employees.

Lines connect them.

The lines describe authority and reporting relationships.

This is useful.

It is also increasingly incomplete.

### The organisation already exceeds the chart

Real work flows through:

customers;

contractors;

partners;

suppliers;

regulators;

software;

cloud platforms;

APIs;

automations;

communities;

AI agents.

A customer claim may involve:

a customer;

claims staff;

a repairer;

payment infrastructure;

external data;

an AI assistant;

fraud systems;

regulatory obligations;

a manager.

The organisational chart may represent one or two of these relationships.

Yet all influence the actual outcome.

### The chart is an authority map

This suggests a useful distinction.

The organisational chart is not the organisation.

It is primarily a representation of selected authority relationships.

As work becomes more networked and agentic, other relationships become equally important.

Information relationships.

Protocol relationships.

Dependency relationships.

Credential relationships.

Evidence relationships.

Tool relationships.

Customer relationships.

The organisation begins to look less like a hierarchy and more like a network.

### Hierarchy does not disappear

This is not an argument that hierarchy becomes irrelevant.

Boards still possess legal authority.

Executives still carry responsibilities.

Managers still lead people.

Certain decisions legitimately remain centralised.

Conducting is not a claim that networks replace hierarchy.

It is a claim that hierarchy alone no longer describes how organisational coherence is produced.

Authority may remain hierarchical while action becomes increasingly networked.

### What should we draw instead?

A conducting representation might ask:

Who or what can act?

Under whose authority?

What information can they access?

Which Protocols connect them?

What evidence do they produce?

Which dependencies exist?

Where does Direction come from?

Where does attention move when the system becomes incoherent?

This creates a much richer view.

The organisation becomes a network of agency operating inside shared conditions.

### Conducting Question

> **Which relationships critical to our actual operation are invisible on our organisational chart?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Who reports to whom?**

ask:

**How does agency actually move through the organisation?**

# Chapter 38

## The Organisation as a Context Network

If the organisational chart is incomplete, what is the organisation?

One useful answer is:

> **A network of actors connected by context.**

Each actor — human or machine — operates inside a field of information and relationships.

They have:

identity;

capabilities;

authority;

information;

constraints;

commitments;

history;

relationships.

Action occurs where these elements intersect.

### Context surrounds every action

Consider a customer-service employee receiving a request.

The action they take depends on:

who the customer is;

what has happened previously;

what the organisation has promised;

which policies apply;

what authority the employee possesses;

which risks exist;

what other teams are doing;

what evidence is required.

The action is not simply produced by the employee's skill.

It is produced by the interaction between skill and context.

The same applies to an autonomous agent.

### Tacit context worked at small scale

Traditional organisations carry enormous amounts of context socially.

Ask Peter.

Sarah knows.

Use Priya's spreadsheet.

The founder remembers.

Legal normally allows this.

Finance hates that.

The experienced employee knows which rule really matters.

This can work extremely well when people know one another.

Growth makes it brittle.

New employees arrive.

People leave.

Teams distribute.

Organisations collaborate across boundaries.

Agents join.

The context no longer travels reliably through proximity.

### Context becomes infrastructure

High-agency organisations must therefore treat context more seriously.

Not everything should be formalised.

Organisations will always contain tacit knowledge.

Human relationships matter.

Experience matters.

But the organisation should identify context whose absence repeatedly creates management dependency.

Direction.

Authority.

Definitions.

Protocols.

Relevant history.

Evidence.

Obligations.

Dependencies.

The more of this that becomes accessible and trustworthy, the less every interaction must begin by finding the right person.

### Verified context

Access to context is not enough.

Context also has quality.

Which version is current?

Where did this information come from?

Who has authority to change it?

Does it apply to this situation?

Can it be trusted?

This matters for humans.

It becomes critical for AI.

An agent acting autonomously against incorrect organisational context may scale an error faster than a human ever could.

The intelligence-age organisation therefore needs not simply more context.

It needs **reliable context**.

### Conducting the network

Conductors do not need to understand every node.

They need sufficient awareness of the relationships.

Where is context missing?

Where is authority ambiguous?

Where are incompatible interpretations forming?

Where are dependencies concentrating?

Where is evidence weak?

Where does attention need to move?

This is organising at the system layer.

### Conducting Question

> **Which organisational knowledge is so important to coherent action that it should no longer depend entirely on knowing the right person?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Who knows how this works?**

ask:

**How does the organisation itself know how this works?**

# Chapter 39

## Leadership After Management

Conducting does not remove leadership.

It raises the value of leadership.

But the work changes.

Traditional management frequently focuses on:

allocating work;

monitoring execution;

reviewing activity;

resolving exceptions;

collecting status;

approving decisions.

As organisational conditions become stronger, less leadership attention is required for these functions.

What remains?

Some of the most deeply human parts of leadership.

### Direction

What are we trying to make possible?

Which opportunities deserve pursuit?

What should we stop doing?

### Meaning

Why does this matter?

What kind of organisation are we trying to be?

### Legitimacy

Which actions are acceptable?

Who should participate in important decisions?

What consequences are we willing to create?

### Reflection

What have we learned?

Which assumption failed?

How has the environment changed?

### Relationships

Where does trust need attention?

Where is conflict useful?

Where is care required?

### System design

Which organisational conditions make useful behaviour natural?

Where does the architecture produce friction?

### Attention

What deserves human attention now?

This is not less demanding than management.

It may be more demanding.

The conductor cannot hide behind activity.

### The loss of visible control

Traditional managers often experience authority through intervention.

People ask.

The manager answers.

The manager's influence is visible.

Conducting leadership often works indirectly.

A leader clarifies Direction.

Weeks later, a team makes a strong independent decision because of it.

A leader improves a Protocol.

Thousands of routine interactions become easier.

A leader fixes an Accountability weakness.

Managers no longer need to watch a Process.

The leader does not appear inside each outcome.

Their influence exists in the system.

This requires a different understanding of leadership success.

### The leader who can leave the room

A useful test is:

> **What happens if the leader disappears for two weeks?**

Does normal activity stop?

Do decisions accumulate?

Do people wait?

Does Direction disappear?

If so, the leader may still be functioning as organisational infrastructure.

A mature conductor creates capability that persists beyond their presence.

This does not make them unimportant.

It makes their leadership scalable.

### Leadership becomes more human as coordination becomes less human

There is an important paradox.

AI and automation can make leadership more human.

If machines and Protocols absorb:

status collection;

routine coordination;

information retrieval;

standard verification;

repeatable Process;

leaders can spend more time on work machines cannot legitimately inherit simply because they can optimise.

Care.

Meaning.

Values.

Conflict.

Relationships.

Purpose.

Judgement.

Community.

Legitimacy.

The intelligence age does not necessarily reduce the importance of human leadership.

It may strip away administrative activity that has obscured what leadership is actually for.

### Conducting Question

> **If routine coordination disappeared from my role, what leadership work would remain — and am I spending enough attention on it now?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do I stay involved?**

ask:

**Where does my human judgement create unique value?**

# Chapter 40

## The Organisational Shift of Our Time

Every organisational age is shaped by what is scarce.

Industrial organisations faced scarcity of coordination.

Large-scale production required reliable ways of directing human activity.

Management became a technology for turning intent into organised execution.

The knowledge economy changed the constraint.

Expertise moved towards the edge.

The people doing the work increasingly possessed knowledge management could not fully specify.

Organisations responded with greater delegation, teamwork and autonomy.

Now another constraint is changing.

Intelligence itself is becoming easier to access and distribute.

Artificial intelligence can already perform forms of cognitive work that previously required significant human effort.

Agentic systems increasingly move from answering questions towards performing actions.

The organisational problem changes again.

### The old question

The old management question is:

> **What should everyone be doing?**

It assumes that leadership should determine enough activity that the organisation can be coordinated through instruction.

This question made sense under conditions of scarce intelligence.

It becomes increasingly expensive under conditions of distributed intelligence.

### The new question

The conducting question is:

> **What needs our attention now?**

This assumes capable actors already exist.

Humans.

Teams.

Systems.

Agents.

Leadership does not need to originate every useful movement.

Its job is to maintain the conditions through which useful movement remains coherent.

This is a profound shift.

From managing activity.

To conducting agency.

### The scarce-intelligence organisation

```text id="14uvvm"
SCARCE INTELLIGENCE ORGANISATION

Intent
  ↓
Managers
  ↓
Plans
  ↓
Tasks
  ↓
Human Execution
  ↓
Reporting
  ↺
```

Information moves towards management.

Instructions move away from management.

Coherence depends heavily on central interpretation.

### The abundant-intelligence organisation

```text id="0y51uq"
ABUNDANT INTELLIGENCE ORGANISATION

                Direction
                   ↓
             Shared Context
                   ↓
       ┌──────── Agency ────────┐
       │                        │
    Humans                  AI / Systems
       │                        │
       └──────── Action ────────┘
                   ↓
          Evidence + Outcomes
                   ↓
               Reflection
                   ↓
           Conducting Focus
                   ↺
```

This reference model is central to the underlying conducting framework.

The shift is not from hierarchy to anarchy.

It is from central instruction towards shared conditions.

### The organisational transitions

The move from micromanagement to conducting can be summarised through a series of transitions.

**From task to context.**

Tasks remain.

But they stop carrying the entire burden of organisational control.

**From instruction to Direction.**

People understand where the organisation is going and why.

**From communication to Engagement.**

Participants can interpret the organisation, not merely receive messages from it.

**From activity to Enablement.**

Useful outcomes matter more than visible motion.

**From permission to Protocols.**

Routine coordination becomes reusable.

**From utilisation to Sustainability.**

Human attention is treated as finite capital.

**From administration to Process.**

Recurring activity becomes easier rather than bureaucratic.

**From surveillance to Accountability.**

Important actions remain verifiable without requiring continuous observation.

**From coordination to Organisational synchronisation.**

Relationships between Areas receive explicit attention.

**From delegation to agency.**

Useful action can originate locally.

**From management to conducting.**

Leadership moves up an abstraction layer.

### Coherent agency

The goal is not autonomy for its own sake.

Agency without coherence produces fragmentation.

Nor is the goal control for its own sake.

Coherence without agency produces bureaucracy.

The target is:

> **coherent agency without continuous central instruction.**

This is the organising principle of the intelligence-age organisation.

### The role of AI

Artificial intelligence makes the transition urgent.

But AI is not the centre of the book.

The organisational problem existed before AI.

Founders became bottlenecks.

Managers approved routine work.

Meetings compensated for missing context.

Professionals waited for permission.

Organisations reconstructed Accountability after the event.

AI increases the scale.

It increases the speed.

It makes existing architecture more visible.

And it creates the possibility of thousands of new actors operating inside organisational systems.

This produces a choice.

We can use AI to perfect micromanagement.

Observe more.

Measure more.

Control more.

Generate more tasks.

Or we can use intelligence to reduce the need for micromanagement.

Sense.

Interpret.

Explain.

Coordinate.

Verify.

Reflect.

Assist conductors.

The technology does not make this choice for us.

Organisational design does.

### What remains human

As routine cognitive work becomes cheaper, human value does not disappear.

Its location changes.

Purpose.

Care.

Relationships.

Values.

Legitimacy.

Curiosity.

Creativity.

Meaning.

Judgement.

Reflection.

These become more visible when people spend less of their time acting as routers between systems.

This may be one of the most hopeful possibilities of abundant intelligence.

Machines do not have to make organisations less human.

Used well, they can allow organisations to stop spending humans on work that never needed to consume so much humanity in the first place.

### The conductor

The conductor is therefore not another managerial title.

It is a way of understanding leadership.

The conductor sees capable participants.

They do not ask how to control every movement.

They ask what conditions allow those participants to move coherently.

They strengthen Direction.

Make meaning understandable.

Protect useful Enablement.

Establish Protocols.

Maintain Sustainability.

Improve Processes.

Create Accountability.

Synchronise the organisation.

Then they pay attention.

Sense.

Reflect.

Orient.

Focus.

Enable.

Observe.

Synchronise.

Adapt.

The organisation becomes progressively less dependent on constant instruction.

Not because leadership has disappeared.

Because leadership has strengthened the system.

### The final shift

The management organisation says:

> I need to know what everyone is doing.

The conducting organisation asks:

> Do they have what they need to act coherently?

The management organisation says:

> Bring the decision to me.

The conducting organisation asks:

> Why does this decision need to travel?

The management organisation says:

> Show me more activity.

The conducting organisation asks:

> What useful thing are we enabling?

The management organisation says:

> Increase visibility.

The conducting organisation asks:

> What needs to be verifiable?

The management organisation says:

> How do we control AI?

The conducting organisation asks:

> Under what conditions should this form of agency operate?

And the leader stops asking:

> **How do I manage all these movements?**

They begin asking:

> **What needs our attention now?**

That is the shift.

From instruction to context.

From permission to Protocol.

From surveillance to evidence.

From delegation to agency.

From scarce intelligence to abundant intelligence.

From control of the parts to coherence of the whole.

From micromanagement to conducting.
