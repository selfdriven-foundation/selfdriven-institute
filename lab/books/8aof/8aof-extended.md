# Preface

## The Organisation Has Changed, Management Hasn't

There is a strange contradiction at the centre of the modern organisation.

We have never had more information.

We have never had more computing power.

We have never had more ways to communicate.

We have never had more ability to automate routine activity.

And we have never had greater access to intelligence.

Yet many organisations have responded by increasing the amount of management.

More project management.

More reporting.

More dashboards.

More meetings.

More approvals.

More performance indicators.

More planning.

More status updates.

More oversight.

More software that watches work.

More systems that break work into smaller pieces and distribute those pieces to people.

Technology has accelerated, but the underlying management architecture has often remained remarkably familiar.

At the top sits intention.

Below intention sits management.

Management translates intention into plans.

Plans become projects.

Projects become workstreams.

Workstreams become tasks.

Tasks are allocated to people.

People perform them.

Progress is reported upward.

Management compares progress with the plan.

Corrections are issued.

And the cycle repeats.

```text
Intent
   ↓
Management
   ↓
Plans
   ↓
Projects
   ↓
Tasks
   ↓
People
   ↓
Reporting
   ↺
```

This architecture is so familiar that it can appear inevitable.

It isn't.

It emerged from particular historical conditions.

Information was expensive.

Communication was slow.

Expertise was concentrated.

Organisations were difficult to coordinate.

Most workers had access to only a small part of the information available to senior management.

The ability to observe, analyse and decide was scarce.

Hierarchy was therefore not merely an authority structure.

It was an information-processing system.

Management gathered information from below, interpreted it, made decisions and returned simplified instructions downward.

For more than a century, this mechanism helped people coordinate at scales that would otherwise have been impossible.

It built factories.

It ran railways.

It administered governments.

It organised banks.

It coordinated hospitals.

It built global corporations.

We should not dismiss that achievement.

The management organisation was one of the great coordination technologies of the industrial age.

But the conditions beneath it are changing.

Information is no longer inherently scarce.

Analysis is no longer confined to specialists.

Knowledge can move almost instantly.

Processes can be made machine-readable.

Evidence can be captured automatically.

Organisational history can be searchable.

Systems can detect events continuously.

Artificial intelligence can interpret information, identify patterns, create options and increasingly initiate actions.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that organisational AI adoption continued to rise during 2025, while the use of autonomous agents remained substantially earlier in its deployment. The important point is not that every organisation has suddenly become agentic. It is that the technological direction is increasingly clear: organisations are gaining participants that can not only process information but act upon it.

We are moving from a world in which useful intelligence was scarce toward one in which useful intelligence may become abundant.

That changes the central organisational problem.

The question is no longer simply:

> How do we get people to execute the tasks required by the plan?

The emerging question is:

> How do we enable growing numbers of intelligent actors to exercise agency while remaining part of a coherent whole?

That is a different problem.

It requires a different organisational response.

This book calls that response **conducting**.

Conducting does not mean abandoning management discipline.

It does not mean removing structure.

It does not mean eliminating managers.

It does not mean replacing plans with improvisation.

It does not mean telling everyone to do whatever they want.

And it certainly does not mean allowing autonomous AI systems to act without boundaries.

Conducting means moving leadership **up an abstraction layer**.

Instead of continuously directing movements, we establish and maintain the conditions through which good movement can emerge.

Instead of prescribing every action, we establish shared direction.

Instead of repeatedly explaining individual decisions, we create understandable context.

Instead of routing routine decisions through managers, we establish protocols.

Instead of equating visibility with accountability, we create verifiable evidence.

Instead of maximising activity, we protect sustainability.

Instead of supervising every interaction, we improve processes.

Instead of coordinating every individual movement, we synchronise the organisation.

The central leadership question changes from:

> What should everyone be doing?

to:

> **What requires our attention now?**

And the organisational objective becomes:

> **coherent agency without continuous central instruction.**

The framework used throughout this book is organised around eight persistent Areas of Focus:

**Direction.  
Engagement.  
Enablement.  
Protocols.  
Sustainability.  
Processes.  
Accountability.  
Organisational.**

They are not departments.

They are not a hierarchy.

They are not another way of categorising tasks.

They are interconnected organisational conditions. 

A task still matters.

A project still matters.

A plan still matters.

But none of these should be mistaken for the organisation itself.

The organisation is the context in which people, systems, teams and increasingly autonomous agents are able to act together.

The task is merely one local expression of that context.

That distinction lies at the heart of the organisational shift of our time.

# Chapter 1

## The Management Machine

Most organisations possess an invisible machine.

Its inputs are intentions.

Its outputs are tasks.

You can watch this machine operate in almost any organisation.

Leadership decides that customer experience must improve.

Someone creates an initiative.

The initiative becomes a program.

A program manager is appointed.

Meetings begin.

The program becomes a set of projects.

Projects acquire owners.

Milestones are established.

Milestones become work packages.

Work packages become tasks.

Tasks enter a tracking system.

People receive assignments.

Managers monitor completion.

Reports aggregate the results.

Eventually senior leaders receive a dashboard showing whether the original intention is red, amber or green.

The organisation has converted purpose into administrable units.

This is useful.

Complex work must often be decomposed.

Coordination requires some form of representation.

Progress sometimes needs to be visible.

The problem emerges when this translation becomes the **primary mechanism of organisational coherence**.

At that point, anything not represented in the task system begins to disappear.

Learning becomes difficult to represent.

Relationships are difficult to represent.

Judgement is difficult to represent.

Emerging opportunity is difficult to represent.

Context is difficult to represent.

Meaning is difficult to represent.

Yet tasks are wonderfully representable.

They can be counted.

Assigned.

Prioritised.

Estimated.

Scheduled.

Completed.

Reopened.

Reported.

And therefore organisations begin managing what their systems can see.

This produces a subtle inversion.

The task was originally created to help realise the purpose.

Eventually completing the task becomes the purpose.

The project plan was created to support Direction.

Eventually compliance with the plan becomes more important than reflection on whether the Direction still makes sense.

The dashboard was created to help understand reality.

Eventually people change their behaviour to make the dashboard look healthy.

The reporting system was created to increase Accountability.

Eventually enormous effort is spent producing reports while the evidence needed for genuine accountability remains scattered.

The management machine begins to serve itself.

### When the representation replaces reality

Imagine an organisation with a customer problem.

Complaints are increasing.

Leadership responds by creating a customer-improvement project.

Twenty tasks are created.

Nineteen are completed.

The project dashboard shows 95 per cent completion.

But customers remain unhappy.

Has the organisation succeeded?

Of course not.

Yet task-centric systems make this error surprisingly easy.

They confuse implementation activity with Enablement.

They measure what has been done rather than what has become possible.

This is why a conducting organisation keeps returning to the underlying condition.

What are we actually trying to make possible?

What has changed?

What useful outcome is occurring?

What are we learning?

Tasks are implementation artefacts.

They are not organisational truth.

The framework underpinning this book makes the distinction explicit: tasks still exist, but they are local implementation artefacts rather than the primary mechanism of organisational control.

This is a deceptively profound change.

Once tasks stop being the organisational control surface, leadership must find another way to maintain coherence.

That is where conducting begins.

# Chapter 2

## The Industrial Logic of Control

To understand why contemporary organisations behave as they do, it helps to understand where the logic came from.

Industrial production posed a formidable coordination problem.

Large numbers of people had to perform interdependent work.

Materials had to arrive at the correct time.

Machines had to operate in sequence.

Quality had to remain predictable.

Output had to increase.

Costs had to fall.

Variation had to be controlled.

In that environment, breaking work into repeatable activities was extraordinarily powerful.

Frederick Winslow Taylor became the figure most associated with what came to be known as **scientific management**. Working in industrial settings in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Taylor studied work in an attempt to systematically improve productivity. His *Principles of Scientific Management* was published in 1911.

Taylor's world was very different from ours.

The dominant organisational challenge was not how to coordinate thousands of autonomous digital agents.

It was how to organise physical labour and machinery efficiently.

The logic of management therefore focused on questions such as:

What is the best way to perform the work?

How should the activity be divided?

Who should determine the method?

How should performance be measured?

How should output be increased?

Taylorism is often caricatured today, but that risks missing why it mattered.

It represented an attempt to replace informal, inconsistent work organisation with systematic understanding.

The enormous gains produced by industrial coordination helped shape the modern world.

But every successful architecture leaves descendants.

We still see Taylor's conceptual fingerprint in places far removed from the factory.

Time tracking.

Activity targets.

Call handling quotas.

Productivity scores.

Detailed workflow scripts.

Standard operating procedures.

Work decomposition.

Employee surveillance.

Algorithmically allocated gig work.

Digital dashboards showing whether individuals are "active".

The stopwatch has become software.

### The separation of thinking and doing

The deeper legacy is not merely measurement.

It is the organisational separation between those who determine work and those who perform it.

Thinking happens somewhere.

Execution happens somewhere else.

That distinction shaped generations of management systems.

Senior leadership determines strategy.

Middle management translates strategy.

Employees execute.

Information returns upward.

The hierarchy acts as the interpretation layer.

For repetitive industrial work this structure can be effective.

But difficulty arises when we apply the same architecture to work that depends upon judgement, relationships, creativity, specialist expertise and rapidly changing information.

The more intelligent the edge of the organisation becomes, the stranger it is to insist that every meaningful decision travel inward before action can travel outward.

The industrial organisation solved the coordination problem partly by concentrating decision-making.

The conducting organisation solves it differently.

It strengthens shared conditions so decision-making can be distributed without losing coherence.

# Chapter 3

## From Taylor to the Knowledge Worker

Management did not stop evolving with scientific management.

As work changed, management thinkers increasingly recognised the limits of treating organisations as machines.

Peter Drucker was among the most influential.

In 1954 he published *The Practice of Management*, helping establish management as an integrated discipline, and in 1959 he coined the term **knowledge work**.

The knowledge worker created a problem for traditional management.

When important productive knowledge sits inside the worker's own expertise, management cannot completely specify the work from above.

The software engineer knows things the manager does not.

The surgeon possesses specialised judgement.

The researcher encounters information that could not have been anticipated in the project plan.

The teacher interprets a classroom in real time.

The designer notices an emerging pattern.

The lawyer sees a subtle risk.

The field engineer encounters conditions headquarters has never experienced.

The person doing the work increasingly becomes part of the thinking system.

Drucker anticipated organisations with fewer layers of management and specialists who directed much of their own performance through information and feedback. In 1988 he described the emerging "information-based organisation" and pointed to the symphony orchestra as one analogy for a knowledge-based organisation of specialists.

This is especially interesting in the context of conducting.

The orchestra metaphor is not new.

But abundant intelligence changes its significance.

We are no longer dealing only with increasingly capable human specialists.

We are beginning to introduce increasingly capable machine participants as well.

### Deming and the system

W. Edwards Deming pushed in another important direction.

Deming argued that quality should be built into the system rather than achieved primarily through inspection.

His management philosophy emphasised leadership, improvement of systems, cooperation and removal of barriers that prevented people from doing good work.

There is an important continuity here.

Taylor sought systematic management.

Drucker recognised the rise of knowledge and self-direction.

Deming emphasised systems and leadership over inspection.

Lean thinking emphasised flow and waste.

Agile approaches attempted to shorten feedback loops and increase adaptation.

Modern product thinking increasingly focuses on outcomes rather than output.

Each movement loosened some part of the industrial management architecture.

Yet the organisation still commonly falls back on a familiar mechanism:

break the work into smaller pieces and manage the pieces.

Why?

Because task control remains simple to understand.

It is visible.

It feels concrete.

And when uncertainty increases, managers often seek more control rather than less.

That brings us to micromanagement.

# Chapter 4

## Why Micromanagement Persists

Micromanagement is commonly treated as a character flaw.

The insecure boss.

The founder who cannot let go.

The executive who wants everything done their way.

The supervisor who checks every detail.

Sometimes this diagnosis is correct.

But it is incomplete.

Micromanagement is frequently a **system response**.

Consider a manager who insists on approving every customer communication.

Why?

Perhaps the organisation has poorly defined customer commitments.

Perhaps legal boundaries are unclear.

Perhaps previous mistakes created significant harm.

Perhaps staff do not know what authority they possess.

Perhaps no reliable evidence is preserved.

Perhaps tone varies widely between teams.

Perhaps escalation criteria exist only as organisational folklore.

The manager therefore fills multiple gaps.

They are the policy interpreter.

The protocol.

The knowledge base.

The quality system.

The escalation mechanism.

The risk control.

The evidence checker.

The memory.

Remove their approval tomorrow and you may not get healthy autonomy.

You may simply expose the missing organisational infrastructure.

This produces one of the central principles of conducting:

> **You cannot sustainably remove micromanagement without replacing the organisational functions that micromanagement was performing.**

This is why simplistic advice such as "leaders just need to trust their teams" often fails.

Trust matters.

But trust cannot substitute for organisational design.

Imagine telling an airline pilot:

"We trust you, so we've removed the flight procedures."

That would be absurd.

High-agency systems do not necessarily have less structure.

They often have **better structure**.

The important distinction is between structure that enables local action and structure that continuously interrupts local action.

A protocol enables.

An unnecessary approval interrupts.

Shared Direction enables.

A stream of changing instructions interrupts.

Clear authority enables.

Ambiguous responsibility interrupts.

Automatic evidence enables.

Repeated status reporting interrupts.

Good process enables.

Bureaucracy interrupts.

Conducting is therefore not a move from structure to freedom.

It is a move from **intervention-dependent structure to agency-enabling structure**.

# Chapter 5

## Management Debt

Software teams sometimes speak of technical debt.

A quick implementation solves today's problem but creates hidden future cost.

The system still works.

But every change becomes more difficult.

Organisations accumulate something similar.

Call it **management debt**.

Management debt arises whenever recurring organisational ambiguity is solved through repeated human intervention rather than improvement of the underlying conditions.

A founder personally approves every proposal.

It works.

Then there are ten proposals a week.

Then fifty.

A manager manually resolves every scheduling conflict.

It works.

Then the team doubles.

A senior engineer is asked every security question.

It works.

Then every project depends on them.

A project manager manually consolidates status from fifteen systems.

It works.

Then the organisation launches another twenty initiatives.

Each intervention solves today's problem.

But the organisation learns nothing.

The system remains dependent on the person.

### The interest payments

Management debt carries interest.

Its interest is paid in human attention.

Every recurring approval is an interest payment.

Every meeting required because systems cannot share state is an interest payment.

Every status report manually created from information already stored somewhere else is an interest payment.

Every question answered for the twentieth time is an interest payment.

Every decision that has to be escalated because authority was never clarified is an interest payment.

Every senior person whose calendar is filled by organisational mediation is an interest payment.

The organisation can carry management debt for a surprisingly long time.

Growth often hides it.

More managers are hired.

More coordinators appear.

New layers are added.

Another SaaS platform is purchased.

Another reporting function is established.

The structure becomes more capable of servicing the debt.

But servicing debt is not the same as removing it.

Conducting asks a different question:

> What would need to become true so this intervention is no longer routinely necessary?

That question moves us from activity to architecture.

# Chapter 6

## The New Constraint Is Coherence

For much of organisational history, intelligence was scarce.

A relatively small number of people possessed specialised knowledge.

Information gathering was expensive.

Analysis took time.

Communication across distance was difficult.

Institutional knowledge was often inseparable from the individuals who possessed it.

Management concentrated scarce cognitive capability.

The digital age weakened these constraints.

Search made information easier to find.

Cloud systems made data broadly accessible.

Collaboration platforms reduced communication cost.

Software automated repetitive work.

AI now dramatically lowers the cost of producing analysis, explanation, code, plans, summaries and other cognitive outputs.

The constraint begins to shift.

When intelligence becomes abundant, the challenge is no longer only creating enough intelligent action.

It is ensuring intelligent actions remain compatible.

Imagine one hundred highly capable people acting independently.

That may be powerful.

Imagine one million autonomous software processes acting independently.

That may be chaos.

Local intelligence does not guarantee global coherence.

An AI agent may rationally optimise customer conversion while harming regulatory compliance.

A sales team may rationally maximise revenue while creating unsustainable delivery promises.

An operations team may rationally reduce costs while degrading customer experience.

A risk team may rationally reduce exposure while making useful activity impossible.

Every actor can be locally correct while the system becomes globally incoherent.

This is the new organisational constraint.

Not simply intelligence.

**Coherence.**

The organisation of abundant intelligence therefore needs mechanisms that allow agency while protecting the whole.

That is the work of conducting.

# Chapter 7

## Moving Up an Abstraction Layer

The conductor metaphor is useful because it changes where leadership acts.

A conductor does not play every instrument.

They do not position every finger.

They do not make every breath.

They do not strike every drum.

The orchestra contains distributed capability.

Each musician can act locally.

Yet they do not produce independent music.

They share composition.

Timing.

Interpretation.

Signals.

Protocols.

Awareness of one another.

And a conductor pays attention to the relationships across the system.

An organisation can operate similarly.

The traditional manager may spend much of the day inside execution:

Review this document.

Approve that expense.

Rewrite this email.

Check this task.

Attend this meeting.

Update this spreadsheet.

Confirm this decision.

Tell that team what to do.

The conductor asks questions at another level:

Why does this need approval?

Why is this information unavailable?

Which protocol is missing?

What conflict in Direction produced these incompatible decisions?

Why are teams repeatedly asking the same question?

Where is attention being consumed?

What evidence should be automatically generated?

What condition would allow these people to proceed independently?

What has changed?

What are we learning?

Which Areas of Focus require attention now?

The framework expresses this change directly:

| Task-Based Management | Conducting |
|---|---|
| Assign tasks | Maintain Direction |
| Communicate instructions | Maintain Engagement |
| Measure activity | Observe Enablement |
| Tell people exactly how to work | Establish Protocols |
| Maximise utilisation | Maintain Sustainability |
| Administer workflows | Improve Processes |
| Supervise individuals | Establish Accountability |
| Coordinate every action | Synchronise the organisation |
| Push work downward | Surface areas requiring attention |
| Control execution | Enable coherent agency |



Notice what is happening.

The conductor is not doing less.

The conductor is operating on conditions that have wider leverage.

One good protocol may eliminate thousands of approvals.

One clarification of Direction may prevent months of wasted work.

One improvement to Process may remove hundreds of manual hand-offs.

One accountability mechanism may eliminate an entire layer of supervisory reporting.

The unit of leadership changes.

From intervention.

To environment.

# Chapter 8

## Coherent Agency

There are two organisational qualities that are often treated as opposites:

**agency** and **coherence**.

Agency means the ability to perceive, decide and act.

Coherence means those actions continue to make sense as part of a larger whole.

Too little agency produces bureaucracy.

Everyone waits.

Everything escalates.

Initiative declines.

People learn that acting without permission is dangerous.

Eventually employees stop solving problems and begin reporting them.

Too little coherence produces fragmentation.

Teams optimise incompatible goals.

Standards diverge.

Promises conflict.

Risks accumulate invisibly.

Customers experience different organisations depending on whom they encounter.

Autonomous systems magnify inconsistencies.

The traditional solution is to protect coherence by constraining agency.

Centralise decisions.

Increase approval.

Standardise everything.

Require reporting.

Limit discretion.

This can work.

But it creates management dependency.

Conducting seeks a harder and more valuable outcome:

> **high agency and high coherence simultaneously.**

That requires strong shared conditions.

```text
                  HIGH COHERENCE

                     │
     Bureaucracy     │     Conducting
                     │
      Low agency     │     High agency
                     │
─────────────────────┼─────────────────────
                     │
    Disconnection    │     Fragmentation
                     │
                     │
                  LOW COHERENCE
```

The upper-right quadrant is the target.

People can act.

Systems can act.

Teams can adapt.

Yet actions remain connected to purpose, boundaries and evidence.

This is coherent agency.

The canonical summary of the framework describes the goal in exactly these terms: **coherent agency without continuous central instruction.**

This idea becomes the organising principle for everything that follows.

# Chapter 9

## The Eight Areas of Focus

How do we maintain coherent agency?

Not by managing every movement.

By maintaining the conditions that make useful independent movement possible.

The conducting model uses eight persistent Areas of Focus:

### Direction
Overall direction, path, intent and design setting, including reflection.

### Engagement
Explainability of values, protocols and processes — the soul and the story.

### Enablement
The outputs and products of the organisation.

### Protocols
Standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.

### Sustainability
Long-term viability, including capital for sustained human attention and conductors.

### Processes
Operational processes that drive enablement and organisational functioning.

### Accountability
Internal and external accountability, including sovereign-state regulatory obligations.

### Organisational
Coordination and synchronisation across Areas of Focus, including team alignment.

These are the canonical definitions of the framework.

They should not be converted into eight departments.

Marketing is not Engagement.

Product is not Enablement.

Technology is not Protocols.

Finance is not Sustainability.

Compliance is not Accountability.

Operations is not Processes.

Each Area cuts across the entire organisation.

A marketing team has Direction.

A finance system has Protocols.

A product team has Accountability.

A technology program has Sustainability.

A board has Engagement.

An AI agent operates through Processes.

The Areas describe dimensions of organisational coherence.

They are also non-hierarchical.

Direction is not "above" Accountability.

Processes are not "below" Protocols.

Their interaction matters more than their position.

```text
             Direction
           ↗           ↘
 Engagement               Protocols
      ↕                       ↕
 Enablement ←──────────→ Processes
      ↕                       ↕
 Sustainability ←────→ Accountability
           ↘           ↙
           Organisational
```

When one Area weakens, others feel the effects.

Unclear Direction produces irrelevant Enablement.

Weak Engagement makes useful Protocols feel arbitrary.

Poor Protocols create manual management dependencies.

Unsustainable Processes consume human attention.

Weak Accountability damages trust.

Poor Organisational synchronisation creates conflict between otherwise competent teams.

Conducting is therefore systemic.

The conductor listens for relationships.

# Chapter 10

## Direction

Direction is not a strategic plan.

It is not an annual target.

It is not a mission statement attached to a website.

Direction is the living answer to:

> **Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?**

The inclusion of reflection matters.

Without reflection, Direction becomes doctrine.

The organisation chooses a destination and continues moving toward it even after reality changes.

A conducting organisation treats Direction as persistent but adaptive.

Purpose may remain stable.

Path may change.

Assumptions may fail.

Opportunities may emerge.

New evidence may require redesign.

Direction therefore contains at least five layers:

**Purpose** — why do we exist?

**Intent** — what change are we trying to enable?

**Orientation** — what do we currently believe about our environment?

**Priorities** — where should attention and resources currently concentrate?

**Reflection** — what have we learned that might change the above?

Traditional organisations frequently jump from purpose directly into tasks.

Conducting keeps the intermediate context visible.

### Direction as a boundary

Good Direction does not tell everyone what to do.

It helps people decide what **not** to do.

Suppose a team encounters an unexpected opportunity.

There is no task for it.

There is no approval workflow.

There is no project.

Can they recognise whether the opportunity aligns with Direction?

If yes, the organisation possesses a form of distributed strategic capability.

If no, strategy remains trapped at the top.

Direction becomes especially important when AI systems participate.

An agent capable of taking action needs more than a prompt saying "maximise sales".

It requires context.

What trade-offs are acceptable?

Which commitments are inviolable?

What customer outcomes matter?

What boundaries cannot be crossed?

Where should uncertainty trigger escalation?

What evidence must be preserved?

A narrow optimisation objective is not Direction.

Direction is the contextual field within which optimisation has meaning.

### Reflection questions

- What are we trying to make possible?
- What has changed since our current Direction was established?
- Which assumptions are no longer secure?
- Which activities appear successful locally but no longer support our Direction?
- Can people explain the Direction without repeating slogans?
- Can an autonomous system determine when an action conflicts with it?

# Chapter 11

## Engagement

Organisations often misunderstand Engagement as communication.

Send the email.

Publish the strategy.

Run the town hall.

Create the presentation.

Update the intranet.

But information transmission is not the same as shared understanding.

The conducting framework defines Engagement as the explainability of values, protocols and processes — **the soul and the story**.

That phrase matters.

Every organisation contains formal structures and lived meaning.

The formal value may be "customer first".

The lived meaning may be "never issue a refund without approval."

The formal value may be "innovation".

The lived meaning may be "do not fail publicly."

The formal structure may say employees are empowered.

The lived process may require three managers to approve a $100 decision.

People learn the real organisation from repeated experience.

Engagement is the work of making the organisation intelligible.

Why does this protocol exist?

Why does this process matter?

Why is this boundary here?

What story connects this activity to the whole?

What values should guide judgement when no rule exists?

People participate more intelligently when they understand not only **what** the organisation does but **why the system has the shape it does**.

### Explainability becomes infrastructure

This becomes increasingly important in complex organisations.

A rule that cannot be explained becomes brittle.

A process nobody understands becomes ritual.

A policy without narrative becomes bureaucracy.

A protocol without meaning becomes an obstacle people work around.

Conducting therefore treats explainability as operational infrastructure.

If participants understand the reason behind a mechanism, they are better able to recognise when its application no longer makes sense.

That enables reflection.

It also enables trust.

### Machines need engagement context too

As AI enters organisational processes, Engagement acquires a new dimension.

Machines do not absorb organisational culture through years of informal participation in the same way humans do.

If an organisation expects AI to assist with meaningful decisions, the organisation must make more of its context explicit.

Values.

Definitions.

Policies.

Decision principles.

Tone.

Escalation rules.

Examples.

Constraints.

Organisations that have operated primarily through tacit knowledge may discover that AI exposes how little of the organisation has ever been made explainable.

That is not an AI problem.

It is an Engagement problem that AI makes visible.

# Chapter 12

## Enablement

What does the organisation actually make possible?

That is the Enablement question.

Every organisation contains activity.

Few lack work.

The danger is mistaking the existence of work for the existence of value.

Consider two teams.

Team A completes 1,000 tasks.

Team B completes 200.

Which is more effective?

There is no answer.

We know almost nothing.

What did the tasks achieve?

Who benefited?

What became possible?

What useful output entered the world?

Enablement moves the organisation away from activity as the primary unit of success.

The canonical definition is deliberately simple:

> **The outputs and products of the organisation.**

That simplicity provides discipline.

A university may conduct thousands of administrative activities.

Its Enablement might include learning, qualifications, research and community capability.

A hospital may operate huge internal systems.

Its Enablement concerns health outcomes and services.

A software organisation may write millions of lines of code.

Its Enablement lies in what users can now accomplish.

A regulator may process forms.

Its Enablement may be trusted participation within a safe market.

### Output versus activity

A conducting organisation keeps asking:

What useful thing exists because we did this?

This question can be uncomfortable.

Some long-standing activities may have no convincing answer.

The weekly report exists because the weekly report has always existed.

The steering meeting exists because the project methodology requires it.

The spreadsheet exists because someone once requested it.

The approval exists because something once went wrong.

Activity acquires organisational inertia.

Enablement breaks the spell.

If the activity disappeared, what useful capability would disappear with it?

If the answer is "none", the organisation has found an opportunity.

### AI and the explosion of output

AI makes this distinction even more important.

Generative systems can produce enormous quantities of output.

Reports.

Plans.

Code.

Slides.

Analysis.

Emails.

Images.

Documentation.

If organisations continue measuring visible output, AI may create the illusion of extraordinary productivity while useful outcomes barely change.

The future organisation will need to distinguish **cheaply generated activity** from **meaningful enablement**.

The faster output becomes, the more important this distinction becomes.

# Chapter 13

## Protocols

Protocols may be the least glamorous and most transformative Area of Focus.

A protocol is a reusable agreement that allows interaction without renegotiating the interaction every time.

The internet works because protocols exist.

A web browser does not call a committee meeting with a server before every request.

Systems interact because shared rules make interaction predictable.

Organisations can operate similarly.

Protocols can establish:

identity;

authority;

permissions;

data structures;

decision rights;

templates;

interfaces;

credentials;

escalation rules;

technical standards;

trust mechanisms;

interoperability.

The framework asks:

> **What shared rules or interfaces allow participants to coordinate without continuous manual intervention?**

This question directly attacks management dependency.

### The repeated question test

Listen for repeated organisational questions.

"Who approves this?"

"Which template should I use?"

"Can this customer receive a refund?"

"Who owns this data?"

"Can this system access that information?"

"What happens if the request exceeds the limit?"

"Who is allowed to sign?"

"Which version is authoritative?"

"Does this require legal review?"

If these questions recur, the organisation may be using humans as protocols.

The person answering becomes an interface.

The organisation scales by adding more interface people.

Conducting attempts to encode repeatable coordination.

Not because every decision should be automated.

But because human judgement should be preserved for situations that genuinely require judgement.

### Protocols create freedom

This can seem paradoxical.

Rules are commonly associated with constraint.

But good protocols increase freedom.

Road rules constrain drivers.

Those constraints make it possible for millions of strangers to move independently through shared infrastructure.

Internet protocols constrain software interactions.

Those constraints enable extraordinary permissionless innovation above them.

A good organisational protocol works similarly.

It defines enough shared structure that local actors can move confidently.

Protocol is therefore not the opposite of autonomy.

It is often a prerequisite for scalable autonomy.

# Chapter 14

## Sustainability

Every organisation operates within limits.

Money is one.

Time is another.

Energy is another.

Attention is another.

The conducting framework explicitly treats **human attention** as a finite resource.

This deserves far more organisational attention than it receives.

Imagine a company where every customer exception must be approved by one executive.

The process may be financially affordable.

The executive already receives a salary.

No new software is required.

The organisation may therefore describe the process as cheap.

But it is consuming something exceptionally scarce:

senior attention.

Every minute spent approving a routine exception is unavailable for reflection, relationships, Direction, crisis response, learning or design.

Human attention is not free simply because it does not appear as a new invoice.

### Conductors can become bottlenecks

Conducting itself can fail if the conductor becomes central to everything.

A leader begins asking excellent systemic questions.

Soon everyone waits for the leader's interpretation.

The organisation has replaced a task bottleneck with a context bottleneck.

The Sustainability question therefore applies to conducting too:

Can this organisation maintain coherence without exhausting its conductors?

If not, context needs to become more distributed.

### Sustainable intelligence

The arrival of AI creates another tempting illusion.

Machine attention can appear unlimited.

Why not review every action?

Analyse every employee interaction?

Score every decision?

Generate reports continuously?

Technically, more may become possible.

But machine-generated oversight can create human interpretation burden.

A system that produces 50,000 alerts does not create accountability.

It creates noise.

A system that generates hundreds of pages of analysis can reduce rather than improve understanding.

Sustainability therefore concerns cognitive load across the whole human-machine system.

The goal is not maximum intelligence utilisation.

It is useful intelligence applied where it produces meaningful leverage.

# Chapter 15

## Processes

Processes turn intent into repeatable activity.

They answer:

> **How does useful activity reliably happen?**

A healthy process reduces the cost of coordination.

An unhealthy process becomes coordination.

Consider expense approval.

A weak process might involve:

emailing a manager;

forwarding a receipt;

waiting;

answering questions;

receiving approval;

sending information to finance;

manually entering it elsewhere;

waiting again.

The participants describe this as "the expense process".

But much of the process is simply compensation for missing integration, unclear rules and fragmented evidence.

A stronger process might encode standard limits, automatically preserve evidence and escalate only exceptions.

The useful work remains.

The coordination overhead disappears.

### Process versus bureaucracy

Bureaucracy is often blamed on process.

That is unfair to process.

Good processes remove uncertainty from routine situations.

Bureaucracy frequently arises when processes repeatedly require human mediation without producing proportional value.

The test is simple:

Does the process reduce future coordination, or create more of it?

### Process as a learning loop

Processes should not be static.

Exceptions contain information.

If an exception happens once, it may genuinely be exceptional.

If it happens every week, the Process probably does not match reality.

Conductors pay attention to repeated exceptions because they reveal opportunities for redesign.

A useful operating loop is:

```text
Act
 ↓
Observe
 ↓
Identify friction
 ↓
Reflect
 ↓
Improve Process
 ↓
Act
 ↺
```

This moves the organisation away from blaming people for repeatedly navigating a poorly designed system.

It turns friction into organisational learning.

# Chapter 16

## Accountability

Accountability is often confused with observation.

If managers can see everything, the organisation feels accountable.

But visibility and accountability are different.

Suppose a manager sits beside an employee and watches every action.

Later a dispute arises.

Why was a decision made?

Which information was used?

What authority applied?

Which policy version was current?

What was promised?

The manager remembers some of it.

The employee remembers something different.

There is little evidence.

The organisation possessed surveillance.

It did not possess strong accountability.

Now imagine another environment.

The employee acts independently.

Relevant authority is explicit.

Important inputs have provenance.

The applicable policy is identifiable.

Material decisions create records.

Exceptions are preserved.

Customer consent is captured.

System activity is attributable.

No manager watches the action live.

Months later, the event can be reconstructed accurately.

That is accountability.

The framework asks:

> **Can we establish what happened, who or what acted, under what authority, and whether obligations were met?**

### Evidence by construction

The strongest accountability is created as work happens.

Do not reconstruct compliance after the event if the process can produce the relevant evidence naturally.

Do not ask employees to remember which information they used if the system can preserve provenance.

Do not create separate audit reality if operational reality itself can be verifiable.

This becomes critical in agentic organisations.

When software can perform thousands of consequential actions, humans cannot retrospectively interview the agents.

Authority, evidence and provenance must become part of the architecture.

Accountability moves from supervision into infrastructure.

# Chapter 17

## Organisational

An organisation can perform well in seven Areas and still fail because they are not synchronised.

Direction may be clear.

Engagement strong.

Enablement useful.

Protocols mature.

Processes efficient.

Accountability excellent.

Sustainability healthy.

But if different parts of the organisation are operating on incompatible timing, assumptions or dependencies, the whole still suffers.

That is why the eighth Area is **Organisational**.

It is the explicit coordination and synchronisation layer.

The key question is:

> **How do the Areas of Focus remain coordinated as one system?**

This is the primary conducting layer.

### The spaces between things

Traditional management focuses heavily on objects.

The project.

The department.

The role.

The budget.

The task.

The employee.

Conducting often pays more attention to relationships.

What sits between Product and Risk?

Between Sales and Operations?

Between customer promise and delivery capacity?

Between Direction and Process?

Between AI autonomy and human Accountability?

Between local optimisation and system Sustainability?

Organisational problems frequently live in these spaces.

No single team owns them.

That is precisely why they persist.

The conductor sees the whole.

Not in the sense of knowing every detail.

But in the sense of watching whether the parts continue to form a coherent system.

# Part III

# Releasing Agency

# Chapter 18

## From Delegation to Agency

Delegation was an important improvement over centralised control.

A manager recognises that they cannot do everything.

Responsibility is transferred downward.

The employee receives authority to perform the work.

But delegation still begins at the centre.

The manager decides what needs doing.

The manager packages the responsibility.

The manager allocates it.

The employee acts.

Agency is different.

Agency exists when an actor can perceive context, recognise what needs attention, choose an appropriate response and act within legitimate boundaries.

Delegation says:

> I have decided this action should occur, and I authorise you to perform it.

Agency says:

> We share sufficient context for you to recognise appropriate action yourself.

This is a significant organisational leap.

### The permission reflex

Many organisations accidentally train agency out of people.

An employee encounters something unexpected.

They act.

A manager later says:

"Why didn't you check with me?"

The lesson is stored.

Next time the employee asks.

The manager becomes frustrated.

"Why does everyone need me to make every decision?"

Because the organisation taught them that unsanctioned initiative is dangerous.

Agency is not created by posters saying "take ownership".

It is created through repeated organisational experience.

Are people rewarded for thoughtful judgement?

Are boundaries clear?

Are good-faith mistakes treated as learning?

Are decision rights understandable?

Is Direction available?

Can people access the evidence they need?

Can they determine when escalation is required?

If these conditions exist, agency emerges.

### Agency is not abandonment

A manager saying "figure it out yourself" has not necessarily created agency.

They may simply have withdrawn support.

Agency requires context.

The stronger the required autonomy, the stronger the surrounding conditions generally need to become.

This is why conducting is not laissez-faire management.

It is deliberate organisational design for responsible independence.

# Chapter 19

## Accountability Without Surveillance

The digital workplace makes surveillance easy.

Keyboard activity.

Application usage.

Location.

Email metadata.

Call durations.

Ticket counts.

Response times.

Screen captures.

AI can potentially analyse all of it.

Organisations may therefore face a dangerous temptation.

If management previously lacked visibility, abundant data appears to solve the problem.

Observe everything.

Measure everything.

Score everything.

Predict everything.

But surveillance and accountability solve different problems.

Surveillance asks:

> Can I see what you are doing?

Accountability asks:

> Can relevant actions and obligations be established and verified?

These can overlap, but they are not equivalent.

### Surveillance can weaken agency

When people know every action is being judged, behaviour changes.

Risk avoidance increases.

People optimise visible indicators.

Experimentation declines.

Context gets lost.

The employee learns to appear productive rather than create value.

AI-enabled surveillance could make this far worse because measurement becomes inexpensive.

The organisation may drown itself in behavioural telemetry.

### Verifiability changes the model

Conducting seeks evidence that is proportional to consequence.

Routine low-risk activity may require little evidence.

High-consequence action may require strong provenance, authority and decision records.

The question becomes:

What evidence is necessary for the organisation and affected parties to establish that obligations were met?

This is more precise than "give managers maximum visibility".

It also respects agency.

People and systems can operate independently while remaining accountable for material consequences.

# Chapter 20

## Protocols Instead of Permission

Every time someone asks permission, listen carefully.

Permission requests are signals.

Sometimes approval is genuinely required.

But repeated approvals often reveal an unencoded organisational decision.

"Can I refund $120?"

"Can I send this proposal?"

"Can I use this supplier?"

"Can this employee access the system?"

"Can this agent execute this transaction?"

If the answer repeatedly depends on the same conditions, the organisation has discovered the shape of a protocol.

Perhaps refunds under a threshold can be authorised locally.

Perhaps proposals within standard contractual boundaries do not need legal review.

Perhaps supplier eligibility can be credentialled.

Perhaps system permissions can derive from verified roles.

Perhaps agents can act inside explicit transaction and risk boundaries.

### From manager to rule designer

The manager's role changes.

Instead of repeatedly making the decision, they help design the environment in which the decision can safely be made without them.

That is an important form of leverage.

The old measure of importance was:

How many decisions come through me?

The new measure may be:

How many routine decisions no longer need to?

### Permission should become exceptional

In a mature conducting organisation, escalation remains available.

The objective is not eliminating central judgement.

The objective is reserving it for situations deserving central judgement.

Unusual risk.

Novelty.

Material consequences.

Conflicting values.

Strategic change.

Irreversible action.

The organisation protects its most scarce human attention by not wasting it on questions already answered in principle.

# Chapter 21

## The Manager as Conductor

What becomes of the manager?

This question creates understandable anxiety.

If teams possess greater agency and AI performs more coordination, is management disappearing?

Some forms of management probably will diminish.

But leadership does not become less important.

It becomes more abstract.

The manager as conductor performs at least seven important roles.

### 1. Sensing

What is changing?

Where is friction appearing?

What signals are being ignored?

What does the formal reporting system not reveal?

### 2. Context building

Do people understand why they are acting?

Is relevant information available?

Are assumptions explicit?

### 3. Protocol design

Where can reusable rules replace repeated intervention?

### 4. Boundary stewardship

Where should action remain constrained?

Which decisions require human legitimacy?

What risks are unacceptable?

### 5. Synchronisation

Which teams or systems are moving out of alignment?

Where are dependencies creating delay?

### 6. Reflection

What did we learn?

Which assumptions failed?

What should change?

### 7. Attention allocation

Which Areas require leadership attention now?

The role becomes less like traffic controller and more like system steward.

### A different experience of importance

This transition can be psychologically difficult.

Managers are often rewarded for being needed.

The expert everyone calls.

The executive whose approval matters.

The founder who knows every customer.

The project manager through whom every decision flows.

Conducting asks leaders to create systems that need less of their routine intervention.

Success can therefore feel like loss.

The organisation stops calling.

Decisions happen without you.

Meetings disappear.

Questions are answered by protocols.

Evidence is automatically available.

Teams solve problems locally.

That should not be experienced as declining importance.

It is leadership leverage.

A conductor's value lies not in being necessary to every movement.

It lies in helping the whole move coherently.

# Chapter 22

## Attention as Organisational Capital

Capital allocation is considered a serious leadership responsibility.

Attention allocation should be treated with similar seriousness.

Senior human attention is finite.

It cannot be replenished by increasing server capacity.

An executive has approximately the same number of hours each day whether the organisation contains fifty people or fifty thousand.

Growth therefore creates an attention problem.

Traditional organisations solve it through hierarchy.

Each manager absorbs complexity from below.

More scale requires more layers.

Conducting provides another mechanism:

reduce the amount of intervention required per unit of agency.

### The attention budget

Imagine treating leadership attention as an explicit budget.

Where is it currently spent?

Routine approvals?

Status meetings?

Correcting work?

Resolving ambiguity?

Chasing evidence?

Recreating decisions?

Handling preventable exceptions?

Or:

Reflection?

Relationships?

Direction?

System design?

Learning?

Novel problems?

Material judgement?

The first category represents organisational friction.

The second represents leadership leverage.

A mature conducting organisation continually shifts attention from the first toward the second.

### AI changes the attention equation

AI can both help and hurt.

It can summarise signals, identify exceptions and reduce manual interpretation.

But it can also generate an almost infinite quantity of things demanding attention.

Reports.

Notifications.

Risks.

Recommendations.

Possible actions.

A good conducting architecture therefore filters.

The aim is not maximum visibility.

It is **appropriate attention**.

The conductor should see what matters, when it matters, at the level at which intervention can create leverage.

# Chapter 23

## Trust as an Architectural Property

Organisations talk constantly about trust.

"Trust your team."

"Build a culture of trust."

"Assume positive intent."

These are valuable principles.

But trust also has an architectural dimension.

Consider two organisations.

In the first, decision rights are unclear.

Records are incomplete.

People possess different versions of information.

Important rules exist only in people's memories.

Authority is informal.

When problems occur, nobody can establish what happened.

Management responds by demanding personal trust.

In the second, Direction is clear.

Authority is understandable.

Information has provenance.

Actions create evidence.

Protocols define normal interaction.

Processes surface exceptions.

People know when to escalate.

Trust has support.

This second environment does not eliminate interpersonal trust.

It makes trust less fragile.

### Trust and verification are allies

There is a false choice between trust and verification.

Healthy verification can increase trust because participants do not need to rely on constant personal oversight.

A customer can trust a process because commitments are verifiable.

A regulator can trust an organisation because evidence is generated consistently.

A team can trust another team because interfaces and responsibilities are explicit.

A leader can trust local action because boundaries are clear.

In this sense, accountability infrastructure can release agency.

People do not need to be watched constantly when consequential actions are reliably attributable and reviewable.

Trust stops depending entirely on proximity.

It becomes portable.

That matters enormously in distributed organisations and ecosystems involving AI.

# Chapter 24

## When Intelligence Stops Being Scarce

Management developed partly as a response to scarce intelligence.

Not intelligence in the abstract.

Useful organisational intelligence.

Who knows what is happening?

Who understands the customer?

Who can analyse the numbers?

Who understands the regulation?

Who knows how the system works?

Who can draft the plan?

Who can make the decision?

Historically, the answers often pointed toward particular people.

Those people became organisational nodes.

AI changes the economics.

The cost of producing a competent first draft of analysis, code, explanation or documentation has fallen dramatically.

The 2026 Stanford AI Index describes continuing gains across reasoning and agentic systems and notes rapid organisational adoption of AI, while also showing that deployment of agents in business functions remains relatively early.

This distinction matters.

We should avoid pretending the fully autonomous organisation already exists.

It does not.

But organisational design cannot wait until a transition is complete before considering its consequences.

### Abundant intelligence does not mean abundant wisdom

AI makes many cognitive outputs cheaper.

It does not make purpose automatic.

It does not make values automatic.

It does not make legitimacy automatic.

It does not remove trade-offs.

It does not eliminate uncertainty.

And it certainly does not guarantee coherence.

Every team can generate a strategy.

Every employee can build software.

Every department can create automation.

Every agent can propose action.

The cost of creating an initiative approaches zero.

The organisational problem becomes deciding which initiatives should exist and how they relate.

The scarce resource moves upward.

From production of intelligence.

Toward **direction, attention, legitimacy and coherence**.

# Chapter 25

## The Agentic Organisation

The first wave of organisational AI largely behaved like a tool.

A human initiated the interaction.

The AI responded.

The human evaluated the response.

The human acted.

```text

 ↓
AI
 ↓
Human judgement
 ↓

```

Agentic systems alter this pattern.

An agent may:

observe an event;

retrieve information;

reason about context;

select tools;

perform actions;

```text
   ↓

   ↓
Decide
   ↓
Act
   ↓

   ↺
```

Some biological.

Some digital.

Some persistent.

Some temporary.

Some internal.

Some external.

### What does an agent need?

A high-agency system requires more than capability.

It needs Direction.

It needs Organisational coordination.

The more independently something can act, the more important the surrounding organisational context becomes.

## AI Should Not Become the Ultimate Micromanager

conductor-assistant.

**Organisational**  
Which dependencies require synchronisation?

Now the organisation is solving the system.

Not merely the queue.

## A Practical Transformation Pattern

Consider a company where every external customer report must be reviewed by a manager.

The organisation wants to remove the bottleneck.

A simplistic solution is:

"Employees may now send reports without approval."

That removes control without replacing its function.

Conducting takes a different path.

### Direction

What is the reporting capability actually for?

To improve shared understanding of customer reality and support useful customer decisions.

### Engagement

What should participants understand?

Reports exist to create clarity, not merely satisfy a reporting ritual.

Accuracy, usefulness and respect for customer context matter.

### Enablement

What should become possible?

Customers and internal teams can make better decisions from timely, reliable insight.

### Protocols

What shared rules are necessary?

Approved evidence sources.

Standard definitions.

Privacy boundaries.

Materiality thresholds.

Reporting schemas.

Escalation criteria.

### Sustainability

How much recurring attention should the capability require?

Routine reporting should not consume significant senior review.

### Processes

How does reporting happen?

Signals are collected continuously.

Material changes are detected.

Draft insight is produced.

Normal reports flow directly.

Exceptions escalate.

### Accountability

How do we establish reliability?

Claims link to evidence.

Data lineage is retained.

Material judgement is attributable.

### Organisational

How does reporting remain connected?

Relevant customer, operational and product signals are synchronised.

Now remove the approval.

The approval is no longer required because the organisational conditions that justified it have been redesigned.

This is the general pattern.

Do not simply remove control.

**Replace control with context, protocol and evidence.**

# Chapter 35

## Measuring What Matters

What does a successful transition look like?

Not fewer managers by itself.

An understaffed organisation can have few managers and terrible agency.

Not more automation by itself.

Automation can accelerate bureaucracy.

Not employee freedom by itself.

Freedom without coherence can fragment the organisation.

We need signals of **coherent agency**.

### 1. Decision latency

How long do normal decisions wait for permission?

A decline suggests stronger local agency.

### 2. Approval density

How many actions require human approval?

Are approvals concentrated on genuinely consequential situations?

### 3. Clarification demand

How often do people ask questions that the organisation has effectively answered before?

Repeated clarification often signals weak context or Protocols.

### 4. Management attention cost

How much senior attention is spent on routine coordination?

### 5. Evidence completeness

Can material events be reconstructed without extraordinary effort?

### 6. Exception quality

Are escalations genuinely unusual and consequential?

Or is the "exception queue" simply the normal process wearing another name?

### 7. Directional coherence

Do locally initiated activities remain aligned with Direction?

### 8. Enablement

Is useful capability improving?

### 9. Sustainability

Can the organisation grow without equivalent growth in coordination overhead?

### 10. Learning velocity

How quickly does recurring friction lead to system improvement?

These measures are not another scorecard to optimise mechanically.

They are reflection signals.

The framework explicitly recommends signals such as fewer unnecessary approvals, reduced waiting, less repeated clarification, improved quality, better traceability, reduced attention cost, stronger alignment and faster autonomous action within boundaries.

# Chapter 36

## The Conducting Maturity Model

Organisations will not move from micromanagement to mature conducting overnight.

A useful way to think about progression is through five states.

## Stage 1 — Directed

Work depends heavily on explicit instruction.

Managers allocate tasks.

Approvals are common.

Context is concentrated.

Evidence is reconstructed.

Individuals rely on management interpretation.

This can work in small or highly repetitive environments.

It scales poorly.

## Stage 2 — Delegated

Managers distribute greater responsibility.

Teams receive clearer ownership.

Decision rights expand.

But the manager remains the source of much context.

Agency exists mainly where authority has explicitly been delegated.

## Stage 3 — Contextual

Direction and principles become more accessible.

Protocols begin replacing recurring permission.

Processes are simplified.

Teams can recognise appropriate action without waiting for assignment.

Evidence improves.

This is where genuine agency begins.

## Stage 4 — Conducted

The Eight Areas are actively maintained.

Human attention moves toward exceptions, reflection and synchronisation.

Routine action proceeds without central instruction.

Accountability is increasingly generated by operation itself.

Managers function as conductors.

## Stage 5 — Agentic

Humans and autonomous systems both exercise bounded agency.

Direction, Protocols and Accountability are sufficiently explicit to support machine-speed activity.

AI assists sensing, interpretation, coordination and verification.

Human attention concentrates on purpose, legitimacy, relationships, novel judgement and systemic reflection.

The organisation can operate at multiple speeds while remaining coherent.

### Maturity is contextual

Higher is not automatically better in every situation.

A crisis may temporarily require centralised Direction.

A highly hazardous activity may appropriately retain strict approval.

A new team may need greater structure while context develops.

Conducting is not ideological decentralisation.

It is the deliberate matching of agency, context, consequence and coherence.

# Chapter 40

## The Organisational Shift of Our Time

Every age creates organisational forms suited to its constraints.

Industrialisation created the need to coordinate large-scale physical production.

Management systems emerged that separated planning from execution, standardised activity and created hierarchy capable of processing information at scale.

The knowledge economy weakened that separation.

Workers increasingly possessed expertise management could not completely specify.

Organisations became more collaborative.

Information became more distributed.

Technology accelerated communication.

Yet many underlying management structures remained.

Then intelligence itself began becoming inexpensive.

Artificial intelligence can already generate outputs that once required significant specialist effort.

Agentic systems increasingly move beyond generation toward action.

The question facing organisations is therefore larger than:

How should we adopt AI?

The deeper question is:

> **What kind of organisation makes sense when intelligence is no longer the scarce resource it once was?**

The answer cannot simply be:

more tasks generated faster;

more employees monitored more precisely;

more decisions routed through automated managers;

more optimisation;

more output.

That would use the technology of a new age to perfect the architecture of the previous one.

The opportunity is more significant.

We can create organisations in which context is widely available.

Direction is understandable.

Protocols replace recurring permission.

Processes enable rather than obstruct.

Accountability is generated through action.

Attention is treated as scarce capital.

Humans and machines operate within clear boundaries.

Reflection is continuous.

Coordination focuses on relationships rather than control.

And useful action can emerge locally without losing connection to the whole.

That is conducting.

### From scarcity to abundance

The old organisation can be represented simply:

```text
SCARCE INTELLIGENCE ORGANISATION

Intent
  ↓
Managers
  ↓
Tasks
  ↓
Human Execution
  ↓
Reporting
  ↺
```

The emerging organisation looks different:

```text
ABUNDANT INTELLIGENCE ORGANISATION

               Direction
                  ↓
            Shared Context
                  ↓
      ┌──────── Agency ────────┐
      │                        │
   Humans                  AI / Systems
      │                        │
      └──────── Action ────────┘
                  ↓
         Evidence + Outcomes
                  ↓
              Reflection
                  ↓
          Conducting Focus
                  ↺
```

This is the reference mental model of the conducting framework.

### From instruction to conditions

The transition can be summarised through a series of shifts.

From tasks to context.

From instructions to Direction.

From communication to Engagement.

From activity to Enablement.

From permission to Protocols.

From utilisation to Sustainability.

From bureaucracy to Process.

From surveillance to Accountability.

From coordination overhead to Organisational synchronisation.

From delegation to agency.

From management intervention to conducting.

None of this removes the human being.

It makes human attention more valuable.

The organisation stops spending people on coordination that machines or protocols can perform and allows people to concentrate on what remains deeply human:

purpose;

relationships;

care;

curiosity;

judgement;

creativity;

meaning;

legitimacy;

reflection.

### The essential question

The industrial manager asked:

> What should everyone be doing?

The conductor asks:

> **What needs our attention now?**

That may sound like a small linguistic change.

It is not.

The first assumes leadership should determine movement.

The second assumes capable actors already exist and leadership must maintain the conditions through which their movement remains coherent.

That is the organisational shift.

Not the end of management.

The maturation of it.

Not freedom from accountability.

Accountability strong enough to support freedom.

Not decentralisation for its own sake.

Agency with coherence.

Not AI replacing the organisation.

Intelligence becoming part of an organisation designed to conduct intelligence well.

The organisations that understand this will not merely perform today's work faster.

They will possess a different kind of capability.

They will be able to sense.

Reflect.

Orient.

Focus attention.

Enable action.

Observe.

Synchronise.

Learn.

And continue moving without waiting for continuous central instruction.

That is the organisation after micromanagement.

That is conducting.

# Appendix A

## The Eight Areas of Focus

### Direction

**Definition:** Overall direction, path, intent and design setting, including the reflection process.

**Key question:**

> Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?

---

### Engagement

**Definition:** Explainability of values, protocols and processes — the soul and the story.

**Key question:**

> Can people understand the organisation's values, way of operating and story well enough to participate meaningfully?

---

### Enablement

**Definition:** The outputs and products of the organisation.

**Key question:**

> What useful things is the organisation actually enabling or producing?

---

### Protocols

**Definition:** Standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.

**Key question:**

> What shared rules or interfaces allow participants to coordinate without continuous manual intervention?

---

### Sustainability

**Definition:** Long-term viability including capital for sustained human attention and conductors.

**Key question:**

> Can this continue without consuming the people, capital, attention or resources that make it possible?

---

### Processes

**Definition:** Operational processes that drive enablement and the functioning of the organisation.

**Key question:**

> How does useful activity reliably happen?

---

### Accountability

**Definition:** Internal and external accountability, including sovereign-state regulatory obligations.

**Key question:**

> Can we establish what happened, who or what acted, under what authority, and whether obligations were met?

---

### Organisational

**Definition:** Coordination and synchronisation across Areas of Focus, including team alignment.

**Key question:**

> How do the Areas of Focus remain coordinated as one system?

These definitions follow the framework's canonical Eight Areas of Focus.
