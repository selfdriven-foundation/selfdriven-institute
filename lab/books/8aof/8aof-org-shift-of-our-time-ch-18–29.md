# Part III — Releasing Agency

# Chapter 18

## From Delegation to Agency

Delegation is one of the foundational practices of modern management.

A manager cannot do everything.

Responsibility must move outward.

Authority is assigned.

The employee receives ownership.

This is better than centralising every decision.

But delegation and agency are not the same thing.

Delegation begins with the manager.

The manager identifies the work.

The manager determines that someone else should perform it.

The manager defines the scope.

The manager transfers authority.

The employee executes.

Agency begins somewhere else.

It begins with an actor capable of observing the environment, understanding relevant context, recognising what requires action and responding within legitimate boundaries.

The difference can be expressed simply.

Delegation says:

> **I have decided what should happen, and I authorise you to do it.**

Agency says:

> **We share enough context for you to recognise what should happen and act appropriately.**

That difference may appear subtle.

Organisationally, it is enormous.

### The delegation ceiling

Delegation scales only as far as management can anticipate the work.

Someone still has to recognise the action before it can be delegated.

This works well in predictable environments.

The manager knows which activities need doing.

Responsibility can be divided.

But in dynamic environments, much of the useful work cannot be completely identified in advance.

A customer encounters an unexpected situation.

A competitor changes direction.

A new technology appears.

An operational weakness becomes visible.

A community need changes.

A security event occurs.

The person closest to the event may be the first actor capable of recognising what should happen.

If the organisation requires that actor to wait until management converts reality into an authorised task, valuable time is lost.

Agency shortens the distance between perception and appropriate action.

### Organisations teach people to wait

Many leaders say they want ownership.

Their organisations teach the opposite.

An employee encounters an unusual problem.

They make a reasonable decision.

Later their manager asks:

> Why didn't you check with me?

The employee learns.

Next time they ask.

Then they ask again.

Eventually the manager becomes frustrated.

> Why does everyone bring every decision to me?

Because the organisation has taught them that permission is safer than judgement.

This is how agency disappears.

Not necessarily through policy.

Through experience.

If independent action is punished whenever it produces an imperfect result, rational employees stop taking independent action.

If approval protects them personally, escalation becomes the safest strategy.

The organisation can talk about empowerment indefinitely.

Its lived incentives teach dependency.

### Agency requires conditions

The alternative is not:

> Do whatever you think is best.

That is not agency.

It is abandonment.

The framework is explicit on this point: autonomy should not be treated as absence of structure. Strong shared Direction, Protocols, Processes, Accountability and coordination allow local agency to exist inside coherent conditions.

A capable actor needs enough context to answer questions such as:

What are we trying to make possible?

What boundaries apply?

What authority do I possess?

Which obligations matter?

What evidence should I preserve?

What level of consequence requires escalation?

Who else could be affected?

Without these conditions, autonomy becomes guesswork.

### From delegated tasks to bounded agency

A conducting organisation therefore moves beyond delegating more tasks.

It improves the environment in which actors operate.

Direction becomes accessible.

Protocols establish normal decision rights.

Processes make information available.

Accountability makes consequential action verifiable.

Organisational coordination exposes relevant dependencies.

Participants can then respond to situations leadership did not explicitly anticipate.

This is the deeper organisational advantage.

The organisation stops depending on central prediction.

It begins depending on distributed interpretation.

### Agency changes the role of leadership

Under delegation, the leader remains the origin of action.

Under agency, leadership increasingly shapes the field within which useful action can originate elsewhere.

This requires a different confidence.

The leader will not know every decision before it occurs.

They will not personally initiate every valuable activity.

Success therefore becomes less visible as direct managerial authorship.

A team identifies an opportunity.

Acts.

Records the evidence.

Produces a useful outcome.

The manager may not have touched the action.

That is not absence of leadership.

It may be the result of good leadership.

### AI implication

The distinction between delegation and agency becomes unavoidable with autonomous systems.

A conventional software tool performs a delegated activity.

A person initiates it.

The system executes a defined operation.

An agentic system may instead:

observe;

interpret;

select an action;

use tools;

observe the outcome;

continue.

It possesses a limited form of operational agency.

That means the organisational question can no longer be only:

**What tasks can we automate?**

It becomes:

> **Under what conditions may this system recognise and initiate action itself?**

That is a conducting question.

### Conducting Question

> **Where are we calling people empowered while still requiring management to originate most meaningful action?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**What more can I delegate?**

ask:

**What context would allow useful action to originate safely without me?**

# Case Study

## The School That Stopped Managing Teachers by Task

Consider a secondary school called Riverbank College.

Its leadership genuinely wants teaching quality to improve.

Over several years, the school introduces:

lesson-plan templates;

curriculum progress reports;

professional-development logs;

assessment calendars;

classroom observation forms;

resource approval;

meeting requirements.

Each addition has a reasonable origin.

Leadership needs evidence.

Teachers need consistency.

Students deserve quality.

Regulatory obligations matter.

But gradually, another system emerges.

Teachers spend increasing amounts of time demonstrating that teaching is occurring.

Experienced teachers feel constrained by standardisation.

Less experienced teachers remain uncertain because completing templates does not automatically develop professional judgement.

Leadership notices inconsistency.

The instinct is to add more control.

### The task-centric interpretation

The problem appears to be variation.

Some teachers follow required Processes more consistently than others.

Management concludes that execution needs tighter supervision.

But teaching is deeply contextual.

A teacher continuously interprets:

student understanding;

individual needs;

classroom dynamics;

curriculum;

relationships;

time;

assessment evidence.

The person in the room possesses information leadership cannot fully anticipate.

Managing teaching primarily through task compliance therefore suppresses precisely the local judgement the school needs.

### The conducting diagnosis

**Direction** becomes clearer: what learning, development and student agency is Riverbank actually trying to make possible?

**Engagement** connects teachers with the educational principles beneath the school's Processes.

**Enablement** shifts attention from completed teaching documentation towards learning capability.

**Protocols** preserve strong curriculum, safeguarding, assessment and professional boundaries.

**Sustainability** recognises that teacher attention spent proving activity is attention unavailable for students.

**Processes** simplify recurring administration.

**Accountability** remains strong through appropriate evidence of learning and professional responsibility rather than constant observation.

**Organisational** synchronises teachers around students, curriculum and shared dependencies without requiring every classroom to operate identically.

### The change

Riverbank does not tell teachers:

> Do whatever you want.

It strengthens the conditions around professional agency.

Several low-value recurring reports disappear.

Common expectations become clearer.

Teachers receive greater freedom in how learning experiences are designed.

Evidence becomes more connected to learning.

Professional reflection becomes more important than procedural compliance.

Leadership conversations change.

From:

> Did you complete the required template?

Towards:

> What are students experiencing?

> What evidence tells us whether learning is happening?

> Where are teachers encountering common friction?

> Which organisational conditions need attention?

### Case Lesson

> **Professional agency does not require the absence of standards. It requires standards strong enough that professionals do not need continuous instruction.**

# Chapter 19

## Accountability Without Surveillance

Agency produces an understandable organisational concern.

What if something goes wrong?

What if someone acts outside their authority?

What if a commitment is missed?

What if a regulator asks what happened?

What if an autonomous system behaves unexpectedly?

Traditional management has often answered these questions through visibility.

Managers watch.

Managers review.

Managers approve.

Managers require status.

Digital systems make far more intensive observation possible.

Emails.

Application usage.

Screen activity.

Call metrics.

Location.

Response times.

Ticket counts.

AI could potentially interpret all of it.

The temptation is obvious:

> If we can observe more, perhaps we can control risk more effectively.

But observation and Accountability are different.

### The difference

Surveillance asks:

> **Can I see what you are doing?**

Accountability asks:

> **Can relevant actions, authority and obligations be established?**

An organisation can have enormous visibility and poor Accountability.

Imagine a manager sitting beside an employee while an important decision is made.

The manager sees the activity.

Six months later a dispute arises.

Which information informed the decision?

Which authority applied?

Which policy version was current?

What exactly was communicated?

Why was an exception made?

Nobody preserved the evidence.

The organisation possessed supervision.

It did not possess strong Accountability.

Now imagine another organisation.

No manager watches the action live.

But:

the actor's authority is clear;

important inputs have provenance;

applicable rules are identifiable;

material decisions create evidence;

exceptions are recorded.

Six months later the event can be reconstructed.

That organisation possesses stronger Accountability.

### Surveillance is expensive

Surveillance also consumes attention.

Someone has to interpret what is observed.

More telemetry can create:

more dashboards;

more exceptions;

more alerts;

more performance reviews;

more disputes about metrics.

The organisation becomes highly visible and increasingly difficult to understand.

People also adapt their behaviour to what is measured.

They optimise visible indicators.

They avoid risk.

They appear busy.

The organisation can accidentally replace accountability for outcomes with performance for observation.

### Strong Accountability can release agency

This is the important inversion.

Accountability is not merely a constraint on autonomy.

Strong Accountability can make autonomy safer.

A leader can allow greater local decision-making when:

authority is explicit;

material actions are attributable;

relevant evidence exists;

obligations are understandable;

exceptions remain visible.

The framework explicitly warns against treating Accountability as surveillance and instead emphasises verifiable actions, authority, evidence and obligations. 

The stronger those conditions become, the less management needs to witness every action.

### Evidence proportional to consequence

Conducting does not require exhaustive recording of everything.

That would become another form of organisational burden.

Accountability should be proportional.

A trivial internal choice may require almost no durable evidence.

A significant financial transaction may require substantial provenance.

A medical decision may require another level.

An autonomous system acting on behalf of thousands of customers may require another.

The conducting question is:

> **What evidence is necessary to establish legitimate action given the consequence involved?**

That is more precise than:

**How much can we monitor?**

### AI implication

AI dramatically increases the importance of this distinction.

If an autonomous system performs thousands of actions, human supervision cannot scale linearly.

Either autonomy remains extremely limited or Accountability becomes architectural.

Systems need to establish enough about consequential actions that those actions remain governable.

Which agent acted?

Under what authority?

Using which relevant information?

Which policy or Protocol applied?

Which external obligation mattered?

What was the outcome?

Where should escalation have occurred?

At machine speed, retrospective human reconstruction becomes impossible.

Evidence increasingly has to be generated at the moment of action.

### Conducting Question

> **Where are we using observation because the organisation does not yet produce trustworthy evidence?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we see more of what people are doing?**

ask:

**What needs to be verifiable?**

# Chapter 20

## Protocols Instead of Permission

Every permission request is a signal.

Not necessarily a problem.

Some decisions genuinely deserve approval.

But when the same class of question repeatedly travels to the same authority, the organisation should pay attention.

Can I refund this amount?

Can I approve this expense?

Can we use this supplier?

Can this customer receive an exception?

Can I access this information?

Can this agent execute this transaction?

If the answer depends repeatedly on the same conditions, a Protocol may be waiting to be created.

### Permission does not scale

Permission is an interpersonal coordination mechanism.

One actor asks.

Another interprets.

The second actor replies.

This can work beautifully at small scale.

But every permission interaction consumes attention and time.

As volume grows, the approver becomes infrastructure.

The organisation begins organising itself around their availability.

This is why approval bottlenecks often appear during growth.

The organisation has scaled activity without scaling its coordination architecture.

### The Protocol move

Instead of answering the hundredth similar request, ask:

What conditions determine the answer?

Perhaps:

transactions below a threshold can proceed automatically;

standard contracts require no additional legal review;

verified suppliers meet predetermined criteria;

particular credentials establish access;

exceptions above defined risk levels escalate;

agents can operate within specified monetary or operational limits.

The decision has moved from personal interpretation towards a reusable interaction rule.

### This is not rigid bureaucracy

The objective is not encoding every possible situation.

That would simply create a different kind of fragility.

A mature Protocol distinguishes:

normal cases;

boundary cases;

exceptions.

Normal cases should require little coordination.

Boundary cases may require additional evidence.

Exceptions should surface for judgement.

Human attention moves towards situations in which interpretation actually adds value.

### Permission is still useful

Conducting does not seek to eliminate approval.

Approval remains appropriate where:

consequences are substantial;

the decision is irreversible;

values conflict;

novel conditions exist;

authority requires legitimacy;

the organisation deliberately wants a human checkpoint.

But permission should increasingly mark **significance**, not routine.

If everything requires approval, the organisation has not identified what deserves approval.

### Protocols distribute confidence

One reason employees escalate is not lack of capability.

It is uncertainty about whether the organisation will support their decision.

Protocols create shared confidence.

The actor knows:

this is inside my authority;

these are the relevant conditions;

this is what evidence I should retain;

this is when I escalate.

The manager knows the same thing.

Agency becomes less personally risky.

### AI implication

Autonomous systems make Protocols unavoidable.

An agent cannot safely operate from:

> Use your judgement, but ask Sarah if it looks unusual.

Its operational boundaries need greater clarity.

What information can it access?

Which credentials matter?

What transactions can it execute?

What limits apply?

Which systems can it modify?

When must it stop?

Which evidence must it produce?

Protocols become part of the organisational control plane for machine agency.

The underlying framework similarly treats standards, permissions, identity, credentials, technical agreements, decision rights and machine-readable rules as Protocol concerns.

### Conducting Question

> **Which repeated approvals represent organisational decisions that have effectively already been made many times?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Who should approve this?**

ask:

**What distinguishes the cases that genuinely require approval from those that do not?**

# Chapter 21

## The Manager as Conductor

If people gain agency, Protocols replace routine permission and systems become more capable, what happens to managers?

This is one of the most important questions in the transition.

The simplistic answer is:

> We need fewer managers.

That may sometimes be true.

But it misses the organisational opportunity.

The more important shift is that management work changes.

The manager stops being primarily a router of tasks and decisions.

They become increasingly responsible for the conditions that allow others to act coherently.

That is conducting.

### The traffic-controller manager

Many managers spend their days routing organisational movement.

Who should do this?

What is the priority?

Can this proceed?

Who needs to attend?

What does leadership want?

Where is the project?

Why is this delayed?

Who should decide?

This role can be exhausting.

It also makes the manager indispensable.

Activity continually arrives and departs through the manager.

The organisation interprets busyness as leadership.

But much of the busyness may be evidence that the system cannot coordinate without them.

### The conductor's work

The conductor shifts attention.

They sense.

What is changing?

Which signals matter?

Where is friction increasing?

They maintain context.

Is Direction understood?

Which assumption has changed?

What information is missing?

They improve Protocols.

Where are people repeatedly asking permission?

Which interactions can become predictable?

They protect boundaries.

Which actions require greater legitimacy or care?

They synchronise.

Which Areas or teams are moving out of alignment?

They reflect.

What did the organisation learn?

They allocate attention.

Where can leadership intervention currently create the most leverage?

The framework's conducting mindset explicitly moves managers from assigning tasks, supervising individuals and coordinating every action toward maintaining Direction, Protocols, Accountability and organisational synchronisation.

### Success becomes less visible

This creates a psychological challenge.

Managers are often rewarded for being needed.

The person with every answer appears important.

The manager whose calendar is full appears committed.

The executive whose approval everyone seeks appears powerful.

Conducting can make successful leadership less visible.

The team solves a problem without you.

The Process works.

The Protocol answers the routine question.

Evidence is available automatically.

A meeting disappears.

Nobody asks for permission.

From one perspective, less management happened.

From another, the organisation became more capable.

The conductor has to become comfortable with success that occurs without direct intervention.

### Leadership leverage

The highest-leverage manager is not necessarily the one making the greatest number of decisions.

It may be the leader who improves one organisational condition and thereby improves thousands of decisions they will never personally see.

Clarify a boundary.

Improve a Protocol.

Fix a recurring Process.

Make evidence trustworthy.

Surface Direction.

Remove a dependency.

These interventions scale.

### The leader as system steward

This does not remove human relationships from management.

Quite the opposite.

As routine coordination becomes structural, managers have more capacity for deeply human work.

Coaching.

Care.

Conflict.

Meaning.

Development.

Judgement.

Reflection.

Relationship.

The shift away from micromanagement does not make leadership mechanical.

It can make leadership more human.

### AI implication

AI can assist the manager-as-conductor.

It can surface unusual organisational signals.

Find repeated exceptions.

Identify work drifting from Direction.

Detect gaps in evidence.

Show increasing coordination load.

Summarise organisational state.

The framework explicitly positions AI as sensor, interpreter, coordinator, verifier, reflector and conductor-assistant rather than automatically making AI "the boss".

The manager then spends less time collecting state and more time exercising judgement about state.

### Conducting Question

> **If I stopped performing my most frequent management interventions, which organisational conditions would fail?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do I become better at handling all these decisions?**

ask:

**Why do all these decisions need to reach me?**

# Chapter 22

## Attention as Organisational Capital

Organisations carefully allocate money.

They are less disciplined about attention.

Yet attention may be the scarcer resource.

A company can raise capital.

Hire more people.

Add computing capacity.

Buy software.

A senior leader still has roughly the same number of waking hours.

A clinician still has finite cognitive energy.

A teacher can only pay deep attention to so many students at once.

A team can only interpret so many competing priorities.

Attention does not scale automatically with activity.

### The hidden cost

Consider a three-minute approval.

It appears trivial.

But if it occurs one hundred times each week, five hours of senior attention disappear.

Add:

status meetings;

manual review;

clarification;

escalation;

information hunting;

Process exceptions.

The manager's week fills.

The organisation may record none of this as a direct incremental cost.

The person was already employed.

Yet something valuable has been consumed.

This is why the framework explicitly places human attention inside Sustainability and treats it as finite.

### Attention has opportunity cost

Every hour spent on routine coordination is an hour unavailable for:

Direction;

reflection;

relationships;

learning;

system design;

novel judgement;

creative work;

care.

This is not an argument that leaders should spend all day "thinking strategically".

Operations matter.

Reality matters.

But organisations should know whether scarce attention is being spent intentionally or merely absorbed by friction.

### The attention budget

Imagine leadership attention as a capital allocation decision.

Where are we investing it?

What return do we receive?

A one-hour conversation resolving a fundamental strategic ambiguity may save thousands of hours elsewhere.

A one-hour approval meeting for decisions already defined by policy may create little value.

Both consume the same clock time.

They do not create the same organisational leverage.

### Conducting allocates attention

This leads to one of the core principles of the whole model:

> **The Areas of Focus persist. Attention moves.**

Direction may be stable today.

Accountability may require urgent attention tomorrow.

Growth may expose Sustainability next month.

A new technology may reveal Protocol problems.

Conducting is partly the disciplined movement of scarce attention towards the conditions currently threatening coherence.

The leader is no longer trying to control everything.

They are deciding where human attention matters most.

### AI changes the attention equation

AI appears to increase attention.

Machines can monitor continuously.

Read everything.

Analyse everything.

Generate summaries.

Detect anomalies.

But AI can also make the attention problem worse.

If analysis becomes free, the organisation can generate infinite analysis.

If alerts become cheap, it can generate infinite alerts.

If recommendations become cheap, it can generate infinite recommendations.

Human interpretation remains finite.

A system that generates one thousand technically relevant alerts has not necessarily increased organisational intelligence.

It may simply have shifted the bottleneck to human attention.

### Useful AI compresses significance

A conductor-assistant should therefore do more than generate information.

It should help identify significance.

What changed materially?

Which exception deserves escalation?

Where is risk concentrating?

Which repeated issue now indicates a systemic pattern?

Which part of the organisation is consuming more attention than before?

Good AI does not merely see more.

It helps humans know where to look.

### Conducting Question

> **Where is scarce human attention being consumed by repeated coordination rather than judgement that genuinely requires a human?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How do we make people more productive?**

ask:

**Where should we stop spending their attention?**

# Chapter 23

## Trust as an Architectural Property

Organisations talk constantly about trust.

Trust the team.

Trust leadership.

Trust the Process.

Trust customers.

Trust partners.

Trust technology.

Much of this is interpersonal and cultural.

But trust also has architecture.

Consider two organisations.

In the first:

authority is ambiguous;

records are incomplete;

people hold different versions of information;

important decisions depend on memory;

Protocols are informal;

when something goes wrong, nobody can clearly establish what happened.

Leadership responds:

> We need to trust each other.

In the second:

Direction is accessible;

authority is explicit;

relevant information has provenance;

material actions leave evidence;

Protocols define normal interaction;

exceptions are visible.

Trust has structure beneath it.

### Trust does not require blindness

A common mistake is framing trust and verification as opposites.

Either we trust people or we check them.

But good verification can reduce the need for intrusive checking.

A customer can trust a Process because commitments are recorded.

A regulator can trust an organisation because evidence is reliable.

One team can trust another because interfaces are explicit.

A leader can trust local agency because authority and Accountability are clear.

Verification makes trust more portable.

It allows trust to survive distance.

### Personal trust is fragile at scale

Small organisations can rely heavily on relationships.

Everyone knows everyone.

Reputation travels quickly.

Context is shared informally.

This can create powerful trust.

Growth changes the situation.

New employees arrive.

Partners join.

Customers scale.

Teams become distributed.

AI agents appear.

Participants increasingly interact without deep personal knowledge of one another.

The organisation cannot require a long personal history before every useful interaction.

Some trust must move from:

**I know you**

towards:

**I can establish enough about this interaction to proceed safely.**

Identity.

Authority.

Credentials.

Evidence.

Provenance.

Protocol.

These become trust infrastructure.

### Trust and agency reinforce one another

Where trust is weak, organisations add permission.

Where permission grows, agency falls.

Where agency falls, people stop exercising judgement.

Where judgement declines, leadership trusts people less.

A negative loop forms.

Strong Accountability and Protocols can reverse that loop.

The organisation can allow greater agency because consequential actions remain bounded and verifiable.

Successful local action then creates confidence.

Trust grows.

This is not blind faith in participants.

It is confidence in the relationship between participants and the organisational system.

### AI implication

Trust becomes especially important when the actor is not human.

"Trust the AI" is not a serious organisational control.

Nor is "never trust AI."

The useful questions are architectural.

What authority has the system been granted?

Which data sources can it use?

Which actions can it perform?

How can its activity be attributed?

Which decisions require human legitimacy?

How does it demonstrate compliance with relevant Protocols?

What happens when confidence is low?

Trust should emerge from conditions, not anthropomorphism.

### Conducting Question

> **Where does our organisation rely on personal trust because authority, evidence or Protocols are too weak to support trustworthy interaction structurally?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Do I trust this person or system?**

ask:

**What makes this interaction trustworthy?**

# Part IV — Abundant Intelligence

# Chapter 24

## When Intelligence Stops Being Scarce

For most of organisational history, useful intelligence was expensive.

Someone had to know.

Someone had to analyse.

Someone had to write.

Someone had to calculate.

Someone had to remember.

Someone had to interpret.

These capabilities took time to develop and were unevenly distributed.

Organisations therefore concentrated expertise around particular people and functions.

AI changes the economics.

The cost of producing competent cognitive output can fall dramatically.

Analysis.

Software.

Documentation.

Research summaries.

Options.

Plans.

Explanations.

Classification.

Interpretation.

The significance is not that AI becomes perfectly intelligent.

It is that useful cognitive capability becomes easier to distribute.

### The wrong question

Organisations naturally ask:

How much productivity can AI create?

How many tasks can it automate?

How much faster can people work?

These questions matter.

But they are downstream questions.

The larger organisational question is:

> **What changes when intelligence itself becomes less scarce?**

The answer is not merely more output.

The scarce resource moves.

### Scarcity moves upward

If analysis becomes inexpensive, choosing what deserves analysis matters more.

If software becomes inexpensive, deciding which software should exist matters more.

If strategies can be generated instantly, Direction becomes more important.

If thousands of autonomous actions become possible, Accountability becomes more important.

If every team can automate independently, Protocols become more important.

If machine activity becomes continuous, human attention becomes more scarce.

Abundant intelligence does not remove organisational constraints.

It relocates them.

Towards:

Direction;

meaning;

attention;

legitimacy;

trust;

coherence.

### Output can become noise

This is why the intelligence-age organisation cannot measure productivity primarily by volume of cognitive output.

AI can generate documents nobody needs.

Code that creates future maintenance.

Analysis that never influences a decision.

Recommendations that compete for attention.

The organisation may become extraordinarily productive at producing things.

Enablement may barely improve.

The better intelligence becomes, the more disciplined the organisation needs to become about purpose.

### More intelligence can increase fragmentation

Every team now has greater capability.

That can improve local performance.

It can also produce divergence.

One team creates its own automation stack.

Another defines different terminology.

Another deploys agents against different objectives.

Another optimises a competing metric.

The intelligence is real.

So is the incoherence.

This is why the thesis of this book is not:

> AI makes management unnecessary.

It is:

> **Abundant intelligence makes conducting more important.**

### Conducting Question

> **Which organisational scarcity becomes more important as intelligence and output become cheaper?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How much more can AI produce?**

ask:

**What becomes more important when production of intelligence is no longer the primary constraint?**

# Chapter 25

## The Agentic Organisation

The first wave of workplace AI mostly behaved like a tool.

A human initiated the request.

AI produced an answer.

The human evaluated it.

The human acted.

```text id="jacq7k"
Human
   ↓
AI assistance
   ↓
Human judgement
   ↓
Human action
```

Agentic systems change the loop.

An agent may:

observe;

retrieve information;

interpret;

choose an action;

invoke a tool;

observe the outcome;

continue.

```text id="yovkpf"
Observe
   ↓
Interpret
   ↓
Act
   ↓
Observe
   ↺
```

The difference is organisationally significant.

The system is no longer merely producing information for an actor.

It is participating in action.

### A new participant

Organisations have long contained multiple types of actors.

Employees.

Contractors.

Partners.

Suppliers.

Customers.

Software has historically been infrastructure used by those actors.

Agentic systems blur that distinction.

A system may possess:

an identity;

permissions;

access to information;

tools;

persistent objectives;

authority to initiate bounded actions.

This makes it useful to think of the agent as an organisational participant.

Not a person.

Not morally equivalent to a person.

But operationally, an actor whose behaviour must fit into the organisational system.

### Agency creates organisational requirements

The more an actor can act independently, the stronger the surrounding conditions need to become.

The framework identifies a high-agency system as requiring Direction, Engagement context, Protocols, Processes, Accountability, Sustainability boundaries and Organisational coordination before broad autonomous actuation.

This is not accidental.

Human agency requires many of the same conditions.

The implementation differs.

The organisational problem is similar.

What are we trying to make possible?

What authority exists?

Which rules apply?

What evidence matters?

When must escalation occur?

What dependencies exist?

### From software architecture to organisational architecture

AI agents are often discussed as a technical architecture.

Models.

Tools.

Memory.

Planning.

Orchestration.

Guardrails.

These matter.

But a technically capable agent can still be organisationally incoherent.

It might optimise the wrong objective.

Act under ambiguous authority.

Use technically available information it should not legitimately use.

Create commitments other teams cannot fulfil.

Take locally rational actions that conflict with broader Direction.

The agent therefore needs to sit inside both a software architecture and an organisational architecture.

This is one of the most important shifts of agentic AI.

### Conducting Question

> **Which autonomous actions would become organisational decisions, not merely technical operations, if a machine began initiating them?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**What can this agent do?**

ask:

**What organisational role is this agent actually being allowed to perform?**

# Chapter 26

## AI Should Not Become the Ultimate Micromanager

There is an obvious application of AI to management.

Observe everything.

Analyse everything.

Allocate work.

Measure performance.

Detect deviation.

Review communications.

Score outcomes.

Recommend interventions.

Generate personalised instructions.

In technical terms, this may become astonishingly capable.

Every employee could effectively have a management system continuously watching them.

The industrial management architecture would not disappear.

It would become automated.

### The perfect stopwatch

Scientific management once depended on human observation.

Digital systems make observation persistent.

AI makes interpretation scalable.

The result could be a workplace in which:

every movement is measurable;

every communication can be classified;

every task can be scored;

every deviation can be detected;

every worker can receive constant algorithmic direction.

One might describe this as efficient.

It is still micromanagement.

Faster micromanagement is still micromanagement.

### Automating dependency

If the problem is that every action depends on central supervision, replacing the supervisor with AI does not eliminate dependency.

It makes dependency cheaper.

The organisation remains based on:

externally assigned tasks;

continuous observation;

central optimisation;

performance against predetermined activity.

People remain execution endpoints.

Only the manager has become infinitely scalable.

The source framework explicitly identifies this as an anti-pattern: AI should not be used to micromanage every employee more efficiently; it should reduce the need for micromanagement by improving sensing, context, Protocols, evidence and coordination.

### AI as conducting infrastructure

A more valuable role is almost the opposite.

Use AI to reduce the conditions that create micromanagement.

AI can identify repeated permission requests.

Surface conflicting interpretations.

Detect missing evidence.

Find Process exceptions.

Show increasing attention demand.

Connect relevant context.

Explain Protocols.

Observe drift from Direction.

The intelligence supports the conditions.

It does not replace human agency with automated supervision.

### The legitimacy boundary

This distinction is not merely about productivity.

It is about power.

A system capable of monitoring, scoring and directing individuals possesses significant organisational power.

Who defines its objectives?

What happens when its interpretation is wrong?

Can people contest its decisions?

Does it measure what actually matters or what is technically visible?

Does it optimise organisational outcomes at the expense of human dignity?

Conducting requires leadership to recognise that not every technically possible form of management should be adopted.

### Conducting Question

> **Are we using AI to reduce the organisational need for supervision — or simply to make supervision cheaper and more pervasive?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How can AI manage this activity?**

ask:

**Why does this activity require so much management in the first place?**

# Chapter 27

## Human Direction and Machine-Speed Action

Autonomous systems create a speed mismatch.

Machines can operate in milliseconds.

Human institutions deliberate slowly.

This creates two unsatisfactory extremes.

At one extreme, humans approve every significant machine action.

Autonomy collapses.

The machine waits for the human.

At the other extreme, machines act freely against objectives too narrow to represent legitimate organisational purpose.

Control collapses.

The solution is not choosing one speed.

It is designing an organisation that can operate at multiple speeds.

### Human-speed questions

Some decisions deserve deliberation.

What are we trying to make possible?

Which values matter?

What commitments are legitimate?

Which harms are unacceptable?

What rights should participants retain?

What level of risk can the organisation legitimately accept?

These are not merely optimisation questions.

They are questions of Direction and legitimacy.

They belong substantially in the human domain.

### Machine-speed questions

Other activities can occur far faster.

Monitoring.

Information retrieval.

Classification.

Routine coordination.

Standard transactions.

Verification.

Bounded execution.

The organisation gains value when these can proceed without unnecessary human delay.

### The boundary between speeds

The critical design problem is escalation.

When should machine-speed activity stop?

Examples might include:

unusual consequence;

novel situations;

irreversible action;

conflicting obligations;

material uncertainty;

values conflict;

low confidence.

A mature agent does not merely know how to act.

It operates inside conditions defining when autonomous action is no longer appropriate.

### Pre-authorised agency

This resembles other high-agency systems.

An actor receives broad authority inside a defined field.

Normal action proceeds without repeated permission.

Crossing a meaningful boundary changes the mode.

Human attention enters.

This is different from approving every action.

The human does not operate the machine manually.

They establish and steward the conditions under which autonomous action remains legitimate.

### The conducting loop

A useful model is:

```text id="3gw9se"
Human Direction
      ↓
Shared Context + Protocols
      ↓
Bounded Machine Action
      ↓
Evidence + Outcomes
      ↓
Reflection
      ↓
Direction
      ↺
```

This resembles the framework's broader Sense → Inject Intelligence → Actuate → Record Evidence → Reflect → Adapt loop.

The important feature is reflection.

Automation without reflection simply accelerates the current assumptions.

Conducting allows assumptions themselves to evolve.

### Conducting Question

> **Which decisions genuinely require human legitimacy, and which activities are merely waiting for humans because our boundaries are poorly designed?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Where should humans stay in the loop?**

ask:

**Which kinds of consequence require human judgement, and how should the system recognise them?**

# Case Study

## The Organisation With 200 People and 5,000 Agents

Consider a services company called NorthGrid several years into widespread agent adoption.

The company employs two hundred people.

Its digital environment contains approximately five thousand persistent and temporary agents.

Some monitor customer environments.

Some reconcile information.

Some prepare proposals.

Some review obligations.

Some generate software.

Some coordinate schedules.

Some monitor infrastructure.

The organisation initially treats agents like very fast junior employees.

Tasks are assigned.

Outputs are reviewed.

People supervise the results.

AI use expands rapidly.

So does a new form of work.

**AI babysitting.**

Employees spend increasingly large parts of their day reviewing, correcting and authorising machine activity.

The organisation has deployed automation.

It has also created an enormous new management layer.

### The coherence failure

A large customer begins increasing its usage.

A commercial agent identifies an expansion opportunity.

It prepares an upsell.

A risk agent detects signs of financial uncertainty.

It recommends tightening commercial exposure.

A retention agent sees recent customer dissatisfaction and recommends concessions.

A pricing agent sees an opportunity to increase margin.

Every recommendation is individually rational.

Together they are incoherent.

The problem is not insufficient intelligence.

The problem is that intelligence is acting against locally defined objectives without enough shared organisational context.

### The conducting diagnosis

**Direction** must describe the wider customer relationship rather than four isolated optimisation targets.

**Engagement** must make important values and interpretations sufficiently explicit for humans and machines.

**Enablement** asks what useful customer outcome the organisation is actually trying to create.

**Protocols** define agent authority, interaction rules and escalation boundaries.

**Sustainability** recognises that human review cannot grow in proportion to agent activity.

**Processes** allow routine agent coordination to occur automatically.

**Accountability** makes identity, authority and consequential agent actions attributable.

**Organisational** detects conflicts between locally intelligent activity.

### The architectural shift

NorthGrid stops assigning humans as supervisors to every agent family.

Instead it establishes stronger conducting layers.

Normal actions occur inside explicit boundaries.

Conflicts between important objectives surface.

Material exceptions reach humans.

Routine state does not.

The number of agent actions rises dramatically.

The number of human approvals does not rise proportionally.

Human attention moves towards:

Direction;

novel judgement;

policy design;

significant relationships;

exception handling;

reflection.

### Case Lesson

> **An agentic organisation cannot scale if every machine action creates a new human management task.**

# Chapter 28

## Evidence at Machine Speed

Traditional Accountability is frequently retrospective.

An event occurs.

Later someone asks what happened.

The organisation searches.

Emails.

Documents.

Logs.

Spreadsheets.

Messages.

Memories.

People reconstruct the story.

This already struggles at human speed.

It fails completely at machine speed.

An ecosystem of autonomous systems may create thousands or millions of consequential micro-actions.

No audit team can reconstruct all of them manually.

Evidence has to move closer to action.

### Evidence by construction

A useful conceptual model is:

```text id="f823xz"
Action
   +
Authority
   +
Relevant Inputs
   +
Applicable Context
   +
Outcome
   =
Evidence Event
```

This is not a literal requirement that every interaction store every possible detail.

It is an architectural principle.

For consequential actions, the system should preserve enough information to answer relevant accountability questions later.

What happened?

Who or what acted?

Under what authority?

Which material conditions applied?

Which obligation mattered?

What resulted?

### Why retrospective compliance becomes impossible

Consider a human employee performing ten material decisions each week.

An organisation may, inefficiently, reconstruct those decisions later.

Now imagine one autonomous agent performing ten thousand actions daily.

The economics change entirely.

Compliance, assurance and Accountability must increasingly become properties of the operational system itself.

The work and the evidence of the work move closer together.

### Evidence does not mean surveillance

This distinction remains important.

Generating evidence about consequential action is not the same as recording every internal event forever.

Evidence should be:

purposeful;

proportionate;

relevant;

governed.

Otherwise Accountability infrastructure itself becomes unsustainable.

The aim is not total observability of people or machines.

It is sufficient verifiability of material action.

### Evidence improves reflection

Operational evidence also strengthens learning.

An organisation can ask:

Why did this exception occur?

Which Protocol caused repeated escalation?

Where did the agent lack context?

Which input produced poor outcomes?

What sequence repeatedly appears before failure?

Accountability evidence therefore supports both assurance and organisational improvement.

### AI implication

Machine-speed action requires machine-speed evidence.

Identity.

Authority.

Provenance.

Decision records where relevant.

Tool invocation history where consequential.

Escalation events.

Outcomes.

This does not solve every problem of AI governance.

But without it, meaningful organisational governance becomes difficult.

### Conducting Question

> **Which machine-speed activities would become effectively unauditable if we relied on humans to reconstruct them after the fact?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**How will we audit this later?**

ask:

**What evidence should the action naturally produce while it happens?**

# Chapter 29

## Organising Humans and Agents Together

The future organisation may not divide neatly into:

human work;

AI work.

Humans and autonomous systems may participate together throughout ordinary activity.

A customer issue arrives.

An agent retrieves history.

Another checks applicable obligations.

A human recognises an important relationship nuance.

A model suggests options.

The human sets a boundary.

An agent executes routine downstream changes.

Evidence is preserved.

A conductor sees only the material exception.

This is not simply automation.

It is orchestration.

### Different actors, different strengths

Humans and AI systems are not interchangeable.

Humans remain deeply important where activity involves:

legitimacy;

care;

relationships;

values;

social meaning;

moral responsibility;

community participation;

ambiguous consequence.

Machines may be particularly effective where activity involves:

scale;

continuous observation;

retrieval;

pattern detection;

routine coordination;

rapid bounded execution.

A mature organisation does not ask which side should win.

It asks how different forms of capability should participate in the same coherent system.

### The common organisational language

This is where the Eight Areas become especially powerful.

Whether the actor is a human employee, a team, an API-driven service or an autonomous agent, the organisation can still ask:

What Direction does this action serve?

What Engagement context is required?

What Enablement should result?

Which Protocols govern interaction?

Is the activity Sustainable?

Through which Process does it occur?

What Accountability is required?

How does it remain Organisationally synchronised?

The implementation differs.

The organising questions remain surprisingly stable.

### Humans should not become AI middleware

One failure mode is already visible.

AI performs part of the work.

A human copies the result into another system.

Another AI analyses it.

A human reviews.

A third system performs another step.

A human approves.

The organisation has automated activities while keeping people as the glue between them.

This may produce local productivity improvements while leaving organisational architecture unchanged.

The conducting objective is different.

Humans should intervene where human judgement matters.

Not simply because the systems cannot coordinate with one another.

### AI should not become management middleware either

The opposite failure is replacing every human coordinator with an AI coordinator while leaving the underlying dependencies untouched.

The organisation becomes faster but remains brittle.

Conducting seeks stronger architecture:

shared Protocols;

clear authority;

reliable Processes;

strong evidence;

explicit escalation;

common Direction.

Then humans and machines can both act without requiring constant mediation.

### Human agency remains explicit

The framework is clear that human and community agency should remain explicit where values, purpose, legitimacy or material consequences are involved.

This is not a nostalgic attachment to human control.

It recognises that organisations exist inside societies.

Their purpose and legitimacy cannot be reduced entirely to optimisation.

Machines can assist with interpretation.

They can show consequences.

They can surface patterns.

They can coordinate.

But deciding what is worth pursuing remains a different category of organisational act.

### The organisation becomes a context network

Once humans and agents both participate, the traditional organisation chart becomes even less descriptive.

Reporting lines still matter.

But so do:

authority relationships;

credential relationships;

data relationships;

Protocol relationships;

dependency relationships;

evidence relationships;

agent-tool relationships.

The organisation increasingly resembles a network of actors operating inside a shared context.

This prepares the ground for the final shift of the book.

Beyond organising people.

Towards organising agency itself.

### Conducting Question

> **Where are humans currently acting as coordination glue between intelligent systems rather than exercising distinctly human judgement?**

### Move Up a Level

Instead of asking:

**Which tasks belong to humans and which belong to AI?**

ask:

**How should different kinds of agency participate together while preserving Direction, legitimacy and coherence?**
