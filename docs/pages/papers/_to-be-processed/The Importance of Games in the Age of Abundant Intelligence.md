# The Importance of Games in the Age of Abundant Intelligence

## When intelligence becomes abundant, challenge becomes something we may need to create for ourselves

For most of human history, life has supplied the game.

Find food.  
Build shelter.  
Raise a family.  
Learn a trade.  
Earn an income.  
Compete for scarce resources.  
Solve problems before somebody else does.

The rules were often harsh, but the objectives were reasonably clear.

Much of what we call **work** emerged because something needed to be done and there were not enough people, resources, knowledge or intelligence available to do it.

Advanced artificial intelligence changes this equation.

If intelligence becomes abundant, and increasingly autonomous systems can design, reason, manufacture, coordinate, teach, diagnose, optimise and operate at extremely low marginal cost, society may gradually encounter a strange new problem:

> What happens when fewer things *need* us?

The answer may be that games become more important, not less.

Not merely computer games.

Games in the broader sense:

- challenges,
- quests,
- competitions,
- collaborations,
- exploration,
- creation,
- discovery,
- mastery,
- achievement,
- community participation,
- and deliberately constructed constraints.

In the age of abundant intelligence and resources, **games may become one of humanity's primary mechanisms for generating meaningful scarcity**.

---

## 1. Scarcity Gave Us the Original Game

Human societies have traditionally been organised around scarcity.

There was never enough:

- food,
- energy,
- land,
- capital,
- knowledge,
- labour,
- expertise,
- computing power,
- or time.

Scarcity naturally generates objectives.

When food is scarce, obtaining food matters.

When knowledge is scarce, learning something valuable creates advantage.

When skilled labour is scarce, becoming highly skilled provides economic value.

When computation is scarce, being able to calculate or reason faster than others matters.

A great deal of human motivation has consequently been attached to solving problems created by scarcity.

Work became one of the largest games ever constructed.

It has:

- objectives,
- rules,
- scores,
- levels,
- status,
- rewards,
- teams,
- competitors,
- achievements,
- progression,
- and consequences.

A career can be understood remarkably easily as a game.

You acquire skills.

You complete challenges.

You gain experience.

You level up.

You earn resources.

You build reputation.

You unlock new opportunities.

Industrial society did not necessarily call this gameplay.

It called it **work**.

---

# 2. Intelligence Abundance Changes the Game

Artificial intelligence introduces something historically unusual.

We may be approaching a world where useful intelligence is no longer particularly scarce.

An AI system can potentially generate thousands of ideas while a person generates ten.

Millions of software agents can potentially operate simultaneously.

A machine can analyse information continuously without fatigue.

A sufficiently capable AI system can potentially teach almost anyone almost anything.

And intelligence can increasingly be duplicated.

Human expertise cannot easily be copied.

Machine intelligence can.

Once a capable system exists, additional instances may become relatively inexpensive.

This changes an economic assumption that has existed throughout civilisation:

> Intelligence has always been scarce because intelligent humans are scarce.

That assumption may no longer hold.

As intelligence abundance combines with abundant computation, robotics, renewable energy and increasingly automated production, other forms of scarcity may also decline.

The result is enormously positive.

But it creates a secondary problem.

Many of the challenges around which human life has been organised may disappear.

---

# 3. The Abundance Paradox

Humans usually imagine abundance as the removal of problems.

That is correct.

But problems also perform an important psychological and social function.

Problems give us something to do.

A mountain creates the possibility of climbing it.

A difficult equation creates the possibility of solving it.

An opponent creates the possibility of competition.

A broken machine creates the possibility of repairing it.

An unknown creates the possibility of discovery.

When the obstacle disappears, the suffering associated with it disappears.

But so does the challenge.

This creates the **Abundance Paradox**:

> The better civilisation becomes at eliminating involuntary problems, the more important it becomes to create voluntary challenges.

Games are extraordinarily good at doing this.

---

# 4. Games Manufacture Scarcity

Consider chess.

There is no physical reason why a bishop must move diagonally.

The restriction is invented.

That invented restriction makes the game possible.

Football would be easier if players simply picked up the ball and carried it into the goal.

But that would destroy the game.

Golf would be trivial if players could simply walk to the hole and place the ball inside.

The difficulty is deliberately constructed.

Games work precisely because they impose unnecessary constraints.

They create artificial scarcity:

- limited moves,
- limited time,
- limited resources,
- boundaries,
- rules,
- opponents,
- uncertainty,
- levels,
- and objectives.

These constraints transform something trivial into something meaningful.

That capability may become extraordinarily important in an abundant civilisation.

We may increasingly move from:

**scarcity imposed by reality**

to:

**constraints chosen by participants.**

Instead of struggling because we have no alternative, we increasingly choose struggles worth having.

---

# 5. From Forced Problems to Chosen Challenges

This may represent one of the largest transitions in human civilisation.

### Scarcity society

> Here is a problem. Solve it or suffer.

### Abundance society

> Here are thousands of possible challenges. Which one is meaningful enough for you to pursue?

The distinction is profound.

The first system is largely driven by necessity.

The second requires agency.

People must increasingly decide:

- What do I care about?
- What do I want to become better at?
- What challenge do I want to attempt?
- Who do I want to attempt it with?
- What can I contribute?
- What world do I want to help create?

The future therefore does not eliminate effort.

It changes the source of effort.

Effort becomes increasingly **self-directed**.

---

# 6. Play Becomes Serious Infrastructure

Play is often treated as the opposite of serious activity.

That distinction may become increasingly obsolete.

Play is actually a sophisticated human learning mechanism.

Through play people:

- experiment,
- model situations,
- test boundaries,
- develop strategies,
- cooperate,
- compete,
- fail safely,
- receive feedback,
- discover abilities,
- negotiate rules,
- develop identity,
- and improve through repeated cycles.

The selfdriven approach explicitly recognises both existing real-world and virtual-world play, alongside the gamification of learning. Its model describes learning as **fun, purposeful and responsive**, incorporating challenges, reflection, scorecards, check-ins and skill recognition.

These characteristics become particularly relevant in an intelligence-abundant society.

The question is no longer merely:

> How can we make education more like a game?

It may become:

> How can we use gameplay to help people construct meaningful lives?

---

# 7. Games Give Humans Something AI Cannot Simply Give Them

Suppose an advanced AI can solve a puzzle immediately.

Does that make the puzzle pointless?

Not necessarily.

A chess engine has been able to defeat almost every human for decades.

Humans still play chess.

A calculator can multiply numbers faster than any person.

People still learn mathematics.

Cars can travel faster than humans.

People still run marathons.

Machines can lift far more than humans.

People still lift weights.

GPS can navigate better than most people.

People still hike through wilderness.

The objective is not always the external output.

Sometimes **the transformation of the participant is the output**.

This distinction becomes critical.

An AI completing a marathon for you has achieved almost nothing of value to you.

An AI lifting your weights does not make you stronger.

An AI playing chess on your behalf does not develop your strategic thinking.

An AI having a meaningful conversation with your friend on your behalf does not deepen your friendship.

Some activities matter precisely because **you participated in them**.

Games therefore protect something essential in an automated world:

> **The value of participation.**

---

# 8. The Outcome Is the Person

Industrial systems overwhelmingly optimise output.

How many units were produced?

How much revenue was generated?

How quickly was the task completed?

AI accelerates this optimisation dramatically.

But games can optimise something different.

The outcome of a good game may be the changed participant.

A person finishes with:

- greater capability,
- deeper relationships,
- better judgement,
- greater confidence,
- stronger identity,
- new knowledge,
- increased resilience,
- memories,
- stories,
- and shared experiences.

This suggests an important inversion.

In the industrial age:

> **The person performs the task to produce the outcome.**

In an abundant-intelligence society:

> **The task may exist to produce the person.**

That is a fundamentally different design philosophy.

---

# 9. Games Create Purpose Without Pretending Scarcity Still Exists

One danger of abundance is attempting to preserve obsolete scarcity merely because our institutions depend upon it.

We could deliberately maintain unnecessary bureaucracy.

We could create artificial employment.

We could prevent automation.

We could preserve inefficient processes so people remain occupied.

But this would be a strange response.

It would amount to maintaining suffering because civilisation has not designed another mechanism for purpose.

Games offer a better alternative.

They openly acknowledge that the constraint is constructed.

Nobody playing football believes humanity requires the ball to enter the opposing goal.

It matters because the participants have collectively agreed that it matters.

That agreement is enough.

This may provide an important insight into future society:

> Meaning does not require scarcity to be physically necessary.

Meaning can emerge from **shared voluntary commitments**.

---

# 10. Games Are Coordination Systems

A game is more than an activity.

It is a miniature governance system.

It defines:

- participants,
- boundaries,
- objectives,
- permitted actions,
- prohibited actions,
- incentives,
- achievements,
- reputation,
- conflict resolution,
- and mechanisms for measuring progress.

Games therefore provide a useful model for coordinating communities.

Imagine community challenges around:

- restoring waterways,
- caring for elderly residents,
- building open-source infrastructure,
- improving local biodiversity,
- mentoring young people,
- discovering scientific knowledge,
- developing new artistic forms,
- improving neighbourhood spaces,
- learning languages,
- building community resilience,
- or exploring entirely new domains.

These are games where the game produces real-world value.

The boundary between **game**, **education**, **work**, **community** and **civic participation** begins to dissolve.

---

# 11. From Jobs to Quests

The job has been one of the dominant coordination units of industrial civilisation.

A job generally says:

> Perform this predefined bundle of activities repeatedly in exchange for access to resources.

But AI may increasingly unbundle the job.

Machines can perform more of its individual tasks.

What remains may be better expressed as dynamic challenges.

Instead of:

**What is your job?**

a future society might increasingly ask:

**What are you working on?**

or even:

**What quest are you currently pursuing?**

A person might simultaneously participate in:

- a community project,
- a research challenge,
- an artistic collaboration,
- a fitness challenge,
- environmental restoration,
- mentoring,
- learning,
- entrepreneurship,
- and games undertaken simply because they are enjoyable.

Identity becomes less tightly bound to occupation.

It becomes connected to **participation**.

---

# 12. AI Makes Better Games Possible

AI does not merely create the need for richer forms of play.

It can also enable them.

AI systems can dynamically generate:

- challenges,
- simulations,
- narratives,
- opponents,
- collaborators,
- learning experiences,
- personalised difficulty,
- real-world quests,
- feedback,
- coaching,
- and pathways between activities.

A static curriculum can become an adaptive learning world.

A community project can become a distributed challenge system.

A person's interests can generate unique pathways rather than forcing everyone through identical sequences.

AI can continuously adjust the difficulty.

Too easy?

Increase the challenge.

Too difficult?

Provide scaffolding.

Lost?

Offer another pathway.

Mastered something?

Open a new level.

In this model AI does not replace human agency.

AI helps create environments in which agency can flourish.

---

# 13. The Infinite Game

Abundance also changes what winning means.

Scarcity encourages finite games.

There is a prize.

Someone wins.

Someone loses.

Resources are allocated.

But an abundant society can increasingly support **infinite games** whose objective is continued participation and growth.

Examples include:

- knowledge,
- science,
- relationships,
- creativity,
- exploration,
- community,
- personal mastery,
- physical development,
- and civilisation itself.

There is no final level of curiosity.

There is no maximum amount of caring.

There is no final piece of music.

There is no final scientific question.

There is no final friendship.

There is no final community worth building.

These games do not need to end.

---

# 14. A Different Kind of Score

If games become more important, society must be careful about what it scores.

Poorly designed games optimise:

- attention,
- consumption,
- addiction,
- status,
- domination,
- financial extraction,
- or meaningless numerical progression.

Good games can encourage:

- curiosity,
- caring,
- construction,
- cooperation,
- creativity,
- mastery,
- exploration,
- contribution,
- health,
- and human connection.

This aligns closely with selfdriven's conception of gameplay as something that includes not only challenges and incentives but reflection, check-ins and recognition of developing skills.

The scoreboard should not become the purpose.

The scoreboard should help participants understand the journey.

---

# 15. The Four Cs as a Game Philosophy

The selfdriven **4Cs** provide a particularly useful framework for designing games for an abundant-intelligence world.

## Curious

Explore.

Ask questions.

Try something unexpected.

Discover what lies beyond the current map.

## Caring

Ensure participation produces relationships rather than merely transactions.

Help other players.

Strengthen communities.

Care about the consequences of the game.

## Constructive

Create something.

Improve something.

Leave the environment better than you found it.

Progress should generate real value.

## Chill

Remember that the game is a game.

Maintain perspective.

Allow experimentation.

Allow failure.

Enjoy participation.

Together they describe a very different objective from industrial optimisation.

The goal is not simply to maximise output.

The goal is to create conditions in which people can **become more human through participation**.

---

# 16. Education May Become the Prototype

Education may be where this transition appears first.

The selfdriven model already describes learners constructing outcome-based learning games involving challenges, levels, incentives, rewards, self-assessment and verification by learning partners.

This becomes considerably more interesting when AI can supply almost unlimited tutoring.

If an intelligent tutor is always available, education no longer needs to primarily optimise the delivery of information.

Information becomes abundant.

Explanation becomes abundant.

Examples become abundant.

Assessment generation becomes abundant.

What remains scarce is:

- attention,
- curiosity,
- motivation,
- experience,
- relationships,
- judgement,
- agency,
- and meaningful participation.

Education therefore shifts from:

> **delivering knowledge**

toward:

> **creating environments in which people want to do something with knowledge.**

That is much closer to game design than traditional instruction.

---

# 17. Games Restore Agency

Perhaps the deepest importance of games is agency.

A player makes choices.

Without meaningful choices there is barely a game.

This matters enormously in a world populated by systems that may eventually be able to make better decisions than humans across many domains.

A civilisation optimised entirely around machine efficiency could gradually remove human decision-making.

The machine knows the fastest route.

The machine knows the ideal diet.

The machine knows the optimal investment.

The machine knows what you should learn.

The machine knows what you should watch.

The machine knows who you should meet.

Eventually the perfectly optimised life risks becoming a life in which the human does very little choosing.

Games push in the opposite direction.

They create spaces where choice itself matters.

They invite the person back into the loop.

---

# 18. Abundant Intelligence Makes Human Engagement Scarce

When intelligence is scarce, intelligence has economic value.

When information is scarce, information has economic value.

When computation is scarce, computation has economic value.

But once these become abundant, another scarce resource becomes increasingly visible:

**human engagement.**

You cannot manufacture genuine participation infinitely.

Attention is finite.

Time is finite.

Relationships are finite.

Human experience is finite.

A person can only deeply care about so many things.

This means a future abundance economy may increasingly compete not for intelligence but for **meaningful human engagement**.

Games are among humanity's most powerful technologies for generating it.

---

# 19. The New Scarcity

The age of abundant intelligence therefore does not eliminate scarcity entirely.

It changes what is scarce.

Scarce things may increasingly include:

- purpose,
- attention,
- trust,
- belonging,
- reputation,
- authenticity,
- human relationships,
- physical experiences,
- shared history,
- agency,
- and time.

These cannot simply be generated by adding another GPU.

They are relational and experiential.

Games create structures in which these forms of scarcity can produce positive outcomes without recreating the destructive scarcity of earlier economic systems.

---

# 20. A Society That Knows How to Play

The great risk of the intelligence age may not be that humans have nothing to do.

There will always be things that can be done.

The harder problem is whether people have things they **want** to do.

That distinction matters.

An abundant civilisation needs mechanisms through which people can continually discover:

- challenges worth attempting,
- people worth collaborating with,
- skills worth developing,
- places worth exploring,
- communities worth strengthening,
- and ideas worth pursuing.

Games provide exactly this structure.

They turn possibility into participation.

---

# Conclusion

The industrial age taught us to work.

The intelligence age may require us to become much better at play.

Not because civilisation becomes frivolous.

Quite the opposite.

As machines increasingly handle activities that are necessary, humans gain the opportunity to spend more of their lives on activities that are **chosen**.

Games allow us to construct meaningful challenges without requiring suffering.

They allow effort without coercion.

Competition without existential scarcity.

Failure without catastrophe.

Progress without endless consumption.

Learning without compulsory instruction.

Coordination without conventional employment.

And achievement without pretending that every activity must have economic necessity.

The ultimate promise of abundance is therefore not a world in which humans do nothing.

It is a world in which humanity gains greater freedom to decide **what is worth doing**.

In such a world, games stop being an escape from reality.

They become one of the ways we construct it.

> **When machines can solve almost any necessary problem, one of humanity's great challenges will be creating games worth playing.**
