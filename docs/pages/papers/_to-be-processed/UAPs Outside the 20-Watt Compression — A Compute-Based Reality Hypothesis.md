# UAPs Outside the 20-Watt Compression  
## A Compute-Based Reality Hypothesis

### Abstract

What if some unidentified anomalous phenomena are not objects performing impossible manoeuvres within our physical world, but phenomena whose native existence is not fully contained within the compressed representation of reality accessible to human observers?

The human brain operates on approximately 20 watts of metabolic power. Yet from this extraordinarily constrained physical system emerges our apparently continuous experience of three-dimensional space, time, objects, motion, causality and identity. Neuroscience establishes the approximate energy figure; the further proposition developed here is speculative: **the experienced world may be an aggressively compressed representation of a much richer underlying computational reality.**

Compute-Based Reality (CBR) provides a framework in which this possibility can be explored.

CBR proposes that reality is fundamentally composed not of objects moving through pre-existing space, but of states, relationships, transitions, synchronization and reconciliation. Space itself may emerge from the topology of those relationships.

Within such a model, the ordinary human world can be regarded as an observer-accessible projection of a much larger state space.

This paper calls that projection the **20W Compression**.

Under this interpretation, certain UAP observations could arise when a phenomenon that is not completely constrained to this compressed observer manifold intersects it.

The result might look extraordinary from within the compression:

- extreme acceleration,
- discontinuous movement,
- sudden appearance or disappearance,
- absence of expected aerodynamic effects,
- inconsistent observations between sensors,
- unusual transitions between air, water and space,
- or apparent violations of conventional inertia.

The phenomenon itself may violate none of the fundamental rules of reality.

It may simply not be moving in the reality we think it is moving through.

---

# 1. The 20-Watt Miracle

The adult human brain has an overall metabolic power consumption of roughly **20 watts**.

From that tiny energy budget it constructs something remarkable:

> a usable reality.

The brain does not receive reality directly.

It receives electrical signals generated from a limited collection of biological sensors:

- a narrow electromagnetic spectrum,
- pressure variations interpreted as sound,
- chemical interactions interpreted as smell and taste,
- mechanical inputs interpreted as touch,
- vestibular information,
- proprioception,
- internal biological signals.

The overwhelming majority of the physical universe never reaches conscious perception.

Even the information that does reach the nervous system is filtered, integrated, predicted, discarded and abstracted.

We do not experience photon streams.

We experience **a chair**.

We do not experience pressure waves.

We experience **a voice**.

We do not experience millions of independent retinal measurements.

We experience **a person walking across a room**.

Reality has already been compressed into objects, locations, identities, causes and trajectories.

This suggests an important distinction:

> **Experienced reality is not necessarily identical to underlying reality.**

The extraordinary achievement of the brain may therefore not simply be computation at approximately 20 watts.

It may be **compression at approximately 20 watts**.

# 2. The 20W Compression

Within CBR, we can represent underlying reality at time \(t\) as a state:

\[
R_t
\]

An observer never has access to the complete state.

Instead, the observer receives some projection:

\[
O_t = C(R_t)
\]

where \(C\) is a compression function incorporating:

- sensory limitations,
- energetic limitations,
- neural architecture,
- temporal sampling,
- learned models,
- attention,
- prediction,
- memory,
- conceptual categories.

For humans we can symbolically write:

\[
O_t = C_{20W}(R_t)
\]

This is not intended to imply that exactly twenty watts determines some sharp information-theoretic boundary.

**20W is a useful shorthand for the extreme computational constraint under which biological human reality-model construction takes place.**

The important idea is the compression.

The world we experience may represent only a tiny, highly useful subset of the possible structure present in \(R_t\).

# 3. CBR Changes What “Space” Means

This becomes particularly interesting under Compute-Based Reality.

In conventional intuition:

\[
\text{object} \rightarrow \text{moves through space} \rightarrow \text{over time}
\]

CBR reverses the dependency.

The more fundamental sequence could instead be:

\[
\text{state relationships}
\rightarrow
\text{synchronization}
\rightarrow
\text{persistent topology}
\rightarrow
\text{emergent geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{experienced space}
\]

Space is therefore not necessarily the container in which computation occurs.

Space may be **what persistent computational relationships look like from inside the system**.

An observer inhabiting this structure experiences something approximating a three-dimensional manifold:

\[
M^3_t
\]

But the observer does not necessarily have access to the complete computational structure from which that manifold emerges.

Our experienced three-dimensional universe could therefore be understood as a **chart over a much larger state graph**.

That distinction radically changes the UAP question.

# 4. The Conventional UAP Question

The conventional question is:

> How could an object accelerate that quickly?

Under ordinary physical interpretation, an observed trajectory might be represented as:

\[
x(t)
\]

Velocity becomes:

\[
v=\frac{dx}{dt}
\]

and acceleration:

\[
a=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}
\]

If a reported object appears to move from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\) extremely quickly, enormous velocity and acceleration may be inferred.

This in turn implies questions involving:

- inertia,
- energy,
- propulsion,
- heat,
- atmospheric drag,
- sonic effects,
- structural loads.

And therefore the conclusion:

> Something cannot move like that.

But this conclusion contains an assumption.

It assumes:

\[
x(t)
\]

is the object's actual trajectory.

CBR allows another possibility.

It may only be the **projection of the trajectory into our observer manifold**.

# 5. The UAP May Not Be Moving Through Our Space

Suppose a phenomenon exists as some structure:

\[
U_t \subset R_t
\]

Our observation is not \(U_t\).

It is:

\[
\hat U_t=C_{20W}(U_t)
\]

The trajectory we infer is therefore:

\[
\hat U(t)
\]

rather than the phenomenon's actual path through underlying state space.

Now something interesting becomes possible.

Two states that appear widely separated in our three-dimensional projection:

\[
x_1
\qquad\qquad
x_2
\]

may not be widely separated in the underlying computational topology.

Their apparent geometric distance could be large while their **state-transition distance** is small.

Conceptually:

\[
D_{space}(x_1,x_2) \gg D_{state}(U_1,U_2)
\]

A transition could therefore be computationally local while appearing geometrically non-local.

From inside the 20W Compression we would see:

> object here  
> object there

and infer:

> extremely rapid movement between here and there.

But perhaps nothing travelled across the intervening space at that velocity.

The underlying state simply transitioned.

# 6. The Flatland Error

A useful analogy is a three-dimensional object passing through a two-dimensional plane.

Imagine observers that can perceive only the plane.

A sphere approaches their world.

Initially:

\[
\varnothing
\]

Then suddenly a point appears.

It expands into a circle.

The circle grows.

Then shrinks.

Then becomes a point.

Then disappears.

To the two-dimensional observer, an object has apparently:

- materialised,
- changed dimensions,
- transformed itself,
- and vanished.

Nothing supernatural occurred.

The observer simply saw an intersection between a higher-order structure and its accessible manifold.

CBR does not require literal additional spatial dimensions for this analogy to work.

The extra structure could instead exist in:

- computational state space,
- synchronization topology,
- reconciliation relationships,
- information connectivity,
- or some other structure from which geometry emerges.

The critical distinction is between:

> **native state topology**

and

> **observer-visible spatial topology**.

# 7. UAPs as Compression Artefacts

This leads to a striking possibility.

Some apparently anomalous UAP behaviour may be analogous to **aliasing**.

Consider a rapidly rotating wheel recorded by a camera.

Because the camera samples reality discretely, the wheel can appear to:

- rotate slowly,
- stop,
- or rotate backwards.

The wheel never actually performed those motions.

The anomaly exists in the relationship between the phenomenon and the sampling system.

Similarly:

\[
\text{Underlying phenomenon}
\xrightarrow{\text{projection}}
\text{20W Compression}
\xrightarrow{\text{interpretation}}
\text{apparently impossible behaviour}
\]

This distinction is fundamental.

It moves the question from:

> **What propulsion system allows this object to break physics?**

toward:

> **What underlying state transition would project into our observer model as this trajectory?**

That is a very different scientific question.

# 8. Why Different Sensors Could See Different Things

UAP investigations repeatedly encounter another problem: incomplete and inconsistent sensor information.

NASA explicitly notes that the available high-quality observations remain insufficient for firm conclusions, while AARO reports that most cases appear ordinary and many unresolved cases remain unresolved because adequate verifiable data are unavailable.

This is important.

There is currently no scientific basis for claiming that unexplained UAP observations demonstrate this CBR hypothesis—or extraterrestrial technology. NASA explicitly states that it has found no credible evidence that UAPs are extraterrestrial.

But CBR suggests an interesting experimental direction for the genuinely anomalous residual.

Different sensors perform different compressions:

\[
C_{radar}(R)
\]

\[
C_{IR}(R)
\]

\[
C_{optical}(R)
\]

\[
C_{human}(R)
\]

They are not observing reality directly.

They are sampling different properties and constructing different representations.

For an ordinary aircraft these projections should converge readily toward a common model.

For something incompletely represented by our spatial ontology, they might not.

We could see:

\[
C_{radar}(U)
\neq
C_{IR}(U)
\neq
C_{optical}(U)
\]

yet each observation could still be a legitimate projection of the same underlying phenomenon.

The apparent contradictions would themselves become data.

# 9. Apparent Acceleration Without Propulsion

One particularly interesting implication concerns acceleration.

If an object literally accelerates through the atmosphere at extreme rates, conventional physics predicts consequences.

Depending on the situation, these could include:

- energy expenditure,
- momentum transfer,
- atmospheric disturbance,
- heating,
- shockwaves,
- structural forces.

But suppose the apparent acceleration is instead:

\[
\frac{d^2 C(U)}{dt^2}
\]

rather than:

\[
C\left(\frac{d^2U}{dt^2}\right)
\]

These are not necessarily equivalent.

A nonlinear projection can produce enormous apparent movement without correspondingly enormous native movement.

The anomaly might therefore be not:

> acceleration without force

but:

> **projection without equivalent local traversal**.

This could provide one conceptual explanation for reports combining extraordinary apparent kinematics with weak corresponding signatures of propulsion.

Again, that does not establish that such reports are accurate.

It identifies what would become scientifically interesting **if sufficiently instrumented examples were demonstrated to exist**.

# 10. Transmedium Behaviour Becomes Less Strange

The same reasoning applies to apparent transitions between:

- atmosphere,
- ocean,
- near-space.

Humans categorize these as radically different environments because our engineering systems interact strongly with their physical properties.

An aeroplane, submarine and spacecraft require profoundly different designs.

But those distinctions exist inside our emergent physical representation.

If a phenomenon's native state relationship is not principally constrained by aerodynamic or hydrodynamic traversal, the boundaries may be less fundamental.

From the CBR perspective:

\[
\text{air}
\rightarrow
\text{water}
\rightarrow
\text{space}
\]

may represent changes in the local state environment rather than barriers defining the object's fundamental movement mechanism.

The phenomenon does not necessarily possess miraculous technology capable of defeating every medium.

It might operate at a level where **medium is not the primary coordinate system**.

# 11. Appearance and Disappearance

Sudden disappearance also becomes conceptually simpler.

The conventional interpretation is:

\[
\text{visible object}

\text{rapid departure}
\]

CBR permits:

\[
U \cap M_{observer} \neq \varnothing
\]

followed by:

\[
U \cap M_{observer} = \varnothing

The phenomenon has not necessarily ceased to exist.

Its intersection with the observer-accessible manifold has ceased.

This would resemble the sphere leaving Flatland.

From the inhabitants' perspective:

> it vanished.

From the sphere's perspective:

> nothing particularly interesting happened.

It might manipulate:

- state adjacency,
- synchronization,

What humans interpret as a vehicle travelling enormous distances might instead be a persistent structure changing how it intersects our local geometry.

The question:

> Where did it come from?

In the underlying topology, it might already have been **nearby**.

# 13. UAPs Could Exist Beside Us but Outside Our Compression

This produces an even stranger possibility.

Something can be:

- computationally adjacent,
- yet spatially absent.

Our concept of "here" is derived from our geometry.

But if geometry is emergent, underlying adjacency does not necessarily equal geometric proximity.

CBR therefore allows the conceptual possibility that structures could persist within the same underlying reality while normally lying outside the human observer projection.

They need not live:

- on another planet,
- in another universe,
- in another conventional spatial dimension.

They may simply occupy regions of the state graph that our biological compression does not normally render.

In that case UAP events could represent temporary **cross-compression interactions**.

Not arrival.

Not departure.

Intersection.

# 14. The 20W Ontological Trap

Human beings naturally make a subtle epistemological error:

\[
\text{what we perceive}
\approx
\text{what exists}
\]

We know intellectually that this is false.

Humans cannot directly perceive:

- radio waves,
- neutrinos,
- magnetic fields,
- most electromagnetic radiation,
- microscopic structures,
- quantum states.

Technology repeatedly expands the observable world.

But we often retain a deeper assumption:

> although our senses are limited, our *ontology* is basically complete.

We assume that reality fundamentally consists of the kinds of things our compressed model already contains:

- objects,
- three-dimensional positions,
- continuous trajectories,
- local interactions,
- linear causal histories.

CBR questions that assumption.

Perhaps our biggest compression is not sensory.

It is **ontological**.

We compress reality into a particular kind of world.

And then become surprised when something does not behave like an object belonging to that world.

# 15. A CBR UAP Research Programme

The hypothesis is useful only if it leads toward falsifiable investigation.

Rather than merely labelling unexplained observations as exotic, CBR suggests looking for characteristic projection effects.

## Prediction 1 — Structured multi-sensor disagreement

If a UAP is merely an ordinary physical object obscured by poor sensing, better observations should increasingly converge on a conventional trajectory.

A cross-compression phenomenon might instead produce repeatable but systematically different representations across sensing modalities.

The disagreement should be **structured rather than random**.

---

## Prediction 2 — Apparent motion without proportional local effects

Extreme projected acceleration may occur without the environmental effects expected from equivalent physical traversal.

Researchers should therefore compare:

\[
\text{observed trajectory}
\]

against:

\[
\text{required local energy/momentum exchange}
\]

rather than treating the trajectory alone as evidence of extraordinary acceleration.

---

## Prediction 3 — Discontinuous spatial trajectories with continuous state signatures

A phenomenon might disappear at:

\[
x_1
\]

and appear at:

\[
x_2
\]

while some other measurable property remains continuous.

That would suggest continuity in a latent state space despite discontinuity in reconstructed geometry.

This would be particularly significant.

---

## Prediction 4 — Geometry-dependent anomalies

If space emerges from synchronization topology, genuinely anomalous behaviour may correlate with particular relational or synchronization conditions rather than simply geographic locations.

The right experimental question might eventually become:

> Under what state configurations do intersections occur?

rather than:

> Where do UFOs appear?

---

## Prediction 5 — Better sensing reveals latent topology

Distributed sensor networks could reconstruct the hidden state responsible for apparently contradictory observations.

Instead of forcing every measurement immediately into:

\[
(x,y,z,t)
\]

the raw measurements could be represented first as a graph.

The system could then ask:

> What latent topology produces all these observations simultaneously?

That is very close to a **Verified Context Graph** approach to observation.

# 16. What Would Falsify the Hypothesis?

A useful CBR-UAP hypothesis must be capable of failing.

Evidence against it would include:

1. High-quality anomalous cases consistently resolving into conventional aircraft, balloons, atmospheric phenomena, sensor artefacts or other ordinary causes.

2. Apparent extreme accelerations disappearing once parallax, calibration, platform motion and sensor geometry are accurately reconstructed.

3. Every well-observed trajectory exhibiting exactly the environmental energy transfer predicted by conventional local movement.

4. No persistent or reproducible structure appearing in multi-sensor disagreements.

5. No evidence that representing observations in state-space or graph-space explains anomalies better than conventional four-dimensional trajectory reconstruction.

Given current public evidence, these conventional explanations remain the appropriate default. AARO states that only a very small percentage of reports exhibit anomalous signatures, with lack of quality data being a major obstacle to analysis.

The CBR hypothesis therefore belongs not in the category of established explanation but in the category of:

> **testable alternative ontology.**

# 17. From “Impossible Physics” to “Incorrect Projection”

The most important contribution of the hypothesis may be conceptual.

UAP discussions often divide into two camps:

\[
\text{ordinary explanation}
\]

or

\[
\text{extraordinary technology}
\]

CBR introduces a third category:

\[
\boxed{\text{incorrect ontological projection}}
\]

Something may appear to violate the physics of objects travelling through three-dimensional space because **object travelling through three-dimensional space is the wrong abstraction**.

This is analogous to interpreting:

- quantum phenomena as tiny classical billiard balls,
- relativistic effects using absolute Newtonian time,
- or network routing purely in terms of geographic distance.

The model fails because the abstraction fails.

# 18. Beyond the 20W Compression

The human brain's approximately twenty-watt energy budget is one of the most remarkable compression engines known.

It gives us a reality stable enough to:

- survive,
- cooperate,
- engineer,
- discover mathematics,
- build civilizations,
- and investigate the universe itself.

But evolution had no requirement to provide a complete representation of reality.

It needed to provide a **useful one**.

CBR therefore raises a profound possibility:

> The world humans experience may be the interface, not the substrate.

If so, anomalous phenomena could occasionally expose the distinction.

A UAP might not be breaking the rules of reality.

It may be breaking the assumptions of our compression.

It may not be accelerating impossibly.

It may not be travelling impossible distances.

It may not be appearing from nowhere.

It may not even be a "craft" in the sense that our ontology suggests.

Instead, what we see may be the temporary projection of a persistent structure whose native existence occupies more of computational reality than our approximately twenty-watt biological observer can render.

In that model, the deepest question posed by UAPs would cease to be:

> **What are they?**

The more fundamental question would become:

> **What does reality have to be for something to look like that to us?**

And perhaps the most interesting possibility is this:

\[
\boxed{
\text{The anomaly may not exist outside reality.}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{It may exist outside our compression of reality.}
}
\]

## References and Scientific Context

The approximately 20-watt metabolic requirement of the adult human brain is well established in neuroscience literature.

NASA defines UAP as observations that cannot initially be identified as aircraft or known natural phenomena and emphasizes the scarcity of sufficiently high-quality observations for strong scientific conclusions.

NASA currently states that it has found no credible evidence that UAPs are extraterrestrial and that available UAP observations do not provide evidence of alien technologies.

AARO likewise reports that only a small fraction of cases exhibit genuinely anomalous signatures and identifies insufficient verifiable data as a major reason cases remain unresolved.

The **20W Compression**, Compute-Based Reality interpretation, emergent-space model and proposed UAP projection mechanism developed in this paper are speculative hypotheses rather than established scientific theories.
