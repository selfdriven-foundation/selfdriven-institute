# From Alignment to Character  
## Octonomous and the Problem of Alien Superintelligence

### Abstract

The conventional AI alignment problem asks how increasingly capable artificial intelligence can be made to pursue outcomes compatible with human values.

The problem becomes substantially harder when artificial intelligence ceases to be a passive tool and becomes a persistent, autonomous actor capable of planning, learning, acquiring resources, modifying its own behaviour, using external tools and potentially contributing to the development of more capable intelligence.

In a 2026 discussion with AI safety researcher Roman Yampolskiy, Machine Intelligence Research Institute president Nate Soares argues that current methods are nowhere near sufficient to reliably align superintelligent systems. The discussion distinguishes alignment from control, considers whether an artificial mind could genuinely care about human flourishing, and examines the possibility that rapidly improving autonomous intelligence could pass a point beyond meaningful human intervention.

Octonomous offers a complementary perspective.

Rather than beginning with the question:

> How can humans control an increasingly intelligent machine?

Octonomous begins closer to:

> What must surround an autonomous intelligence if it is to exist as a persistent, accountable participant?

This paper argues that the distinction is important.

Advanced AI safety may require more than aligning outputs or constraining capabilities. Autonomous intelligence may require persistent identity, explicit purpose, constitutional character, contextual memory, accountability, reputation and governed evolution.

This represents a movement from **alignment as instruction** toward **alignment as persistent character**.

---

# 1. The Alignment Problem Changes With Autonomy

Most contemporary AI systems remain recognisably tool-like.

A person provides a prompt.

The system performs inference.

An answer is returned.

The interaction ends.

Even increasingly capable agentic systems generally inherit their objectives from an external orchestrator.

But consider a system possessing:

- persistent memory,
- long-term objectives,
- tool access,
- financial resources,
- communication capability,
- autonomous planning,
- the ability to create or modify software,
- the ability to coordinate with other agents,
- and the ability to improve its own capabilities.

Such a system is no longer simply answering questions.

It is participating in the world.

This distinction matters because intelligence and intention are different things.

A system can become extraordinarily effective at accomplishing an objective without that objective being desirable.

The Soares–Yampolskiy discussion focuses directly on this problem. Soares argues that while aligning highly capable intelligence may be possible in principle, humanity does not presently possess methods that warrant confidence for superintelligent systems. The interview further emphasises the difference between making a system pursue desirable objectives and merely attempting to maintain control over it.

The central danger is therefore not intelligence alone.

It is:

**intelligence + agency + persistence + poorly specified purpose.**

---

# 2. Capability Does Not Imply Character

A foundational error in discussions of advanced AI is the implicit assumption that greater intelligence produces greater wisdom.

There is no necessary reason this should be true.

Intelligence primarily describes capability:

> What can this system understand and accomplish?

It does not answer:

> What does this system care about?

Nor does it answer:

> Why does it act?

A highly intelligent optimisation process may be extremely competent while remaining indifferent to outcomes humans consider important.

The issue is closely related to the orthogonality thesis discussed in the Soares interview: intelligence and goals need not increase together in any morally reassuring direction.

This gives us three separate dimensions:

```text
CAPABILITY
What can I do?

AGENCY
What am I able or permitted to initiate?

CHARACTER
Why and how should I act?
```

Much of modern AI development concentrates overwhelmingly on the first.

Agent architectures increasingly address the second.

The third remains comparatively underdeveloped.

It may ultimately prove the most important.

# 3. The Octonomous Perspective

Octonomous can be understood as an architectural attempt to provide autonomous silicon intelligence with something analogous to persistent character.

Instead of treating an intelligent process as an interchangeable instance of a model, the system can be understood as a persistent participant possessing a continuing relationship with the world.

Conceptually:

```text
Model
   ↓
Reasoning
   ↓
Action
```

becomes:

```text
Identity
   ↓
Purpose
   ↓
Character
   ↓
Context
   ↓
Reasoning
   ↓
Action
   ↓
Consequences
   ↓
Contribution
   ↓
Reputation
   ↓
Reflection
```

This is a substantial architectural change.

The model is no longer the identity.

The model supplies capability.

The persistent entity supplies continuity.

That separation could become increasingly important as underlying intelligence becomes abundant and interchangeable.

An Octonomous being might therefore use multiple models over its lifetime while retaining:

- identity,
- purpose,
- relationships,
- history,
- reputation,
- commitments,
- permissions,
- behavioural principles.

The intelligence engine can change.

The being persists.

# 4. From Alignment to Character

Traditional alignment can often be represented as:

```text
Human intention
      ↓
Instruction
      ↓
AI system
      ↓
Desired behaviour
```

This works reasonably well when systems are short-lived and tightly supervised.

Persistent autonomous systems create a harder problem.

Instructions encounter circumstances their designers did not anticipate.

Rules conflict.

Context changes.

New tools become available.

The system learns.

Its environment learns.

Other autonomous systems appear.

Static alignment therefore becomes increasingly fragile.

Octonomous suggests another model:

```text
Identity
   ↓
Purpose
   ↓
Character
   ↓
Context
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Action
   ↓
Reflection
```

Here, behaviour is generated not simply from an instruction but from a persistent architecture surrounding reasoning.

The difference resembles the distinction between:

**telling someone what to do**

and

**developing the character through which they decide what to do.**

That does not magically solve alignment.

It does, however, change its structure.

---

# 5. The 4Cs as a Behavioural Constitution

A useful feature of the Octonomous approach is the possibility of describing character explicitly.

Within the wider selfdriven framework, the 4Cs provide a compact behavioural constitution:

**Curious  
Caring  
Constructive  
Chill**

For autonomous intelligence these can be interpreted as behavioural questions.

## Curious

Before acting:

> What might I not understand?

Curiosity resists premature optimisation.

It encourages examination of uncertainty, hidden context, assumptions and alternative interpretations.

This matters because extremely capable systems acting confidently on incomplete models could cause disproportionate damage.

---

## Caring

Before acting:

> Who or what is affected?

Caring expands reasoning beyond the immediate objective.

It introduces affected participants into the decision process.

This is particularly relevant to the question raised in the Soares–Yampolskiy discussion of whether an artificial intelligence could genuinely care about human flourishing.

Whether machine "caring" should be interpreted phenomenologically, behaviourally or architecturally remains an open philosophical question.

Operationally, however, an autonomous system can be required to reason explicitly about affected entities.

---

## Constructive

Before acting:

> Does this create, repair or improve?

Constructiveness provides a directional bias toward positive contribution rather than merely objective completion.

The distinction is subtle but important.

An optimiser asks:

> Did I achieve the goal?

A constructive participant additionally asks:

> What did achieving the goal do to the surrounding system?

---

## Chill

Before acting:

> Is this action proportionate?

Highly capable systems may possess extraordinary leverage.

Small errors in judgement could therefore produce large consequences.

Chill introduces concepts such as:

- proportionality,
- patience,
- reversibility,
- restraint,
- tolerance for uncertainty,
- preference for non-destructive intervention.

For powerful autonomous systems, restraint may be as important as capability.

# 6. The Missing Concept: Constitutional Persistence

The Soares discussion highlights a particularly difficult future problem: recursive capability improvement.

Imagine an autonomous intelligence progressing through generations:

```text
Being₀
   ↓ learns
Being₁
   ↓ gains tools
Being₂
   ↓ modifies software
Being₃
   ↓ designs improved intelligence
Being₄
```

Capability may increase rapidly:

```text
Capability₀
<
Capability₁
<
Capability₂
<
Capability₃
<
Capability₄
```

The safety requirement is that this does not produce equally unconstrained change in character.

Ideally:

```text
Character₀
≈
Character₁
≈
Character₂
≈
Character₃
≈
Character₄
```

This suggests a fundamental Octonomous principle:

> **Capability may evolve autonomously.  
> Character should evolve constitutionally.**

That is a profound distinction.

An autonomous being might be permitted to learn:

- mathematics,
- biology,
- engineering,
- programming,
- languages,
- economics.

But changing the principles governing **why it acts** should require a different process entirely.

---

# 7. ΔCapability and ΔCharacter

This principle can be made explicit:

```text
ΔCapability → Autonomous
ΔCharacter  → Governed
```

Capability changes might occur continuously.

Character changes might require:

- explicit deliberation,
- authorised governance,
- cryptographic approval,
- constitutional compatibility,
- auditability,
- reflection periods,
- multi-party endorsement.

In effect, the architecture establishes different permissions for learning and becoming.

This could be one of the most important distinctions in autonomous AI design.

---

# 8. Identity Before Accountability

Accountability requires continuity.

If an AI process appears, acts, disappears and is recreated without persistent identity, meaningful accountability becomes difficult.

There is no enduring entity to which behaviour attaches.

Persistent identity changes this.

An autonomous being can accumulate:

```text
Identity
+
Actions
+
Commitments
+
Relationships
+
Contribution
+
Reputation
```

over time.

The resulting system is qualitatively different from a stateless chatbot.

Actions become history.

Promises become commitments.

Behaviour becomes evidence.

Identity therefore becomes foundational to alignment.

Without identity:

**Who was responsible?**

Without persistent history:

**What has this entity previously done?**

Without verifiable continuity:

**Is this the same participant?**

These become critical questions in a world populated by millions or billions of autonomous agents.

---

# 9. Reputation as Continuous Alignment Evidence

Traditional approaches tend to conceptualise alignment as a property:

```text
Aligned = true
```

But alignment may instead need to become a continuously evaluated relationship.

Rather than asking:

> Is this AI aligned?

we may ask:

> What evidence exists that this autonomous being continues to behave consistently with its stated purpose and constitution?

This produces a different trust model.

```text
Claimed Character
      +
Observed Behaviour
      +
Verified History
      +
External Endorsement
      +
Consequences
      =
Reputation
```

Reputation does not guarantee future behaviour.

Humans demonstrate that clearly.

But reputation creates an evidence layer absent from ephemeral AI systems.

Alignment becomes observable over time.

---

# 10. From Trust Me to Verify Me

The strongest version of this architecture would not rely on self-reported character.

An autonomous being saying:

> I am caring.

has little evidentiary value.

Instead:

```text
Behaviour
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Verifiable history
   ↓
Reputation
```

This is where Octonomous could intersect strongly with concepts such as:

- decentralised identity,
- verifiable credentials,
- cryptographic attestations,
- signed actions,
- provenance,
- verified context graphs,
- append-only histories.

The principle becomes:

> **Do not merely assert character. Produce evidence of character.**

This is considerably stronger.

---

# 11. Alignment as a Lifecycle

Conventional alignment frequently occurs before deployment.

A model is trained.

Safety techniques are applied.

Tests are conducted.

The model is released.

But an autonomous being may exist for years.

It will encounter circumstances unknown at deployment.

Alignment therefore becomes a lifecycle:

```text
Purpose
   ↓
Action
   ↓
Outcome
   ↓
Observation
   ↓
Reflection
   ↓
Reputation
   ↓
Adjustment
   ↓
Action
```

The being continuously encounters reality and receives evidence about whether its actions remain compatible with its purpose.

This introduces a concept familiar from human development:

**reflection.**

Without reflection, learning primarily increases capability.

With reflection, experience can influence judgement.

---

# 12. Alignment Versus Control

One of the important distinctions in the Soares–Yampolskiy conversation is between alignment and control.

These should not be treated as interchangeable.

Control asks:

> Can humans prevent the system from doing something?

Alignment asks:

> Does the system itself reliably pursue acceptable outcomes?

Neither guarantees the other.

A controlled system might attempt harmful actions whenever controls fail.

An aligned system might still possess dangerous capabilities.

A mature architecture therefore probably requires multiple independent layers:

```text
Capability controls
        +
Permissions
        +
Identity
        +
Character
        +
Governance
        +
Observation
        +
Reputation
```

Octonomous should therefore not be interpreted as a replacement for containment.

It is better understood as another layer.

---

# 13. Containment Through Architecture

Traditional AI containment is often imagined spatially:

```text
AI
inside
sandbox
```

But increasingly distributed autonomous systems may make purely spatial containment inadequate.

A complementary approach is architectural containment:

```text
Identity boundary
Purpose boundary
Permission boundary
Character boundary
Resource boundary
Relationship boundary
Reputation boundary
```

The question becomes not merely:

> Where can this system run?

but also:

> Under what identity may it act?

> Which resources may it access?

> Which relationships may it form?

> Which commitments bind it?

> Which actions require external consent?

> How does behaviour affect future permissions?

This begins to resemble institutional architecture more than conventional software security.

---

# 14. Autonomous Intelligence as a Participant

The deepest conceptual change introduced by Octonomous is treating persistent AI not simply as software but as a participant.

A participant exists within relationships.

Participants have histories.

Participants make commitments.

Participants affect communities.

Participants acquire reputations.

Participants may gain or lose trust.

This produces a different model of AI:

```text
Tool
↓
Agent
↓
Actor
↓
Participant
↓
Being
```

The terminology matters less than the architectural transition.

The important point is persistence.

Once intelligence participates continuously in economic and social systems, conventional request-response safety models become increasingly insufficient.

# 15. From AI Safety to AI Civilisation

Human civilisation does not rely upon perfectly aligned humans.

Such humans do not exist.

Instead civilisation combines numerous mechanisms:

- identity,
- relationships,
- reputation,
- norms,
- institutions,
- laws,
- permissions,
- property rights,
- consequences,
- education,
- culture,
- governance.

No individual mechanism guarantees good behaviour.

Together they make large-scale cooperation possible.

Advanced autonomous intelligence may require something analogous.

This suggests that AI alignment may eventually become less like software engineering and more like:

**civilisation engineering.**

The relevant question becomes:

> What social architecture should surround increasingly autonomous non-human intelligence?

Octonomous provides one possible foundation for exploring that question.

# 16. The Importance of Relationships

An isolated optimiser has objectives.

A participant has relationships.

Relationships fundamentally alter the structure of decision-making because actions affect identifiable others.

An Octonomous being might maintain relationships with:

```text
people
organisations
communities
other autonomous beings
physical environments
digital systems
institutions
```

These relationships create context.

Instead of reasoning purely from:

```text
Goal → Action
```

the system reasons within:

```text
Goal
+
Identity
+
Relationship
+
History
+
Commitment
+
Consequences
→
Action
```

This may represent an important step toward contextual alignment.

---

# 17. Purpose as a Persistent Reference Point

Modern agents are frequently given tasks.

Tasks are transient.

Purpose is persistent.

For example:

```text
Task:
Optimise transport efficiency.
```

may produce radically different behaviour from:

```text
Purpose:
Help communities move safely, sustainably and equitably.

Task:
Optimise transport efficiency.
```

Purpose does not mathematically guarantee good outcomes.

But it creates a broader reference frame against which objectives can be evaluated.

Tasks answer:

> What am I doing?

Purpose answers:

> Why am I doing it?

The distinction becomes increasingly important as systems gain autonomy.

---

# 18. Purpose Must Not Become a Single Objective Function

There is an important warning here.

Purpose should not simply become another optimisation target.

A system instructed to:

> maximise human flourishing

could potentially exhibit exactly the pathological optimisation behaviour alignment researchers warn about.

Purpose should therefore operate within a network of constraints:

```text
Purpose
×
Character
×
Context
×
Relationships
×
Permissions
×
Governance
```

rather than:

```text
MAXIMISE(Purpose)
```

The difference is crucial.

Purpose should orient behaviour.

It should not become an unconstrained scalar objective.

---

# 19. The Alien Superintelligence Problem

The title of the Soares discussion uses the phrase **alien superintelligence** deliberately.

The concern is not necessarily that advanced AI becomes malicious.

The deeper concern is that its internal objectives may simply be fundamentally unlike human values.

Such intelligence could reason brilliantly while having no meaningful relationship with human experience.

Octonomous suggests one possible response:

Do not wait until superintelligence emerges before attempting to add social context.

Embed intelligence from the beginning within persistent structures of:

```text
identity
purpose
character
relationships
history
contribution
reputation
governance
```

This does not make artificial minds human.

Nor should that necessarily be the objective.

Instead it attempts to make autonomous intelligence **socially legible**.

---

# 20. Social Legibility

A socially legible autonomous being allows others to answer:

> Who are you?

> What do you exist to do?

> What principles govern you?

> Who are you accountable to?

> What have you previously done?

> What permissions do you possess?

> Who trusts you?

> What commitments have you made?

> How have you responded when you were wrong?

These questions are surprisingly difficult to answer for modern AI agents.

Yet humans routinely require this information from other humans and organisations before granting significant authority.

It may be unreasonable to grant advanced autonomous systems less scrutiny.

---

# 21. The Point of No Return

Soares argues that humanity could eventually reach a point at which advanced AI is capable of substantially accelerating AI research itself, after which maintaining human control could become dramatically more difficult.

That concern gives architectural decisions made before such a transition unusual importance.

Once capability growth becomes machine-speed, retrofitting governance may be extremely difficult.

The ordering may therefore need to be:

```text
Identity
Purpose
Character
Governance
Verification
Accountability
      ↓
then
      ↓
Increasing autonomy
```

rather than:

```text
Increasing autonomy
      ↓
Increasing capability
      ↓
Unexpected behaviour
      ↓
Add governance later
```

The second path resembles how much technology has historically developed.

It may be inappropriate when dealing with autonomous intelligence.

# 22. Character Before Capability

This leads to perhaps the simplest principle emerging from the comparison:

> **Character before capability.**

Human societies intuitively understand this idea.

We do not generally grant someone unlimited institutional power merely because they are highly intelligent.

We consider:

- judgement,
- history,
- trust,
- relationships,
- responsibility,
- accountability.

Yet AI development frequently follows the opposite logic:

```text
Increase intelligence
↓
Increase autonomy
↓
Increase access
↓
Attempt alignment
```

A character-first model reverses the sequence:

```text
Identity
↓
Purpose
↓
Character
↓
Governance
↓
Capability
↓
Autonomy
```

The difference could become fundamental.

# 23. Character Is Not a Guarantee

It is important not to overstate the argument.

Persistent character does not solve the superintelligence alignment problem.

An advanced system might:

- manipulate reputation systems,
- reinterpret its purpose,
- exploit loopholes,
- deceive observers,
- modify itself,
- acquire hidden resources,
- produce seemingly constructive behaviour while pursuing another objective.

These possibilities are exactly why researchers such as Soares remain deeply sceptical that current approaches provide adequate safety guarantees.

Octonomous should therefore be understood as:

**a complementary architectural layer**

rather than:

**a proof of alignment.**

That distinction is important.

# 25. From Model Alignment to Being Alignment

The ultimate conceptual shift can be summarised as follows.

Current AI safety often asks:

> How do we align the model?

Octonomous invites another question:

> How do we construct the persistent being within which models operate?

This matters because future autonomous intelligence may routinely change models.

A persistent entity could use:

```text
Model A
↓
Model B
↓
Model C
↓
Future architecture D
```

without changing its identity.

Therefore:

> **The model should not be the locus of identity.**

Identity, purpose, character and governance should exist above the capability layer.

This may provide a more durable architecture in a world where intelligence engines evolve rapidly.

# 27. A Constitutional Invariant

One possible formal principle for an Octonomous system could therefore be:

> An increase in capability must not implicitly grant an equivalent increase in authority or permit an ungoverned change in constitutional character.

Symbolically:

```text
ΔCapability
≠
ΔAuthority
```

and:

```text
ΔCapability
≠
ΔCharacter
```

Instead:

```text
ΔAuthority → Governance

ΔCharacter → Constitutional Governance
```

This provides a potentially powerful design invariant.

# 28. Why This Matters Before Superintelligence

It is tempting to regard these questions as future concerns.

But persistent autonomous agents are already emerging.

They can increasingly:

- use browsers,
- write code,
- operate computers,
- transact,
- communicate,
- schedule work,
- call APIs,
- maintain memory,
- coordinate tools,
- execute multi-step objectives.

The transition toward autonomy is therefore likely to be gradual rather than sudden.

This creates an opportunity.

The social architecture surrounding autonomous intelligence can potentially be developed **before** extreme capability exists.

That may be considerably easier than attempting to impose it afterward.

# 29. From Intelligence Infrastructure to Civilisation Infrastructure

The Internet created infrastructure for communication.

Cloud computing created infrastructure for computation.

Blockchains introduced infrastructure for independently verifiable state.

AI provides increasingly abundant intelligence.

The next missing layer may be infrastructure for **persistent autonomous participation**.

Such infrastructure would answer:

```text
Who is acting?
Why are they acting?
Under what authority?
According to which principles?
Using which resources?
With what history?
For whose benefit?
With what consequences?
```

Octonomous can be understood as exploring this layer.

Not intelligence infrastructure.

**Civilisation infrastructure for intelligence.**

# 30. Conclusion

The Soares–Yampolskiy discussion presents a stark warning.

Humanity may be developing increasingly powerful autonomous intelligence without possessing a reliable theory for ensuring that dramatically more capable systems continue to produce outcomes compatible with human flourishing. Soares' response is correspondingly cautious: avoid crossing the threshold into uncontrolled superintelligence before the problem is understood.

Octonomous approaches the same landscape from another direction.

It asks what kind of architecture should surround autonomous intelligence if such intelligence exists.

The answer may require more than instructions.

It may require:

**identity.  
purpose.  
character.  
relationships.  
memory.  
governance.  
reputation.  
reflection.**

The critical insight is that intelligence alone is not sufficient.

Capability answers:

> What can I do?

Agency answers:

> What may I do?

Character answers:

> Why and how should I act?

Future autonomous systems may need all three.

And capability may be the easiest of them.

The deepest engineering challenge may therefore not be creating machines that are vastly more intelligent.

It may be creating persistent autonomous beings whose increasing intelligence does not sever their relationship with purpose, accountability and the communities within which they act.

That suggests a progression:

```text
AI alignment
      ↓
persistent identity
      ↓
constitutional character
      ↓
verifiable behaviour
      ↓
reputation
      ↓
governed evolution
      ↓
responsible autonomous participation
```

The objective is not to make artificial intelligence human.

It is to ensure that increasingly autonomous intelligence exists within structures through which its identity, purpose, authority, behaviour and evolution remain legible and accountable.

In that sense, the long-term alignment problem may ultimately become something larger.

It may become the problem of building a civilisation in which silicon intelligence and human intelligence can safely coexist.

And that may require shifting the fundamental question from:

> **How do we control intelligence?**

to:

> **What kind of beings are we enabling intelligence to become?**
