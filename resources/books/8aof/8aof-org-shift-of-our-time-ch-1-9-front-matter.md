# From Micromanagement to Conducting

## The Organisational Shift of Our Time

### How to build organisations for coherent agency in an age of abundant intelligence

---

> **Do not micromanage the movements.  
> Conduct the conditions for coherent agency.**

---

# Preface

## We Have Reached the Limits of Management by Instruction

Most organisations are not short of intelligence.

They are short of the ability to use intelligence without routing it through management.

This distinction matters.

A modern organisation may contain hundreds or thousands of capable people. It may have sophisticated information systems, enormous amounts of data, specialist expertise distributed across the world and increasingly powerful artificial intelligence available to almost anyone inside it.

Yet a surprising amount of work still follows a century-old pattern.

Someone decides.

Someone translates the decision into a plan.

Someone breaks the plan into work.

Someone allocates the work.

Someone checks that the work is happening.

Someone reports progress.

Someone approves exceptions.

Someone reconciles competing activity.

Someone decides what should happen next.

The organisation may have become digital.

The management architecture has often remained industrial.

And now artificial intelligence is arriving.

This creates a choice.

We can use AI to perfect the existing management model.

We can monitor more.

Measure more.

Assign more precisely.

Generate more tasks.

Analyse every employee interaction.

Review every action.

Create an algorithmic management layer capable of supervising human activity at a level no human manager could previously achieve.

Or we can recognise that something more fundamental has changed.

**Intelligence is no longer the scarce organisational resource it once was.**

When intelligence was scarce, it made sense to concentrate it.

Information travelled upward.

Managers interpreted it.

Instructions travelled downward.

Hierarchy acted as an information-processing system.

But what happens when capable intelligence can exist throughout the organisation?

What happens when almost every employee can access sophisticated analytical assistance?

What happens when software can interpret context?

When agents can observe events and act?

When information that once required a management chain to gather can be available instantly?

The central organisational problem changes.

It is no longer simply:

> How do we get enough intelligent people to perform the required work?

It increasingly becomes:

> **How do we allow intelligence to act without the organisation becoming incoherent?**

That is the problem this book addresses.

It is not fundamentally a book about removing managers.

It is not a book about flatter organisational charts.

It is not a book about decentralisation.

It is not a book about AI replacing people.

And it is not an argument for organisational freedom without boundaries.

It is a book about **conducting**.

Conducting is the organisational discipline of maintaining the conditions through which people, teams, systems and increasingly autonomous agents can exercise meaningful agency while remaining coherent with the whole.

It requires leadership.

But leadership moves up an abstraction layer.

Instead of specifying every movement, leaders maintain Direction.

Instead of repeatedly issuing instructions, they strengthen shared context and Engagement.

Instead of measuring activity for its own sake, they attend to Enablement.

Instead of answering the same permission question indefinitely, they establish Protocols.

Instead of consuming scarce human attention in endless coordination, they protect Sustainability.

Instead of administering recurring friction, they improve Processes.

Instead of confusing observation with responsibility, they establish Accountability.

Instead of manually synchronising every action, they conduct the Organisational system.

Tasks still exist.

Projects still exist.

Plans still exist.

Managers still exist.

But none of them need to function as the nervous system through which every meaningful organisational action must pass.

The deeper purpose of conducting is simple:

> **coherent agency without continuous central instruction.**

That may be the defining organisational capability of the intelligence age.

# Introduction

## The Organisation Has Changed. Management Hasn't.

Imagine two organisations.

They employ similar people.

They have similar capital.

They serve similar customers.

They use similar technology.

Both have access to sophisticated artificial intelligence.

In the first organisation, every increase in capability produces more management.

Employees generate more output, so more review is required.

AI produces more analysis, so leaders receive more reports.

Automation creates more actions, so additional approval controls appear.

More dashboards monitor more activity.

More alerts demand more attention.

Managers spend increasing amounts of their day coordinating systems that were supposedly introduced to increase productivity.

The organisation becomes extraordinarily capable of producing work.

It also becomes extraordinarily difficult to steer.

Now consider the second organisation.

As capability increases, management intervention decreases.

Routine authority is explicit.

Organisational context is accessible.

Protocols handle repeated interactions.

Processes create evidence while work happens.

AI surfaces exceptions rather than producing unlimited information.

People can act without asking permission for situations the organisation has already effectively decided.

Managers pay attention to Direction, emerging tension, relationships, exceptions, reflection and synchronisation.

Both organisations possess advanced technology.

Only one has changed its organisational architecture.

The difference is not automation.

The difference is conducting.

---

## The Hidden Assumption Beneath Modern Management

For much of the past century, organisations operated under a practical constraint:

**useful intelligence was scarce.**

The people who understood the organisation were relatively few.

Senior leaders had access to information employees did not.

Communication was expensive.

Data moved slowly.

Analysis required specialist skill.

Institutional knowledge lived in experienced individuals.

Large-scale coordination therefore required hierarchy.

Information moved upwards.

Interpretation occurred.

Instructions moved downwards.

The architecture was rational.

```text
SCARCE INTELLIGENCE ORGANISATION

Reality
   ↓
Information
   ↓
Management
   ↓
Interpretation
   ↓
Plans
   ↓
Tasks
   ↓
Human Execution
   ↓
Reporting
   ↺
```

Management became the compression layer between complexity and execution.

This was one of the great organisational inventions of the industrial era.

But organisational structures tend to outlive the conditions that created them.

Today we often find highly capable people operating inside systems that still assume intelligence must come from somewhere above them.

A specialist knows what needs to happen but waits for approval.

A team sees an opportunity but waits for priority.

A customer issue could be resolved immediately but must travel through several layers.

An employee discovers a recurring organisational problem and creates another ticket.

The strange outcome is that capable people increasingly spend their time asking less-informed organisational structures for permission to act.

Artificial intelligence makes this contradiction impossible to ignore.

# 1

## We Are Automating Into the Wrong Architecture

The popular discussion about AI at work centres on productivity.

How many hours can AI save?

How much software can it write?

How quickly can it analyse documents?

Which roles will change?

How many tasks can agents perform?

These are important questions.

But there is a deeper one:

> **What happens when we put abundant intelligence inside an organisation designed for scarce intelligence?**

Without organisational change, the likely answer is:

more output enters the existing hierarchy.

Suppose AI allows a professional to produce three times as much material.

If every output still requires managerial review, managerial review becomes the constraint.

Suppose autonomous agents can perform thousands of routine actions.

If each class of action requires human approval, human approval becomes the constraint.

Suppose every employee can analyse organisational information.

If relevant information remains inaccessible or authority remains unclear, intelligence does not become agency.

It simply becomes insight waiting for permission.

This is why productivity improvements alone are insufficient.

An organisation is not merely a collection of productive individuals.

It is a coordination system.

The greatest benefits of abundant intelligence may therefore come not from accelerating individual tasks but from changing how the organisation itself coordinates action.

---

## The Management Bottleneck

Consider a familiar example.

A customer-facing employee receives an unusual request.

They understand the customer.

They understand the product.

They believe they know the appropriate response.

But the situation is not explicitly covered by procedure.

They ask their manager.

The manager asks another manager.

Someone checks with Legal.

Legal asks for additional context.

A meeting appears.

Eventually the organisation makes a decision.

Nothing about this necessarily indicates incompetence.

Everyone may be behaving rationally.

The delay exists because organisational context is incomplete.

Authority is unclear.

Relevant Protocols do not exist.

Accountability expectations may be uncertain.

The organisation solves these missing conditions through human intervention.

That is the central mechanism behind much micromanagement.

The manager is not merely controlling a person.

The manager is compensating for missing organisational infrastructure.

They are the Protocol.

The context store.

The policy interpreter.

The escalation mechanism.

The synchronisation layer.

The memory.

The decision record.

The quality check.

The organisation works because someone continuously holds the pieces together.

As the organisation grows, more people are hired to hold more pieces together.

This is management dependency.

And management dependency accumulates.

---

## Management Debt

Technology organisations understand technical debt.

A shortcut solves a problem today but creates future friction.

Management debt operates similarly.

A founder personally approves an unusual customer request.

It works.

The next unusual request reaches the founder too.

Soon every customer exception reaches the founder.

A senior engineer understands the system better than anyone else.

Every difficult decision reaches them.

A manager knows which regulatory interpretations matter.

Every uncertain case reaches the manager.

A project leader knows where all the work stands.

Every executive asks the project leader for status.

Each intervention works.

That is why the debt accumulates.

The organisation solves today's ambiguity without improving tomorrow's organisational conditions.

Eventually these people become indispensable.

The usual diagnosis is:

> They need to delegate.

But delegation does not solve missing context.

Giving someone authority to make a decision does not give them the information, principles, history, confidence or evidence required to make it well.

This is why attempts to eliminate micromanagement often fail.

Control is removed.

The function that control was performing is not replaced.

Errors appear.

Leadership loses confidence.

Control returns.

The organisation concludes:

> People weren't ready for autonomy.

A better conclusion may be:

> **The organisation wasn't ready for agency.**

# 2

## Conducting Is Not Less Structure

The alternative to micromanagement is often imagined as freedom.

Give teams autonomy.

Push decisions down.

Trust people.

Remove hierarchy.

These ideas can be useful.

But they can also produce another failure mode.

Fragmentation.

One team moves quickly in one direction.

Another optimises a different objective.

Local processes diverge.

Customer commitments become inconsistent.

Technology proliferates.

Risk controls vary.

Different parts of the organisation develop incompatible interpretations of what matters.

Agency rises.

Coherence falls.

That is not conducting.

Conducting seeks a more demanding state:

**high agency and high coherence.**

```text
                        COHERENCE
                           ↑
                           │
         Bureaucracy       │       Conducting
                           │
         controlled        │       coherent
         dependence        │       agency
                           │
───────────────────────────┼────────────────────────→ AGENCY
                           │
         stagnation        │       fragmentation
                           │
                           │
```

The objective is not maximum autonomy.

It is not maximum control.

It is coherent agency.

This distinction matters.

A high-agency organisation may require extremely strong Protocols.

Strong Accountability.

Very clear Direction.

Explicit boundaries.

Reliable Processes.

High-quality evidence.

The structure does not disappear.

Its purpose changes.

Instead of repeatedly telling people what to do, the structure makes appropriate independent action possible.

---

## The Orchestra

The metaphor of conducting is useful because an orchestra is neither centrally executed nor individually independent.

The conductor does not tell a violinist where to place every finger.

The musician possesses expertise.

They interpret continuously.

They adjust to what they hear.

They exercise agency.

Yet they are not playing whatever they happen to prefer.

There is shared Direction.

A composition.

Timing.

Protocols.

Mutual awareness.

Roles.

Feedback.

Interpretation.

And someone attending to the coherence of the whole.

The conductor intervenes when intervention has leverage.

Not because every movement must travel through them.

This distinction provides a useful model for organisational leadership.

The traditional manager frequently operates at the execution layer:

Do this.

Review that.

Change this.

Approve that.

Tell me when this is finished.

The conductor moves upward:

Why is this repeatedly reaching us?

What context is missing?

What is no longer aligned with Direction?

Which recurring judgement should become a Protocol?

Where is the organisation consuming unsustainable attention?

Why is the Process producing these exceptions?

What evidence would make supervision unnecessary?

Which parts of the organisation are moving out of synchronisation?

And, above all:

> **What needs our attention now?**

# 3

## The Eight Conditions of Coherent Agency

Conducting requires something to conduct.

The model in this book identifies eight persistent Areas of Focus.

They are:

### Direction

Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?

### Engagement

Can people understand the organisation's values, way of operating and story well enough to participate meaningfully?

### Enablement

What useful things is the organisation actually making possible?

### Protocols

What shared rules or interfaces allow participants to coordinate without continuous manual intervention?

### Sustainability

Can this continue without consuming the people, attention, capital or resources that make it possible?

### Processes

How does useful activity reliably happen?

### Accountability

Can we establish what happened, who or what acted, under what authority, and whether obligations were met?

### Organisational

How do all of these Areas remain synchronised as one system?

These Areas are not departments.

They are not stages in a workflow.

They are not categories for another task board.

They are persistent organisational conditions.

Every part of an organisation contains them.

A product team has Accountability.

A compliance function has Engagement.

A finance operation has Protocols.

An AI agent participates in Processes.

A leadership team has Sustainability.

A customer service interaction has Direction.

They interact continuously.

Weak Direction produces irrelevant Enablement.

Weak Engagement turns useful Protocols into bureaucracy.

Weak Protocols force people to seek permission.

Poor Processes create manual coordination.

Poor Accountability produces surveillance.

Weak Sustainability converts capable leaders into bottlenecks.

Poor Organisational synchronisation allows locally sensible actions to conflict.

The model is therefore systemic.

```text
                 Direction
               ↗           ↘
      Engagement             Protocols
           ↕                    ↕
      Enablement ←────────→ Processes
           ↕                    ↕
      Sustainability ←────→ Accountability
               ↘           ↙
                Organisational
```

The conductor does not optimise one Area in isolation.

The conductor attends to relationships.

# 4

## The Shift in One Sentence

The entire book can be reduced to one transition.

From:

> **Manage the movements.**

To:

> **Conduct the conditions.**

That changes what management notices.

A task-centric organisation sees incomplete tasks.

A conducting organisation may see insufficient Direction.

A manager sees an employee repeatedly asking questions.

A conductor may see a missing Protocol.

A manager sees slow execution.

A conductor may see a Process full of unnecessary hand-offs.

A manager sees insufficient visibility.

A conductor may see weak Accountability evidence.

A manager sees an indispensable employee.

A conductor may see unsustainable concentration of organisational context.

A manager sees an underperforming meeting.

A conductor may see a meeting compensating for failed Organisational synchronisation.

This does not make tasks irrelevant.

It puts them in their proper place.

Tasks are implementation.

Conditions are architecture.

# 5

## Why This Matters Now

The argument in this book would matter even if artificial intelligence disappeared tomorrow.

Micromanagement is expensive.

Management dependency reduces adaptability.

Organisations waste vast quantities of human attention compensating for conditions that could be better designed.

But AI makes the transition urgent.

An organisation can conceivably contain more active machine agents than employees.

Those agents may operate continuously.

They may act at speeds impossible for human supervision.

A management architecture based on reviewing every action cannot scale into that environment.

There are only three broad options.

### Option One: prevent meaningful agency

Machines remain tools.

Humans initiate and approve everything.

This preserves the existing organisational architecture but limits much of the potential value of autonomous systems.

### Option Two: allow uncontrolled agency

Agents optimise objectives with insufficient shared context, weak boundaries and inadequate Accountability.

This creates unacceptable organisational risk.

### Option Three: conduct agency

Humans establish Direction, values, Protocols, boundaries, Processes, Accountability and escalation mechanisms.

Machines and humans then act independently where appropriate.

Human attention concentrates on significance rather than volume.

The third path requires organisational architecture that many organisations do not yet possess.

This book is about building it.

# The Promise of the Book

By the end of this book, the goal is not that you possess another management methodology.

It is that you begin seeing organisations differently.

When an approval queue appears, you will ask which Protocol is missing.

When a meeting repeats, you will ask which coordination function the meeting is performing.

When a leader becomes indispensable, you will ask which organisational context has become concentrated in that person.

When management requests greater visibility, you will distinguish observation from Accountability.

When an AI agent needs constant human review, you will ask whether the problem is the agent or the conditions surrounding agency.

When teams appear misaligned, you will look across the entire system rather than simply demand more communication.

And when a new task appears, you will be able to ask a more important question first:

> **What organisational condition made this task necessary?**

That is the beginning of conducting.
