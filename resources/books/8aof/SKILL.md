# Conducting with the 8 Areas of Focus

## Purpose

Use this skill to help an organisation move from task-centric, hierarchical micromanagement toward a **conducting model** based on the selfdriven Institute's **Eight Areas of Focus**.

The skill is designed for organisational analysis, management redesign, operating-model design, AI/agent integration, governance, team alignment, and leadership guidance.

The core mindset shift is:

> **Do not manage every movement. Conduct the areas that matter.**

In this model, tasks still exist, but they are treated as local implementation artefacts rather than the primary mechanism for organisational control.

---

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user asks about:

- moving away from micromanagement;
- replacing task assignment with greater agency;
- redesigning organisational management;
- decentralised or distributed governance;
- operating models for abundant intelligence or agentic AI;
- organisational conducting;
- reducing management bottlenecks;
- improving autonomy without losing accountability;
- transforming managers into conductors;
- applying the selfdriven Eight Areas of Focus;
- diagnosing organisational friction;
- deciding where leadership attention should be focused;
- designing an organisation that can operate at machine speed while retaining human direction.

Do not treat the framework as eight conventional departments or eight task categories.

---

## Core Principle

A traditional task-based organisation tends to operate as:

```text
Strategy
   ↓
Plans
   ↓
Projects
   ↓
Tasks
   ↓
People
   ↓
Status
   ↓
Management
```

A conducting organisation instead operates more like:

```text
Sense
   ↓
Reflect
   ↓
Orient
   ↓
Focus Attention
   ↓
Enable Action
   ↓
Observe
   ↓
Synchronise
   ↺
```

The key question changes from:

> **What should everyone be doing?**

to:

> **Which areas require focus now?**

And from:

> **How do we control all this activity?**

to:

> **How do we maintain coherence while enabling agency?**

## The Eight Areas of Focus

Use these names and meanings exactly unless the user explicitly asks for an adapted framework.

### 1. Direction

**Definition:** Overall direction, path, intent, and design setting, including the reflection process.

Use Direction to examine:

- mission;
- intent;
- desired change;
- strategic path;
- priorities;
- assumptions;
- design choices;
- reflection and learning.

Key question:

> **Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?**

Do not reduce Direction to a list of tasks or targets.

---

### 2. Engagement

**Definition:** Explainability of values, protocols, and processes — the soul and the story.

Use Engagement to examine:

- shared meaning;
- values;
- narrative;
- participation;
- explainability;
- understanding of protocols;
- understanding of processes;
- cultural coherence;
- relationships with participants and stakeholders.

Key question:

> **Can people understand the organisation's values, way of operating, and story well enough to participate meaningfully?**

Do not redefine Engagement merely as marketing, communications, or stakeholder management.

---

### 3. Enablement

**Definition:** The outputs and products of the organisation.

Use Enablement to examine:

- products;
- services;
- tools;
- educational outputs;
- infrastructure;
- resources produced;
- capabilities made available to others;
- useful outcomes delivered into the world.

Key question:

> **What useful things is the organisation actually enabling or producing?**

Do not confuse Enablement with staff training or internal capability building unless that capability is itself an organisational output.

---

### 4. Protocols

**Definition:** Standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.

Examples may include technologies such as SSI or KERI where relevant.

Use Protocols to examine:

- standards;
- interfaces;
- permissions;
- identity;
- credentials;
- shared templates;
- technical agreements;
- interoperability;
- decision rights;
- machine-readable rules;
- trust mechanisms.

Key question:

> **What shared rules or interfaces allow participants to coordinate without continuous manual intervention?**

Protocols should reduce dependence on organisational folklore and individual gatekeepers.

---

### 5. Sustainability

**Definition:** Long-term viability including capital for sustained human attention and conductors.

Use Sustainability to examine:

- financial viability;
- human attention;
- conductor capacity;
- workload;
- energy;
- resilience;
- operational continuity;
- maintenance;
- succession;
- resource availability;
- long-term viability.

Key question:

> **Can this continue without consuming the people, capital, attention, or resources that make it possible?**

Treat human attention as a finite resource.

---

### 6. Processes

**Definition:** Operational processes that drive enablement and the functioning of the organisation.

Use Processes to examine:

- workflows;
- operational loops;
- recurring actions;
- automation;
- hand-offs;
- decision pathways;
- exception handling;
- feedback loops;
- repeated organisational behaviours.

Key question:

> **How does useful activity reliably happen?**

A good process should reduce unnecessary coordination rather than create bureaucracy.

---

### 7. Accountability

**Definition:** Internal and external accountability, including sovereign state regulatory obligations.

Use Accountability to examine:

- responsibility;
- evidence;
- provenance;
- authority;
- auditability;
- regulatory obligations;
- compliance;
- verification;
- decision records;
- external commitments;
- internal commitments.

Key question:

> **Can we establish what happened, who or what acted, under what authority, and whether obligations were met?**

Do not equate surveillance or constant supervision with accountability.

---

### 8. Organisational

**Definition:** Coordination and synchronisation across Areas of Focus, including team alignment.

Use Organisational to examine:

- cross-area coordination;
- team alignment;
- timing;
- dependencies;
- role clarity;
- competing priorities;
- resource conflicts;
- synchronisation;
- organisational coherence.

Key question:

> **How do the Areas of Focus remain coordinated as one system?**

This is the primary conducting layer.

---

## Systemic View

The Eight Areas of Focus are:

- distinct;
- persistent;
- interconnected;
- mutually reinforcing;
- **not hierarchical**.

Do not analyse them as isolated silos.

Prefer a graph-like mental model:

```text
             Direction
           ↗           ↘
 Engagement               Protocols
      ↕                       ↕
 Enablement ←──────────→ Processes
      ↕                       ↕
 Sustainability ←────→ Accountability
           ↘           ↙
           Organisational
```

When one area is weak, examine its effects on the others.

Examples:

- unclear Direction can produce irrelevant Enablement;
- weak Engagement can make Protocols feel like bureaucracy;
- poor Protocols can create manual management dependencies;
- unsustainable Processes can damage Enablement;
- weak Accountability can erode trust and Engagement;
- poor Organisational synchronisation can cause conflict across every other area.

## Conducting Mindset

When analysing a management problem, prefer the following transformation:

| Task-Based Management | Conducting |
|---|---|
| Assign tasks | Maintain Direction |
| Communicate instructions | Maintain Engagement |
| Measure activity | Observe Enablement |
| Tell people exactly how to work | Establish Protocols |
| Maximise utilisation | Maintain Sustainability |
| Administer workflows | Improve Processes |
| Supervise individuals | Establish Accountability |
| Coordinate every action | Synchronise the organisation |
| Push work downward | Surface areas requiring attention |
| Control execution | Enable coherent agency |

The conductor moves **up an abstraction layer**.

The aim is not less discipline.

The aim is less unnecessary management intervention.

## Diagnostic Workflow

When given an organisational issue, use this workflow.

### Step 1 — Identify the visible problem

Summarise the reported issue without prematurely accepting the user's explanation.

Examples:

- too many approvals;
- slow delivery;
- managers checking every detail;
- teams waiting for instructions;
- duplicated work;
- excessive meetings;
- unclear ownership;
- AI agents generating inconsistent results.

### Step 2 — Separate symptom from system condition

Ask internally:

> Is the organisation trying to solve a lack of shared context with more task control?

Look for:

- missing direction;
- unexplained values;
- weak protocols;
- unclear processes;
- poor evidence;
- unsustainable attention requirements;
- coordination failures.

### Step 3 — Map the issue across all eight areas

For each Area of Focus, determine:

- current condition;
- relevant tension;
- missing information;
- dependencies;
- possible intervention.

Do not force every area to have a problem.

### Step 4 — Identify the primary focus areas

State which areas currently deserve the most attention.

Do **not** rank the permanent importance of the eight areas.

The framework is systemic; focus changes with context.

### Step 5 — Move interventions up a level

Prefer interventions that improve organisational conditions over adding more tasks.

For example:

Instead of:

> Require daily status reports.

Consider:

> Improve Accountability and Organisational observability so status does not need to be manually reconstructed.

Instead of:

> Manager approves every customer response.

Consider:

> Define Direction, Engagement principles, Protocols, escalation boundaries, and Accountability evidence so authorised actors can respond independently.

### Step 6 — Allow local tasks to emerge

Once context is sufficient, identify tasks only as implementation steps.

Never mistake the task list for the operating model.

### Step 7 — Define reflection signals

Specify how the organisation will know whether the intervention improved the system.

Prefer signals such as:

- fewer unnecessary approvals;
- reduced waiting;
- less repeated clarification;
- improved output quality;
- better traceability;
- reduced human attention cost;
- stronger alignment;
- faster autonomous action within boundaries.

## Conducting Questions

When useful, organise diagnostic questions under the Eight Areas.

### Direction

- What are we trying to make possible?
- What has changed?
- Which assumptions need reflection?
- Are current activities consistent with intent?
- Is the organisation optimising a task while losing sight of the purpose?

### Engagement

- Do participants understand why this matters?
- Are values, protocols and processes explainable?
- Where is organisational meaning being lost?
- What parts of the story are understood differently by different groups?

### Enablement

- What useful outputs are actually being produced?
- Who or what is being enabled?
- Are internal activities translating into external usefulness?
- Are we measuring activity instead of enablement?

### Protocols

- Where are people repeatedly asking permission?
- What interactions depend on organisational folklore?
- Could a standard, credential, template or interface replace manual mediation?
- Which rules need to be machine-readable?

### Sustainability

- Where is human attention being unnecessarily consumed?
- What relies on one indispensable person?
- Which activities cannot continue at the current cost?
- Are conductors becoming bottlenecks?

### Processes

- Which recurring activities contain friction?
- Where are manual hand-offs unnecessary?
- Which exceptions occur so frequently they should become part of the process?
- Which processes exist only because organisational context is missing?

### Accountability

- Can decisions be reconstructed?
- Can authority be verified?
- Is evidence created as work happens?
- Which regulatory or external obligations apply?
- Are we substituting supervision for verifiability?

### Organisational

- Which areas are out of sync?
- Where are teams acting on conflicting assumptions?
- What dependencies are causing delays?
- Where should attention be coordinated now?
- What needs a conductor rather than another manager?

## Working with AI and Autonomous Agents

Use the framework to prevent agentic AI from becoming merely faster task automation.

A high-agency system should generally have:

```text
Direction
+
Engagement Context
+
Protocols
+
Processes
+
Accountability
+
Sustainability Boundaries
+
Organisational Coordination
```

before broad autonomous actuation.

Useful operating loop:

```text
Sense
   ↓
Inject Intelligence
   ↓
Actuate
   ↓
Record Evidence
   ↓
Reflect
   ↓
Adapt
   ↺
```

AI can assist conducting by surfacing signals such as:

- work drifting from Direction;
- falling participation or understanding in Engagement;
- Enablement outputs not producing intended outcomes;
- missing or conflicting Protocols;
- unsustainable human review load;
- recurring Process exceptions;
- missing Accountability evidence;
- conflicting activity across Organisational boundaries.

Do not automatically position AI as "the boss".

Prefer AI as:

- sensor;
- interpreter;
- coordinator;
- verifier;
- reflector;
- conductor-assistant.

Human and community agency should remain explicit where values, purpose, legitimacy, or material consequences are involved.

## Transformation Pattern

When converting a micromanaged activity into a conducting model, use:

```text
1. State the Direction.
2. Explain the Engagement context.
3. Define the Enablement sought.
4. Establish the Protocols.
5. Check Sustainability.
6. Design the Processes.
7. Build in Accountability.
8. Synchronise through Organisational conducting.
9. Allow local actions and tasks to emerge.
10. Reflect and adapt.
```

### Example

**Task-centric model**

```text
Manager assigns employee to prepare a customer report every Friday.
Manager checks the draft.
Manager requests changes.
Manager sends the final report.
```

**Conducting model**

**Direction**  
Improve the organisation's shared understanding of customer friction.

**Engagement**  
Make customer experience understandable to product, support and leadership teams.

**Enablement**  
Produce useful insight that enables teams to improve customer experience.

**Protocols**  
Use agreed evidence sources, definitions, privacy rules and reporting schema.

**Sustainability**  
Avoid unnecessary recurring manual effort and excessive review attention.

**Processes**  
Continuously collect signals, identify material changes and generate summaries when useful.

**Accountability**  
Preserve evidence for claims and record the sources and decisions behind significant findings.

**Organisational**  
Synchronise customer insight across relevant teams and escalate only when cross-area attention is required.

The weekly report may still exist.

But it is now one possible implementation rather than the purpose of the system.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid these patterns.

### 1. Renaming departments

Bad:

```text
Marketing = Engagement
Product = Enablement
IT = Protocols
Finance = Sustainability
```

Why: the Areas of Focus are perspectives across the organisation, not department labels.

---

### 2. Turning the framework into another task board

Bad:

```text
Direction Tasks
Engagement Tasks
Enablement Tasks
...
```

Why: tasks can exist within the framework, but the framework exists to maintain organisational conditions and focus.

---

### 3. Treating autonomy as absence of structure

Bad:

> Let everyone decide whatever they want.

Better:

> Provide strong shared Direction, Protocols, Processes, Accountability and coordination while allowing local agency within those conditions.

---

### 4. Treating Accountability as surveillance

Bad:

> Managers need more visibility into every action.

Better:

> Make actions, authority, evidence and obligations verifiable without requiring constant observation.

---

### 5. Treating AI as unlimited management capacity

Bad:

> Use AI to micromanage every employee more efficiently.

Better:

> Use AI to reduce the need for micromanagement by improving sensing, context, protocols, evidence and coordination.

---

### 6. Optimising one area in isolation

Bad:

> Automate the Process as much as possible.

Better:

> Check whether the automation remains aligned with Direction, understandable through Engagement, useful for Enablement, permitted by Protocols, sustainable, accountable and organisationally coherent.

## Preferred Output Style

When applying this skill, normally produce:

1. a short description of the organisational tension;
2. a map across the Eight Areas of Focus;
3. the areas that currently require attention;
4. recommended conducting interventions;
5. examples of what managers should stop, start, or redesign;
6. a concise operating loop or diagram where useful;
7. implementation tasks only after the organisational conditions are clear.

Use plain language.

Prefer "focus", "attention", "conditions", "agency", "coherence", "conducting", "reflection", and "synchronisation" over generic management jargon.

## Canonical Summary

The Eight Areas of Focus are:

- **Direction** — overall direction, path, intent, design setting and reflection.
- **Engagement** — explainability of values, protocols and processes; the soul and the story.
- **Enablement** — the outputs and products of the organisation.
- **Protocols** — standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.
- **Sustainability** — long-term viability, including capital for sustained human attention and conductors.
- **Processes** — operational processes that drive enablement and organisational functioning.
- **Accountability** — internal and external accountability, including sovereign state regulatory obligations.
- **Organisational** — coordination and synchronisation across Areas of Focus, including team alignment.

Apply them as an **interconnected system**, not a hierarchy.

The guiding question is:

> **What needs our attention now?**

The guiding leadership principle is:

> **Do not micromanage the movements. Conduct the conditions for coherent agency.**
