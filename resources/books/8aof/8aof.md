# From Micromanagement to Conducting

## The Organisational Shift of Our Time

### A book about coherent agency in an age of abundant intelligence

---

## Preface

For more than a century, organisations have been designed around a simple assumption:

**Useful intelligence is scarce.**

The people who know what should happen sit somewhere near the top. Information travels upward. Decisions are made. Instructions travel downward. Work is divided into tasks. Managers monitor execution. Employees report progress. Problems move back up the hierarchy.

This model built factories, governments, banks, universities, hospitals and multinational corporations.

It worked remarkably well.

But the assumption beneath it is changing.

Intelligence is becoming abundant.

Information can be searched instantly. Software can observe systems continuously. Artificial intelligence can analyse documents, generate options, draft plans, write software, interpret evidence and increasingly act through autonomous agents.

A person at the edge of an organisation may soon have access to more analytical capability than an entire management department possessed a generation ago.

Yet many organisations are responding to this extraordinary increase in capability by adding more management.

More dashboards.

More reporting.

More project management.

More meetings.

More approval steps.

More performance metrics.

More task tracking.

More surveillance.

The result is a strange contradiction.

We are entering an era of unprecedented intelligence while maintaining organisational structures designed around intelligence scarcity.

The alternative is not chaos.

It is not eliminating management.

It is not telling everyone to do whatever they want.

The alternative is **conducting**.

Conducting means moving leadership up an abstraction layer.

Instead of attempting to prescribe every action, the organisation maintains the conditions within which coherent action can occur.

Instead of asking:

**What should everyone be doing?**

we ask:

**What requires our attention now?**

Instead of asking:

**How do we control all this activity?**

we ask:

**How do we maintain coherence while enabling agency?**

Tasks remain.

Projects remain.

Plans remain.

Leadership remains.

But they occupy a different place in the system.

They become implementation mechanisms rather than the operating system of the organisation.

The organisation itself is conducted through eight persistent Areas of Focus:

**Direction.  
Engagement.  
Enablement.  
Protocols.  
Sustainability.  
Processes.  
Accountability.  
Organisational.**

These areas are not departments.

They are not boxes on an organisational chart.

They are not another project-management taxonomy.

They are the conditions that allow people, teams, systems and increasingly autonomous agents to act with agency while remaining part of a coherent whole.

This book explores that transition.

It is a transition from management of movements to stewardship of conditions.

From instruction to context.

From supervision to verifiability.

From delegation to agency.

From organisational hierarchy toward organisational coherence.

From micromanagement to conducting.

# Part I — The Organisation We Inherited

# Chapter 1 — The Task Machine

Modern organisations are extraordinarily good at turning intention into tasks.

A strategy is created.

The strategy becomes a plan.

The plan becomes projects.

Projects become workstreams.

Workstreams become tasks.

Tasks are assigned to people.

People complete the tasks.

Managers collect status information.

Status information is aggregated.

Leadership reviews progress.

The loop begins again.

The model looks approximately like this:

```text
Strategy
   ↓
Plans
   ↓
Projects
   ↓
Tasks
   ↓
People
   ↓
Status
   ↓
Management
   ↺
```

There is nothing inherently wrong with any element of this system.

Tasks are useful.

Plans are useful.

Projects are useful.

Reporting can be useful.

The difficulty arises when these mechanisms become the primary means through which an organisation maintains coherence.

At that point, the organisation becomes dependent on continuous instruction.

Someone must define the work.

Someone must break it down.

Someone must distribute it.

Someone must monitor it.

Someone must decide when it is complete.

Someone must resolve conflicts.

Someone must interpret exceptions.

Someone must approve changes.

Someone must reconstruct what happened.

Management becomes the nervous system of the organisation.

The organisation may appear decentralised because thousands of people are performing work.

Yet cognitively it remains highly centralised.

People execute locally.

Meaning is maintained centrally.

This structure creates what might be called **management dependency**.

When context is missing, another instruction is required.

When authority is unclear, another approval is required.

When evidence is weak, another status report is required.

When processes are ambiguous, another meeting is required.

When priorities conflict, another manager must intervene.

The organisation compensates for weak shared conditions with increased human coordination.

This is the hidden mechanism beneath much micromanagement.

Micromanagement is rarely created simply because managers enjoy controlling people.

More often, the system itself lacks sufficient context, protocols, accountability, clarity or trust to function without intervention.

The manager becomes the patch.

And because the patch works, the underlying organisational problem remains hidden.

# Chapter 2 — Why Micromanagement Emerges

Micromanagement is usually described as a personality problem.

The controlling manager.

The insecure executive.

The perfectionist founder.

The supervisor who cannot let go.

These people certainly exist.

But treating micromanagement primarily as individual behaviour misses something important.

Micromanagement is often a rational response to an organisation that cannot otherwise maintain coherence.

Consider a manager who insists on reviewing every customer email.

The obvious diagnosis is lack of trust.

But look beneath the behaviour.

Perhaps the organisation has never clearly articulated its values.

Perhaps customer promises are inconsistent.

Perhaps legal boundaries are poorly understood.

Perhaps escalation rules exist only in people's heads.

Perhaps previous mistakes created significant consequences.

Perhaps there is no reliable record of what was communicated.

Perhaps people do not know how much authority they actually possess.

The manager therefore becomes the protocol.

The manager becomes the quality system.

The manager becomes the memory.

The manager becomes the accountability layer.

The manager becomes the exception handler.

Removing the manager's approval without changing these conditions does not create autonomy.

It creates unmanaged risk.

This gives us an important principle:

> **You cannot sustainably remove micromanagement without replacing the organisational functions that micromanagement was performing.**

The answer is therefore not merely:

**Trust people more.**

Trust matters, but trust alone is not an operating model.

People require context.

They require boundaries.

They require understandable values.

They require access to the information needed to act.

They require protocols.

They require evidence.

They require processes capable of handling normal situations and paths for handling abnormal ones.

They require confidence that others are operating from compatible assumptions.

Agency requires conditions.

The goal is therefore not to remove structure.

The goal is to build structure that does not require continuous intervention.

# Chapter 3 — The Scarcity Assumption

Most modern organisations were built during an era in which information processing was expensive.

Senior people possessed information others did not.

Documents travelled slowly.

Communication was expensive.

Data was difficult to collect.

Analysis required specialised expertise.

Coordination across large distances was hard.

Institutional memory lived largely in people.

Management therefore performed an essential compression function.

Large amounts of organisational complexity moved upward through layers of management.

Managers interpreted the complexity.

Simplified instructions moved downward.

```text
Complex reality
      ↓
Management interpretation
      ↓
Simplified instructions
      ↓
Execution
```

Hierarchy was not merely about authority.

It was an information-processing architecture.

But information-processing economics have changed dramatically.

Knowledge can be distributed almost instantly.

Processes can be made machine-readable.

Evidence can be generated automatically.

Organisational history can be searchable.

Systems can continuously observe operations.

AI can help interpret enormous amounts of context.

Agents can increasingly perform actions directly.

The organisation is no longer constrained only by access to intelligence.

A new constraint appears:

**coherence.**

If hundreds of humans and thousands of software agents can act intelligently, how do their actions remain aligned?

How do they share direction?

How do they know what authority they possess?

How do they interact?

How do we know what happened?

How do we prevent locally intelligent actions from producing globally incoherent outcomes?

These are not primarily task-management questions.

They are conducting questions.

# Part II — The Conducting Organisation

# Chapter 4 — Moving Up an Abstraction Layer

Imagine an orchestra.

A conductor does not tell the violinist where to place every finger.

The conductor does not operate the percussion instruments.

The conductor does not breathe for the woodwind section.

The conductor does not individually execute the music.

The musicians possess their own capability.

They know their instruments.

They interpret local conditions.

They make constant micro-adjustments.

Yet this autonomy does not produce chaos.

There is a score.

There is timing.

There is shared interpretation.

There are protocols.

There is listening.

There is mutual awareness.

There is a conductor paying attention to the system as a whole.

This metaphor has limits, but it reveals the organisational transition.

The traditional manager often operates at the same level as the activity:

> Do this task.

> Change this sentence.

> Call this customer.

> Complete this report by Friday.

> Use this template.

> Send this to me before it goes out.

The conductor moves upward:

> What are we trying to achieve?

> What conditions allow people to make these decisions themselves?

> What information is missing?

> Where are activities becoming incoherent?

> Which protocol would remove repeated coordination?

> Where is attention currently needed?

> Which exceptions reveal a systemic problem?

> Are we still moving in the intended direction?

This is not less leadership.

It is a different kind of leadership.

The central shift can be expressed simply:

| Task-based management | Conducting |
|---|---|
| Assign tasks | Maintain Direction |
| Communicate instructions | Maintain Engagement |
| Measure activity | Observe Enablement |
| Tell people exactly how to work | Establish Protocols |
| Maximise utilisation | Maintain Sustainability |
| Administer workflows | Improve Processes |
| Supervise individuals | Establish Accountability |
| Coordinate every action | Synchronise the organisation |
| Push work downward | Surface areas requiring attention |
| Control execution | Enable coherent agency |

The purpose is not less discipline.

It is less unnecessary management intervention.

# Chapter 5 — The Eight Areas of Focus

A conducting organisation can be understood through eight persistent Areas of Focus.

They are:

1. Direction
2. Engagement
3. Enablement
4. Protocols
5. Sustainability
6. Processes
7. Accountability
8. Organisational

These names and meanings matter. Their canonical definitions establish a system intended to support conducting rather than recreate conventional departmental management.

They are not hierarchical.

They do not represent stages.

They are not owned exclusively by individual departments.

They are interconnected perspectives through which organisational conditions can be observed and improved.

A useful mental model is:

```text
             Direction
           ↗           ↘
 Engagement               Protocols
      ↕                       ↕
 Enablement ←──────────→ Processes
      ↕                       ↕
 Sustainability ←────→ Accountability
           ↘           ↙
           Organisational
```

Let us consider each one.

---

## Direction

**Direction is the organisation's overall direction, path, intent and design setting, including reflection.**

Direction asks:

> Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?

Traditional organisations frequently translate direction into targets too quickly.

Revenue target.

Product target.

Project milestone.

Headcount target.

Deadline.

These may be useful manifestations of direction, but they are not direction itself.

Direction includes intent.

Why does this organisation exist?

What change are we trying to enable?

What do we currently believe?

What assumptions are we making?

What have we learned?

Has the environment changed?

Should our path change?

Conducting requires direction to remain alive.

Direction is therefore not simply a strategic plan written once each year.

It is an ongoing reflective capability.

---

## Engagement

**Engagement is the explainability of values, protocols and processes — the soul and the story.**

People cannot act coherently around things they do not understand.

An organisation may have policies, values and processes while still lacking engagement.

A forty-page policy stored on an intranet is not necessarily understood.

A values poster does not guarantee shared values.

A workflow diagram does not guarantee that participants understand why the workflow exists.

Engagement concerns shared meaning.

Can people understand the organisation well enough to participate intelligently?

Can customers?

Can partners?

Can regulators?

Can communities?

Increasingly, we must ask another question:

Can machines understand enough of the organisation's operating context to assist without distorting its intent?

Engagement becomes the connective tissue between formal organisational structures and lived participation.

---

## Enablement

**Enablement concerns the outputs and products of the organisation.**

This sounds obvious, but organisations frequently confuse internal activity with useful output.

Meetings happen.

Documents are written.

Tickets are completed.

Projects progress.

Dashboards turn green.

But what has actually become possible because the organisation exists?

What product exists?

What service exists?

What capability has been created?

What educational resource became available?

What infrastructure now supports others?

Who has been enabled?

Enablement brings the organisation back into contact with usefulness.

A conducting organisation repeatedly asks whether its internal activity is translating into meaningful external outcomes.

---

## Protocols

**Protocols are standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.**

Protocols are one of the most powerful alternatives to micromanagement.

Where repeated human mediation is required, ask whether a protocol could make the interaction independently navigable.

A protocol might define:

- authority;
- permissions;
- identity;
- credentials;
- interfaces;
- data formats;
- templates;
- escalation rules;
- technical standards;
- decision rights;
- trust mechanisms.

Good protocols allow participants to coordinate without repeatedly asking a central authority what to do.

Think about the internet.

Two computers do not phone a manager every time they communicate.

They operate through shared protocols.

Organisations can move in the same direction.

The stronger the shared protocols, the less organisational folklore is required.

---

## Sustainability

**Sustainability concerns long-term viability, including the capital required for sustained human attention and conducting.**

Organisations traditionally think of sustainability primarily in financial terms.

Can we afford this?

That remains important.

But conducting introduces another scarce resource:

**human attention.**

An operation that requires a senior person to review 500 decisions every week is not sustainable simply because the person's salary is affordable.

Attention has capacity limits.

So does emotional energy.

So does organisational complexity.

A conducting system asks:

Can this continue?

Does it depend on one irreplaceable person?

Are conductors becoming bottlenecks?

Does the process consume more attention than the value it creates?

Can the system survive growth?

Can it survive crisis?

Can it survive leadership change?

Sustainability forces us to consider the carrying capacity of the organisation.

---

## Processes

**Processes are the operational mechanisms that drive enablement and organisational functioning.**

Processes answer:

> How does useful activity reliably happen?

A good process makes repeated action easier.

A bad process creates bureaucracy.

The distinction is important.

Processes should reduce coordination cost.

If a process requires six meetings, three spreadsheets, four approvals and continuous chasing, it may technically be defined but organisationally dysfunctional.

Processes should increasingly encode routine coordination.

The more predictable the situation, the less it should require ad hoc human intervention.

Humans can then concentrate their attention on uncertainty, exceptions, judgement, creativity and reflection.

---

## Accountability

**Accountability concerns internal and external accountability, including sovereign-state regulatory obligations.**

Micromanagement frequently disguises itself as accountability.

It is not the same thing.

Watching someone constantly does not necessarily make an organisation accountable.

Accountability asks different questions:

What happened?

Who or what acted?

Under what authority?

What information informed the decision?

What evidence exists?

Were obligations met?

Can the event be reconstructed?

Can claims be verified?

In a mature organisation, evidence is created as work occurs.

Accountability is therefore not reconstructed months later from scattered emails, memories and spreadsheets.

It becomes a property of the operating system.

This distinction becomes critical as autonomous systems participate in consequential decisions.

---

## Organisational

**Organisational is coordination and synchronisation across all Areas of Focus, including team alignment.**

This is the primary conducting layer.

It watches the relationships.

Direction may be clear, but Processes may not support it.

Protocols may be excellent, but Engagement may be poor.

Enablement may be strong, but Sustainability may be deteriorating.

Two teams may each be behaving rationally while operating from incompatible assumptions.

Organisational conducting asks:

What is out of sync?

Where are dependencies creating friction?

Which areas require attention?

What must be synchronised?

Where is coordination necessary?

This is where leadership shifts from managing activities to maintaining coherence across the whole.

# Part III — From Delegation to Agency

# Chapter 6 — Delegation Is Not Agency

Traditional management frequently celebrates delegation.

A manager delegates responsibility.

An employee receives the task.

Authority moves downward.

At first glance, this seems like autonomy.

But delegation still begins with a central actor.

Someone decides that action should happen.

Someone defines the scope.

Someone gives permission.

Someone may revoke it.

Agency is different.

Agency exists when an actor can perceive context, interpret conditions and initiate appropriate action within understood boundaries.

The distinction is subtle but profound.

Delegation says:

> I have decided what should happen, and I am allowing you to do it.

Agency says:

> We share sufficient direction, context, protocols and accountability for you to recognise what needs doing and act appropriately.

The second model scales far further.

It also feels very different to participate in.

People stop waiting to be assigned useful work.

They begin recognising opportunities to contribute.

The organisation moves from a queue of assigned activities toward a field of coherent initiative.

This is not unrestricted freedom.

Strong agency usually requires stronger shared context.

The paradox of high-agency organisations is that they may have fewer detailed instructions while possessing clearer principles, protocols and accountability.

Less instruction.

More clarity.

---

# Chapter 7 — Accountability Without Surveillance

One of the strongest arguments for micromanagement is risk.

What if people make mistakes?

What if people act outside their authority?

What if commitments are missed?

What if regulation is breached?

What if an AI agent does something unexpected?

These are legitimate concerns.

But surveillance is a weak substitute for accountability.

Imagine two organisational models.

In the first, the manager watches everything.

Emails are reviewed.

Meetings are attended.

Dashboards are monitored.

Employees prepare regular updates.

Decisions are verbally discussed.

When something goes wrong six months later, everyone searches through emails trying to reconstruct events.

The organisation was heavily supervised.

Yet accountability is poor.

In the second model, authorised actions create durable evidence.

Authority is explicit.

Material decisions have provenance.

Relevant policies are linked.

Inputs are recorded.

Exceptions are surfaced.

Obligations are machine-readable where possible.

The manager does not observe every action.

Yet events can be reliably reconstructed.

Which organisation is actually more accountable?

The second.

A useful formula is:

```text
Accountability
≠
Continuous Observation
```

Instead:

```text
Accountability
=
Authority
+
Evidence
+
Provenance
+
Obligations
+
Verifiability
```

This becomes increasingly important as organisations operate at machine speed.

No human management structure can manually supervise every micro-action generated by thousands of autonomous processes.

Evidence must increasingly be generated by the system itself.

Accountability must be architectural.

# Chapter 8 — Protocols Replace Permission

Consider a common organisational interaction.

An employee asks:

> Can I approve this?

The manager responds.

A few days later someone asks a similar question.

The manager responds again.

Another team encounters the same ambiguity.

A meeting is organised.

This pattern may continue for years.

The organisation believes it has a people problem.

In reality it may have a protocol problem.

Repeated permission-seeking is often a signal.

It means decision boundaries are unclear.

A mature conducting organisation notices the repetition and moves upward an abstraction layer.

Instead of answering the tenth approval question, it asks:

Can the decision rule itself be clarified?

Could authority be expressed through roles?

Could credentials establish permission?

Could a template encode requirements?

Could an interface enforce boundaries?

Could an escalation protocol handle unusual cases?

Could a machine-readable rule make the normal case automatic?

The objective is not to eliminate judgement.

It is to stop spending judgement on questions that the organisation has already effectively answered.

Protocols transform institutional knowledge into reusable coordination infrastructure.

Every effective protocol removes a small piece of unnecessary management.

At scale, the effect is profound.

# Part IV — Conducting Abundant Intelligence

# Chapter 9 — The Arrival of the Agentic Organisation

Artificial intelligence first entered most organisations as a tool.

People asked questions.

AI produced responses.

Then AI began producing work.

Documents.

Code.

Analysis.

Designs.

Recommendations.

Soon the transition moved further.

AI systems began using tools.

Reading information.

Updating systems.

Calling APIs.

Executing workflows.

Monitoring environments.

Making bounded decisions.

This creates the possibility of an agentic organisation.

Humans and machines both possess agency.

```text
             Shared Context
                  ↓
        ┌─────────┴─────────┐
        ↓                   ↓
      Humans            AI / Systems
        ↓                   ↓
        └──────── Action ───┘
                  ↓
              Outcomes
```

The instinctive organisational response may be to insert AI into the existing management hierarchy.

AI writes tasks.

AI monitors workers.

AI scores performance.

AI checks activity.

AI escalates deviations.

AI becomes an infinitely scalable micromanager.

Technically, this may be possible.

Organisationally, it may be disastrous.

We would have taken the most powerful coordination technology yet created and used it to reinforce a century-old management architecture.

The better opportunity is different.

Use AI to reduce the need for management dependency.

AI can help sense.

AI can interpret.

AI can surface anomalies.

AI can verify evidence.

AI can explain protocols.

AI can detect drift from Direction.

AI can identify recurring Process exceptions.

AI can observe Sustainability pressures.

AI can highlight gaps in Accountability.

AI can help synchronise activity across organisational boundaries.

AI becomes not the boss but part of the conducting infrastructure.

The uploaded framework describes this role well: AI can operate as sensor, interpreter, coordinator, verifier, reflector and conductor-assistant rather than automatically becoming “the boss”.

That distinction may become one of the defining organisational choices of the coming decade.

# Chapter 10 — Human Direction, Machine-Speed Action

Autonomous systems introduce a difficult mismatch.

Machines can increasingly act at machine speed.

Human institutions deliberate at human speed.

The obvious reaction is either to constrain machines until everything moves slowly or to allow machines to act without sufficient human legitimacy.

Neither is satisfactory.

Conducting suggests another architecture.

Humans remain deeply involved in establishing:

- purpose;
- values;
- legitimacy;
- boundaries;
- acceptable risk;
- direction;
- significant trade-offs.

Machines increasingly assist with:

- sensing;
- analysis;
- execution;
- verification;
- coordination;
- routine adaptation.

The operating loop becomes:

```text
Sense
   ↓
Inject Intelligence
   ↓
Actuate
   ↓
Record Evidence
   ↓
Reflect
   ↓
Adapt
   ↺
```

The critical element is reflection.

Without reflection, automation simply accelerates the current system.

With reflection, the system can learn.

What changed?

Did the action produce the intended result?

Did assumptions remain valid?

Did the environment shift?

Did unintended consequences appear?

Does Direction require adjustment?

Do Protocols need refinement?

Is the Process still sustainable?

The organisation becomes adaptive rather than merely efficient.

# Part V — What Managers Become

# Chapter 11 — The Manager as Conductor

If organisations require less task management, what happens to managers?

Their role becomes more important, not less.

But the work changes.

A conductor observes patterns.

A conductor notices friction before it becomes crisis.

A conductor pays attention to relationships that individual participants cannot easily see.

A conductor protects coherence.

A conductor asks better questions.

A conductor improves the conditions under which others act.

The manager stops being the universal router through which work must pass.

Instead of saying:

> Send everything through me.

the conductor asks:

> Why does everything need to come through me?

Instead of:

> Give me more visibility.

the conductor asks:

> What evidence would make constant visibility unnecessary?

Instead of:

> I need to approve this.

the conductor asks:

> What protocol would make normal cases independently decidable?

Instead of:

> Everyone needs to update the tracker.

the conductor asks:

> Can the system generate reliable organisational state automatically?

Instead of:

> People need to execute faster.

the conductor asks:

> Which condition is slowing useful action?

This requires confidence.

Managers have historically derived status partly from control.

People report to them.

Decisions flow through them.

Information accumulates around them.

Their diary demonstrates their importance.

Conducting requires a different conception of leadership.

A successful conductor may make themselves less operationally necessary.

That is not loss of relevance.

It is evidence of system maturity.

# Chapter 12 — Attention Is the New Management Currency

In task-centric organisations, managers allocate work.

In conducting organisations, leaders increasingly allocate **attention**.

This distinction matters.

Most organisational problems do not require equal attention at all times.

Direction may be stable for months.

Then a market shock makes it the dominant issue.

Processes may operate smoothly.

Then growth exposes hidden bottlenecks.

Accountability may seem adequate.

Then a regulatory change requires immediate redesign.

Engagement may weaken after organisational change.

Sustainability may deteriorate during a prolonged delivery push.

Conducting therefore asks continuously:

> **What needs our attention now?**

Not:

> Which Area of Focus is most important?

All eight remain important.

But the location of tension changes.

Leadership attention should move accordingly.

This creates a dynamic organisation.

Instead of permanent management intensity everywhere, attention concentrates where coherence is threatened.

This is closer to how complex living systems function.

Resources move toward stress.

Signals surface.

Responses adapt.

Equilibrium changes.

The organisation becomes capable of regulating itself.

# Part VI — Making the Transition

# Chapter 13 — Stop Starting with Tasks

The easiest way to preserve the old system is to take the new language and turn it into another task framework.

Direction tasks.

Engagement tasks.

Protocol tasks.

Accountability tasks.

Soon the organisation has recreated its previous task board with different column headings.

That misses the point.

The Eight Areas exist above the task layer.

The transformation pattern is instead:

```text
1. State the Direction.
2. Explain the Engagement context.
3. Define the Enablement sought.
4. Establish the Protocols.
5. Check Sustainability.
6. Design the Processes.
7. Build in Accountability.
8. Synchronise through Organisational conducting.
9. Allow local actions and tasks to emerge.
10. Reflect and adapt.
```

This ordering comes directly from the conducting model in the underlying framework.

Tasks appear near the end.

That is deliberate.

Tasks are responses to context.

They should not substitute for context.

# Chapter 14 — A Practical Example

Consider a customer-reporting process.

## The micromanaged version

Every Friday an employee prepares a report.

The manager checks the report.

The manager requests changes.

The employee updates the report.

The manager approves it.

The manager sends it.

The process works.

But the organisation has embedded management dependency into the workflow.

Now move up a level.

### Direction

Why does the reporting exist?

To improve the organisation's shared understanding of customer friction.

### Engagement

Why should people care?

Customer experience should be understandable across product, support and leadership teams.

### Enablement

What should become possible?

Teams should receive useful insight that helps improve customer experience.

### Protocols

What shared rules are required?

Agreed evidence sources.

Definitions.

Privacy requirements.

Reporting schema.

Materiality thresholds.

### Sustainability

Can the system continue?

Avoid unnecessary manual work and repeated senior review.

### Processes

How does activity happen reliably?

Continuously collect signals.

Detect material change.

Generate summaries when useful.

### Accountability

Can claims be trusted?

Preserve evidence.

Record sources.

Record significant decisions.

### Organisational

How does the insight remain coherent across teams?

Synchronise relevant information and escalate only issues requiring cross-area attention.

The Friday report may still exist.

But something fundamental has changed.

The report is no longer the purpose.

It is one implementation of a larger organisational capability.

If a dashboard, event stream, AI summary or automated alert becomes more useful next year, the organisation can change the implementation without losing the underlying intent.

That is the power of conducting.

# Chapter 15 — Diagnosing Micromanagement

An organisation seeking to change should begin with symptoms.

Look for:

- too many approvals;
- excessive meetings;
- duplicated reporting;
- work waiting for decisions;
- managers reviewing routine actions;
- people afraid to act;
- unclear ownership;
- repeated clarification;
- undocumented exceptions;
- senior staff becoming bottlenecks;
- teams optimising conflicting goals;
- AI outputs requiring constant manual correction.

Do not immediately attack the symptom.

Ask instead:

> Is the organisation trying to solve missing shared context with more task control?

Then examine each Area of Focus.

### Direction

Is intent sufficiently clear?

### Engagement

Do participants understand values, processes and purpose?

### Enablement

Are activities producing useful outcomes?

### Protocols

Where are repeated decisions still dependent on individuals?

### Sustainability

Where is scarce human attention being consumed?

### Processes

Which repeated activities contain unnecessary friction?

### Accountability

Can actions and decisions be reliably reconstructed?

### Organisational

Where are the areas out of sync?

The visible problem often points toward a deeper system condition.

The goal is to intervene there.

# Chapter 16 — The First Ninety Days

A transition toward conducting does not require an enormous transformation program.

In fact, turning conducting into a transformation bureaucracy would be contradictory.

Start with one management dependency.

Choose a recurring situation in which useful activity repeatedly waits for managerial intervention.

An approval.

A report.

A meeting.

A recurring clarification.

An escalation.

A manual coordination process.

Then examine the condition behind it.

## Days 1–30: Observe

Do not immediately redesign the process.

Watch it.

What causes intervention?

What information is missing?

Which decisions repeat?

Where is authority unclear?

Which people possess undocumented knowledge?

What evidence is unavailable?

How much attention does the process consume?

Map the situation across the Eight Areas.

## Days 31–60: Move up a level

Change organisational conditions.

Clarify Direction.

Explain intent.

Improve Protocols.

Make authority clearer.

Redesign recurring Processes.

Build evidence into the operation.

Remove unnecessary approvals.

Improve cross-team synchronisation.

## Days 61–90: Release agency

Allow participants to act within the improved environment.

Do not replace every removed approval with a new dashboard.

Observe outcomes.

Measure waiting time.

Observe exceptions.

Measure management attention.

Study whether decisions remain coherent.

Then reflect.

What improved?

What failed?

What new tensions appeared?

The organisation learns conducting by conducting.

# Part VII — Measuring What Matters

# Chapter 17 — Signals of a Conducting Organisation

Traditional organisations measure activity easily.

Tasks completed.

Hours worked.

Tickets closed.

Meetings attended.

Reports produced.

These metrics can remain useful, but they say little about whether the organisation has become more capable of coherent agency.

Different signals are required.

A conducting organisation should look for outcomes such as:

### Fewer unnecessary approvals

Routine decisions happen without senior intervention.

### Reduced waiting

People and systems are not continuously blocked by organisational permission queues.

### Less repeated clarification

Shared context becomes stronger.

### Lower human attention cost

Managers spend less time manually reconstructing or routing information.

### Better traceability

Important actions create usable evidence.

### Faster autonomous action

Participants can respond appropriately within understood boundaries.

### Stronger alignment

Independent activity remains connected to Direction.

### Better exception quality

Issues reaching senior leadership are genuinely unusual or consequential.

### Improved sustainability

Growth does not produce proportional growth in coordination overhead.

These are not merely efficiency gains.

They represent increased organisational capability.

# Chapter 18 — The Conducting Test

A simple test can reveal whether an organisation is truly changing.

Ask:

**If the manager disappeared for two weeks, what would stop?**

If routine activity immediately stalls, the manager is probably still part of the execution machinery.

Now ask:

**If the organisation doubled in size tomorrow, would management coordination need to double too?**

If yes, the operating model probably still depends heavily on human mediation.

Ask:

**Can people explain why the organisation operates the way it does?**

If not, Engagement is weak.

Ask:

**Can authority be established without asking a particular person?**

If not, Protocols may be weak.

Ask:

**Can a material decision be reconstructed six months later?**

If not, Accountability may be weak.

Ask:

**Can teams recognise when their work has moved away from organisational intent?**

If not, Direction may be weak.

Ask:

**Where is senior human attention consumed by repetition?**

That may reveal one of the best opportunities for conducting.

# Conclusion — The Shift of Our Time

The management revolution of the industrial age was coordination.

Large groups of people could be organised to accomplish things that individuals could never achieve alone.

Hierarchy helped make that possible.

Management converted organisational purpose into increasingly specific instructions.

Tasks distributed intent.

Supervision maintained alignment.

Reporting allowed control.

For its time, this was an extraordinary invention.

But every organisational architecture contains assumptions about the world in which it operates.

The architecture of twentieth-century management assumes that intelligence is scarce, information moves slowly and coordination requires significant human mediation.

Those assumptions are weakening.

Intelligence is becoming abundant.

Information is becoming ambient.

Software increasingly observes organisational reality continuously.

Processes can become machine-readable.

Evidence can be created at the moment of action.

Humans can access extraordinary analytical capability.

Autonomous systems can increasingly act.

The great organisational problem therefore changes.

It is no longer simply:

> How do we get everyone to perform the required tasks?

It becomes:

> How do we enable growing numbers of intelligent actors to exercise agency while remaining part of a coherent system?

That is a conducting problem.

The conducting organisation does not abandon tasks.

It puts them in their proper place.

It does not abandon managers.

It asks managers to operate at a higher level.

It does not abandon accountability.

It replaces surveillance with stronger evidence.

It does not abandon structure.

It builds structures that enable action rather than continuously interrupting it.

It does not surrender human leadership to artificial intelligence.

It uses intelligence to strengthen sensing, interpretation, coordination, verification and reflection while retaining human and community agency over purpose and legitimacy.

The management question of the previous era was:

**What should everyone be doing?**

The conducting question is:

**What needs our attention now?**

And the leadership challenge is no longer to control every movement.

It is to create and maintain the conditions in which coherent agency can flourish.

That is the transition.

From tasks to context.

From permission to protocols.

From supervision to accountability.

From delegation to agency.

From scarce intelligence to abundant intelligence.

From managing people to conducting systems.

From micromanagement to conducting.

# Appendix A — The Eight Areas of Focus

## Direction

Overall direction, path, intent and design setting, including reflection.

**Question:**  
Where are we going, why, and what are we learning as we move?

## Engagement

Explainability of values, protocols and processes — the soul and the story.

**Question:**  
Can people understand the organisation's values, way of operating and story well enough to participate meaningfully?

## Enablement

The outputs and products of the organisation.

**Question:**  
What useful things is the organisation actually enabling or producing?

## Protocols

Standards, technologies, templates and interoperability frameworks.

**Question:**  
What shared rules or interfaces allow participants to coordinate without continuous manual intervention?

## Sustainability

Long-term viability, including capital for sustained human attention and conductors.

**Question:**  
Can this continue without consuming the people, capital, attention or resources that make it possible?

## Processes

Operational processes that drive enablement and organisational functioning.

**Question:**  
How does useful activity reliably happen?

## Accountability

Internal and external accountability, including sovereign-state regulatory obligations.

**Question:**  
Can we establish what happened, who or what acted, under what authority, and whether obligations were met?

## Organisational

Coordination and synchronisation across Areas of Focus, including team alignment.

**Question:**  
How do the Areas of Focus remain coordinated as one system?

These definitions follow the canonical summary of the Eight Areas in the underlying Conducting framework.
