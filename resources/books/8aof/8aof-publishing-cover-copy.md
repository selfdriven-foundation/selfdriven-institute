# Back-Cover Copy

Management was designed for a world in which information moved slowly, expertise was scarce and meaningful decisions had to travel through hierarchy.

That world is disappearing.

Today capable people sit throughout the organisation. Information is immediate. Artificial intelligence can analyse, interpret and increasingly act. Yet many organisations are responding to abundant intelligence with more reporting, more approvals, more dashboards and more management.

**From Micromanagement to Conducting** argues that the next organisational revolution is not about managing people more efficiently.

It is about changing the role of management itself.

The conductor does not control every movement. The conductor creates and maintains the conditions through which capable participants can act independently while remaining coherent with the whole.

Using eight interconnected Areas of Focus — **Direction, Engagement, Enablement, Protocols, Sustainability, Processes, Accountability and Organisational** — the book provides a practical model for moving from:

- task control to shared context;
- permission to protocols;
- delegation to genuine agency;
- surveillance to verifiable accountability;
- management bottlenecks to sustainable attention;
- isolated AI automation to human-and-machine coordination.

Through practical examples across technology, government, healthcare, education, insurance and autonomous systems, the book shows why micromanagement is often not simply a leadership failure but a symptom of missing organisational infrastructure.

The central challenge of the intelligence age is no longer finding enough intelligence.

It is enabling intelligence to act coherently.

**Do not micromanage the movements. Conduct the conditions for coherent agency.**

# Publisher Synopsis

For more than a century, the dominant organisational model has translated intention through layers of management into plans, projects and tasks. The model developed under conditions in which information was difficult to obtain, expertise was concentrated and large-scale coordination required substantial human mediation.

Those conditions are changing.

Knowledge is widely distributed. Information is immediate. Artificial intelligence dramatically reduces the cost of cognitive work and increasingly enables software systems to act autonomously.

Yet organisations continue to use management structures designed around intelligence scarcity.

The result is a growing contradiction: increasingly capable people and machines operating inside increasingly elaborate systems of approval, reporting, coordination and supervision.

**From Micromanagement to Conducting** argues that the next major shift in organisational design is from managing activity to conducting coherent agency.

The book introduces eight persistent Areas of Focus:

**Direction** — where are we going and what are we learning?

**Engagement** — can participants understand the organisation's values, processes and story?

**Enablement** — what useful capability is actually being created?

**Protocols** — which shared rules allow independent coordination?

**Sustainability** — can the system continue without exhausting its people, attention or resources?

**Processes** — how does useful activity reliably occur?

**Accountability** — can actions, authority and obligations be established and verified?

**Organisational** — how do all of these remain synchronised?

Rather than treating autonomy as the absence of structure, the book argues that high agency requires strong organisational conditions.

Protocols can replace repeated permission.

Evidence can replace surveillance.

Shared Direction can replace detailed instruction.

Good Processes can replace human coordination.

AI can operate as sensor, interpreter, verifier and conductor-assistant rather than becoming an infinitely scalable micromanager.

The book combines organisational theory with practical composite cases from technology, government, insurance, healthcare, education and agentic organisations.

Its central idea is straightforward:

> The challenge of the intelligence age is not simply producing more intelligence. It is enabling intelligence to act coherently.

The management question of the previous era was:

**What should everyone be doing?**

The conducting question is:

**What needs our attention now?**

# Distinctive Positioning

The book should not be positioned as:

**another productivity book**

because its central subject is organisational architecture.

It should not be positioned as:

**another AI management book**

because the conducting model remains useful with or without AI.

It should not be positioned as:

**a decentralisation manifesto**

because coherent agency can include strong central boundaries.

It should not be positioned as:

**an anti-management book**

because its thesis is that management must move to a more valuable abstraction layer.

The strongest positioning is:

> **A new operating model for organisations moving from scarce intelligence to abundant intelligence.**

# Foreword Strategy

A true foreword should ideally be written by someone other than the author.

The ideal contributor would be someone recognised for one of:

organisational leadership;

systems thinking;

future of work;

AI governance;

large-scale organisational transformation.

The foreword should not explain the Eight Areas in detail.

Its purpose is to establish why the timing matters.

Suggested brief:

> Organisations are gaining extraordinary access to intelligence, but most still operate through structures designed to ration and control intelligence. Introduce the tension between rapidly increasing capability and legacy management architecture, explain why this is a leadership problem rather than merely an AI problem, and position conducting as a timely contribution to organisational design.

Target length:

**1,200–1,800 words.**

The author's Preface should remain immediately after it.
